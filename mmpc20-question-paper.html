Q1. Discuss the consequential ethical theories with examples in the context of ethical decision-making from a managerial perspective.

Answer: Consequentialist theories judge actions by their outcomes — led by Utilitarianism, which seeks the greatest good for the greatest number — and managers apply them through everyday cost-benefit analysis.

Consequentialist (or teleological) theories judge the morality of an action by its outcomes rather than by the action itself. The most important is Utilitarianism, developed by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, which holds that an action is right if it produces the greatest good for the greatest number of people. Managers use this logic whenever they run a cost-benefit analysis: a decision is ethical if its benefits to the many outweigh its harms to the few. In contrast, non-consequentialist (deontological) theories such as Kant's ethics judge actions by their inherent rightness — duty must be followed regardless of the outcome, as expressed in Kant's categorical imperative: act only according to rules you could will to become universal laws.

From a managerial perspective, consequential thinking shapes everyday decisions. Example: a pharmaceutical company pricing AIDS drugs affordably in Africa follows the utilitarian approach — the greatest good for the greatest number of patients. A manager deciding whether to lay off 50 workers to save the jobs of 500 others is weighing consequences. However, pure consequentialism has dangers: if only outcomes matter, an unethical method that "works" can be justified. The Volkswagen emission scandal illustrates this — VW used cheat devices to pass emission norms, reasoning from short-term business consequences while ignoring the ethical duty to be honest. Good managerial decision-making therefore combines both schools: evaluate consequences (utilitarian) but respect duties and rights (deontological) so that means and ends both remain ethical.

Key points:
  • Consequentialism (teleology) judges means by ends — an action is right if its results are good (Bentham & Mill's Utilitarianism).
  • Non-consequentialism (deontology) judges the action itself — Kant's categorical imperative demands universalisable duties regardless of outcome.
  • Managerial application: cost-benefit analysis, pricing and layoff decisions — choosing the option with the best net outcome.
  • Danger: pure consequentialism can justify wrong means — VW's emission cheating "worked" commercially but was unethical.
  • Good practice: combine utilitarian outcomes with deontological duties so that both ends and means stay ethical.
Takeaway: Judge consequences with utility, but honour duties — both lenses together protect decisions.

Q2. Discuss with relevant examples how one can overcome ethical dilemmas when confronted with ethical challenges in one's daily life.

Answer: An ethical dilemma is a choice between options that are each ethically problematic, and structured frameworks — the Navigation Wheel, Moral Compass, Kidder's check points and a stakeholder-based four-step method — resolve them.

An ethical dilemma is a situation where every available option is ethically problematic, so there is no "perfect" answer. Common types include truth vs loyalty (whistleblowing on company fraud), individual vs community (factory pollution vs jobs), short-term vs long-term (cost-cutting vs quality), and justice vs mercy (punishing or forgiving a wrong). To overcome such dilemmas, structured frameworks help. The Ethical Navigation Wheel (Kvalnes & Ovarenget) guides systematic analysis. The Moral Compass (Lynn Paine) applies moral principles. Ethical Check Points (Rushworth Kidder) offer a step-by-step method: identify the dilemma, consider the parties affected, apply ethical standards, test possible solutions, and take action.

A practical four-step method works in daily life: (1) identify all stakeholders affected, (2) apply ethical theories — ask what a utilitarian, a Kantian, and a virtuous person would do, (3) evaluate alternatives against these lenses, and (4) choose and implement the best course, then review. Example: an employee discovers that her company is inflating sales figures. Truth vs loyalty dilemma — reporting it (truth) may cost her job; staying silent (loyalty) lets fraud continue. Using the framework, she identifies stakeholders (investors, colleagues, customers), applies the theories (honesty as a universal duty under Kant), and uses the company's whistleblower channel to report internally — protecting both truth and fairness. Another example: online gaming companies designing addictive games face the profit-vs-youth-harm dilemma; ethical companies respond with age limits and time controls, balancing stakeholder welfare.

Key points:
  • Dilemma types: truth vs loyalty, individual vs community, short-term vs long-term, justice vs mercy.
  • Frameworks: Ethical Navigation Wheel (Kvalnes & Ovarenget), Moral Compass (Lynn Paine), Ethical Check Points (Rushworth Kidder).
  • Four-step method: identify stakeholders → apply theories (utilitarian, Kantian, virtue) → evaluate alternatives → act and review.
  • Whistleblowing case: an employee reporting inflated sales runs truth-vs-loyalty analysis and uses internal whistleblower channels.
  • Addictive-game dilemma: profit vs youth harm — ethical firms add age limits and time controls.
Takeaway: No perfect answer exists — but a framework turns a dilemma into a defensible decision.

Q3. Discuss briefly the ethics at workplace and "Moral Muteness" in the context of organisational wrongdoing with the help of any corporate scam/scandal.

Answer: Workplace ethics is morality inside the firm, operating at individual, business and society levels; moral muteness — staying silent about wrongdoing — is what lets scandals like Enron and Satyam grow.

Workplace ethics refers to the moral principles that guide behaviour inside an organization — honesty, fairness, confidentiality, compliance, and respect. It operates at three levels in the IBS framework: the individual level (personal values and moral development), the business level (organizational culture, ethical climate, and codes of conduct), and the society level (social norms and legal frameworks). "Moral muteness" is the phenomenon where employees know an action is wrong but stay silent — they fail to voice ethical concerns because of fear of retaliation, career risk, group pressure, or the belief that "it's not my job." Moral muteness is how ordinary wrongdoing escalates into major scandals.

The Enron scandal (2001) is the classic case. What started as aggressive accounting became massive fraud because employees, auditors, and even board members remained morally mute — few questioned the "mark-to-market" practices, and the auditor (Arthur Andersen) failed to report irregularities, violating auditor independence. The collapse destroyed a Fortune 500 company, erased employees' savings, and triggered the Sarbanes-Oxley Act. In India, the Satyam scam (2009) followed the same pattern: inflated profits and fabricated cash balances continued for years because ethical concerns were silenced by the promoter's dominance. These scandals show that organizational wrongdoing thrives on moral muteness. The antidote is ethical leadership that sets the tone from the top, codes of ethics, whistleblower protection, ethics training, and anonymous reporting channels — so employees can speak instead of staying silent.

Key points:
  • Three levels (IBS): individual values, business culture and codes, societal norms and law.
  • Moral muteness: employees know it is wrong but stay silent from fear, career risk, group pressure or "not my job".
  • Enron (2001): aggressive accounting grew because auditors and board stayed mute — collapse triggered Sarbanes-Oxley.
  • Satyam (2009): promoter dominance silenced ethical voices over fabricated accounts.
  • Antidote: ethical leadership, codes of conduct, whistleblower protection, ethics training and anonymous channels.
Takeaway: Silence is the first accomplice of fraud — speak up or the scandal grows.

Q4. Discuss how stakeholders have become strong drivers of CSR with suitable examples.

Answer: Stakeholders — consumers, civil society, shareholders and employees — force CSR onto corporate agendas through four forms of activism, making responsibility a strategic necessity rather than charity.

Stakeholders are any individuals or groups who can affect or are affected by the organization's objectives — employees, customers, suppliers, shareholders, communities, and regulators. Stakeholder theory (Freeman) argues companies must balance all these interests, and modern activism proves that stakeholders actively force CSR onto corporate agendas. There are four dominant forms of activism. Consumer activism uses boycotts and ethical purchasing decisions. CSO (civil society organization) activism runs NGO campaigns and regulatory pressure. Shareholder activism uses proxy resolutions and dialogue with management. Employee activism uses whistleblowing and internal advocacy.

Examples show each form driving CSR. Greenpeace's boycott of Shell over oil spills forced Shell to adopt stronger environmental policies — consumer and CSO activism combined. BP Amoco shareholders supported climate-change resolutions at annual meetings, pushing the company toward clean-energy commitments — shareholder activism. The Coca-Cola pesticide controversy in India (CSE report, 2003) showed how civil society investigation could force a global brand to address environmental and health concerns. On the positive side, HUL's Project Shakti empowers rural women as entrepreneurs — a CSR response to community expectations. Stakeholder activism works because reputation and license to operate depend on stakeholder trust: consumers stop buying, investors sell shares, employees leave, and communities block operations. This is why socially responsible investment (SRI) and ethical consumerism have grown — stakeholders now reward responsible firms and punish irresponsible ones, making CSR a strategic necessity rather than charity.

Key points:
  • Stakeholder theory (Freeman): balance everyone who affects or is affected by the firm.
  • Four activisms: consumer (boycotts), CSO (NGO campaigns), shareholder (proxy resolutions), employee (whistleblowing).
  • Cases: Greenpeace vs Shell, BP Amoco climate resolutions, Coca-Cola pesticide controversy (CSE report 2003).
  • Positive response: HUL's Project Shakti, ethical consumerism and SRI growth.
  • Mechanism: reputation and the social license to operate — stakeholders reward and punish.
Takeaway: Reputation risk converts CSR from charity into strategy.

Q5. Discuss different approaches of business strategy for CSR.

Answer: Five approaches — Cost & Risk Reduction, Competitive Advantage, Reputation & Legitimacy, Synergistic Value, Multi-Stakeholder Value (CROSS) — with integrated CSR outperforming peripheral charity.

There are five main approaches to CSR strategy (mnemonic "CROSS"). First, Cost & Risk Reduction — CSR used to lower operating costs and manage risks, such as energy efficiency programs that cut bills and emissions together. Second, Competitive Advantage — CSR differentiates the firm in the market, like Toyota's Prius, which became a best-selling hybrid by combining environmental ethics with innovation. Third, Reputation & Legitimacy — CSR builds public trust and the social "license to operate" from communities, not just government permits. Fourth, Synergistic Value — CSR activities that create shared value for business and society (Porter & Kramer's shared value concept), where economic value and social progress reinforce each other. Fifth, Multi-Stakeholder Value — CSR designed to benefit all stakeholders simultaneously.

The strategy choice also depends on whether CSR is peripheral or integrated. Peripheral CSR keeps activities separate from the core business — charity donations, one-off sponsorships — giving limited, short-term impact. Integrated CSR embeds responsibility into the core strategy, culture, and decision-making, producing significant, sustainable impact. Cisco Networking Academy exemplifies integration: by training computer network administrators, Cisco removed a growth constraint in its own industry while creating jobs — business and social value from one program. Cause-related marketing (linking philanthropy to marketing) and the license to operate concept further show how CSR strategies are chosen: firms select the approach that fits their industry, stakeholder pressure, and strategic goals.

Key points:
  • CROSS: Cost & Risk Reduction, Competitive Advantage, Reputation & Legitimacy, Synergistic Value, Multi-Stakeholder Value.
  • Peripheral vs integrated: separate charity donations vs CSR embedded in core strategy and culture.
  • Synergistic/shared value (Porter & Kramer): economic value and social progress reinforce each other.
  • Examples: Toyota Prius (competitive advantage), Cisco Networking Academy (integrated value).
  • Cause-related marketing and the license to operate shape the strategic choice.
Takeaway: Integrated, competency-based CSR beats cheque-book charity.

Q6. How does one go about reporting on CSR? Discuss with examples various methods and tools for reporting.

Answer: CSR reporting is the mandatory annual disclosure of policy, committee, expenditure and impact — built on tools like BRR (9 principles), GRI standards, ISO 26000 and SDG-linked reporting.

CSR reporting means disclosing the company's CSR policy, activities, and expenditure to stakeholders; it is mandatory in India for eligible companies and is the "R" in the RIPE mnemonic (Reporting, Impact assessment, Policy disclosure, Expenditure tracking). A CSR report must contain: the CSR policy, CSR committee composition, details of CSR expenditure, unspent amounts (if any), and impact assessment for large programs. The process involves collecting data on projects, measuring outcomes against targets, and publishing the report as part of the Board's report annually.

The main tools and methods are: Business Responsibility Reporting (BRR) — a framework of 9 principles under which listed companies in India report their social, environmental, and governance performance; the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) — the international standard for sustainability reporting with structured indicators; and ISO 26000 — the international standard providing guidance on social responsibility. Many companies also link reports to the 17 UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), showing which goals their programs support — education programs to SDG 4, gender equality to SDG 5, and environment projects to SDGs 13-15. CSR reporting differs from CSR audit: reporting is disclosure of activities (mandatory, annual), while audit is verification of compliance (recommended best practice). Example: Indian banks and large corporates such as Infosys publish annual sustainability reports following GRI indicators along with their statutory CSR disclosures, enabling investors and NGOs to evaluate performance transparently.

Key points:
  • Process: collect project data → measure outcomes vs targets → publish in the Board's report annually.
  • Contents: CSR policy, committee composition, expenditure, unspent amounts, impact assessment.
  • Tools: BRR (9 principles, listed companies), GRI (international indicators), ISO 26000 (guidance), SDG mapping.
  • RIPE mnemonic: Reporting, Impact assessment, Policy disclosure, Expenditure tracking.
  • Reporting (disclosure) differs from audit (verification of compliance).
Takeaway: What gets reported gets managed — transparency is the proof of CSR.

Q7. What are the essential components of a CSR and sustainability policy? Also discuss the benefit of social audits.

Answer: A CSR and sustainability policy covers the policy statement, committee, 2% spend, project selection, make-vs-buy, 5% admin cap, monitoring and disclosure — and social audits verify that the money truly reached the beneficiaries.

The essential components of a CSR and sustainability policy are: (1) a clear CSR policy statement aligned with the company's business values and Schedule VII activities (education, healthcare, environment, gender equality, rural development); (2) a CSR committee with a minimum of 3 directors including at least 1 independent director to oversee the policy; (3) the amount to be spent — at least 2% of the average net profits of the preceding 3 years; (4) selection criteria for projects and implementing agencies (agencies must have a 3+ year track record and CSR-1 registration); (5) a make-vs-buy decision — whether to run projects in-house or through implementing agencies; (6) administrative overhead limits (capped at 5% of total CSR expenditure under the 2021 amendments); (7) monitoring and impact assessment mechanisms; and (8) disclosure provisions for annual reporting in the Board's report.

Social audits are a systematic evaluation of an organization's social impact and ethical performance, and they deliver several benefits. They verify that CSR funds are actually spent on the declared projects, preventing greenwashing and fund diversion. They provide independent, credible evidence to stakeholders — investors, regulators, and communities — strengthening trust and reputation. They identify gaps between promises and delivery, enabling corrective action and continuous improvement of programs. They also help companies claim genuine ESG performance, which increasingly matters for investors and for Socially Responsible Investment (SRI). In short, a well-designed policy defines what to do and how to govern it, while social audits prove that the policy is honestly implemented.

Key points:
  • Policy elements: statement aligned to Schedule VII, committee (3 directors, 1 independent), 2% of 3-year average net profit.
  • Project governance: selection criteria, implementing agencies with CSR-1 registration and a 3-year track record, make-vs-buy.
  • 2021 amendments: 5% admin overhead cap, impact assessment for obligations of Rs 10 crore or more, 3-year excess carry-forward.
  • Social audit benefits: verifies real spend, prevents greenwashing and fund diversion, builds stakeholder trust.
  • Disclosure: annual Board's report; unspent amount transferred to specified funds within 6 months.
Takeaway: The policy defines the promise; the social audit proves it.

Q8. What are the different phases of development of CSR in India? Briefly explain the trends and practices of CSR in India.

Answer: CSR in India evolved in four phases — Charity & Philanthropy (1850-1914), Social Development (1914-1960), Mixed Economy (1960-1980), Interface (1980-present) — and now runs as the world's only statutorily mandated CSR regime under Section 135.

CSR in India developed in four phases. Phase 1 (1850-1914): Charity & Philanthropy — driven by culture, religion, and tradition; industrial families like the Tatas and Birlas built schools, hospitals, and temples. Phase 2 (1914-1960): Social Development — shaped by Gandhi's trusteeship theory, which held that wealth is held in trust for society; businesses supported the freedom struggle and social welfare. Phase 3 (1960-1980): Mixed Economy — the License Raj and state-led development meant government drove social programs, and business CSR receded into compliance. Phase 4 (1980-present): Interface — after liberalization, CSR became strategic, combining philanthropy with business logic; the Ethical (Gandhian trusteeship), Statist (Nehru), Liberal (Friedman's profit-maximization) and Stakeholder (Freeman) models all operate in India (TERI classification).

Current trends and practices: India became the first country to mandate CSR by law — Section 135 of the Companies Act 2013 requires eligible companies (net worth ≥ Rs 500 crore OR turnover ≥ Rs 1,000 crore OR net profit ≥ Rs 5 crore) to spend 2% of average net profits on CSR, with the 2021 amendments adding a 5% admin overhead cap, impact assessment for large spenders, CSR-1 registration, and 3-year carry-forward of excess spend. Practices now include SDG-aligned programs, ESG reporting, partnerships with implementing agencies, and thematic focus on education, healthcare, environment, and rural development — as seen in Infosys Foundation, Tata initiatives, and Amul's cooperative model that pays farmers 80% of the consumer price.

Key points:
  • Phase 1 (1850-1914): charity and philanthropy driven by religion and tradition — Tatas, Birlas.
  • Phase 2 (1914-1960): social development under Gandhian trusteeship — wealth held in trust for society.
  • Phase 3 (1960-1980): mixed economy and licence raj — CSR receded into state-led development.
  • Phase 4 (1980-present): interface — strategic, liberalised and statutory CSR (Section 135, 2013; 2021 amendments).
  • TERI models: Ethical (Gandhi), Statist (Nehru), Liberal (Friedman), Stakeholder (Freeman).
Takeaway: From charity boxes to board-level law — India made CSR a statutory duty.

Q1. What is business ethics? How is business ethics different from law? Discuss with examples.

Answer: Business ethics is the code of conduct guiding business decisions; law is the codified minimum while ethics goes beyond it — "ethics begins where the law ends."

Key points:

  • Business ethics: principles of right and wrong applied to business situations (Crane & Matten).
  • Law: codified, enforced by courts, common minimum standard; Ethics: uncodified, contextual, enforced by society.
  • Legal is not automatically ethical — VW's emission cheating cleared technical rules but failed ethics.
  • Globalization and stakeholder scrutiny raise the ethical stakes.
  • Ethical firms exceed compliance — CSR, fair wages, transparency.
Takeaway: Compliance keeps you out of court; ethics keeps you in trust.

Q2. Discuss Kohlberg's theory of cognitive moral development and its relevance to managers.

Answer: Kohlberg ranks moral reasoning in three levels — pre-conventional, conventional and post-conventional — and managers can use it to understand and develop ethical judgement at work.

Key points:

  • Level 1 — Pre-conventional: self-interest, fear of punishment.
  • Level 2 — Conventional: conformity to norms, law and order.
  • Level 3 — Post-conventional: universal principles and social contract.
  • Most adults reason at the conventional level; scandals often trace to pre-conventional shortcuts.
  • Relevance: ethics training, codes and role models can lift moral reasoning; helps selection and whistleblowing culture.
Takeaway: Ethics is a judgement skill that can be developed, not a fixed personality trait.

Q3. Explain the IBS framework of ethics in business.

Answer: The IBS framework analyses business ethics at three interlocked levels — Individual, Business and Society — because ethical behaviour is shaped by personal values, organisational culture and social norms together.

Key points:

  • Individual level: personal values, moral development, cognitive moral development.
  • Business level: organisational culture, ethical climate, codes of conduct, governance.
  • Society level: social norms, cultural values, legal frameworks.
  • A lapse at any level propagates — a weak culture silences individuals (moral muteness).
  • Application: ethics programmes must work on all three levels simultaneously.
Takeaway: Fix ethics at one level and it leaks at the others.

Q4. Discuss the stakeholder theory of the firm and contrast it with the shareholder perspective.

Answer: Freeman's stakeholder theory says the firm must balance everyone affected by its actions; Friedman's shareholder view says profit is the only social responsibility — modern CSR sides with stakeholders.

Key points:

  • Stakeholders: employees, customers, suppliers, shareholders, community, government, NGOs.
  • Freeman: create value for all; Friedman: maximise shareholder value — "the business of business is business".
  • The stakeholder view drives CSR, ESG and long-term sustainability.
  • The shareholder view, unchecked, breeds short-termism and scandals.
  • Practice balances both: profit is necessary but not sufficient.
Takeaway: Profit is the fuel; stakeholders are the destination.

Q5. Explain Carroll's CSR Pyramid with suitable examples.

Answer: Carroll's pyramid stacks four responsibilities — Economic (base), Legal, Ethical, Philanthropic (top) — a company must be profitable and lawful before it can give back.

Key points:

  • Economic: be profitable — the foundation (Tata's commercial success).
  • Legal: obey the law — FSSAI and cooperative-law compliance (Amul).
  • Ethical: do what is right and fair — Amul paying farmers 80% of the consumer price.
  • Philanthropic: be a good corporate citizen — Infosys Foundation, Tata trusts.
  • The layers are cumulative expectations, not a sequence of duties.
Takeaway: "Every Person Loves Eating Pizza" — Economic, Legal, Ethical, Philanthropic.

Q6. Explain the applicability of Section 135 of the Companies Act 2013 and the activities listed under Schedule VII.

Answer: Section 135 applies to companies meeting any threshold — net worth ≥ Rs 500 crore, turnover ≥ Rs 1,000 crore or net profit ≥ Rs 5 crore — which must spend 2% of average net profit on Schedule VII activities.

  • Triggers: 500 Cr net worth OR 1,000 Cr turnover OR 5 Cr net profit (any one).

Q7. Discuss the arguments for and against CSR.

Q1. Discuss the contemporary approaches to business ethics. Explain with the help of a diagrammatic representation.

Answer: Contemporary approaches to business ethics group into consequentialist (utilitarianism), non-consequentialist (Kantian duty, rights, justice) and virtue-based approaches — each offering a different lens on right and wrong.

Key points:

  • Consequentialist: judge by outcomes — Utilitarianism (greatest good for the greatest number).
  • Non-consequentialist: judge by duty — Kant's categorical imperative.
  • Rights-based: protect fundamental rights (privacy, safety, informed consent).
  • Justice-based (Rawls): fairness behind the veil of ignorance.
  • Virtue ethics (Aristotle): character and moral excellence of the decision-maker.
Takeaway: Contemporary ethics = multiple lenses applied together.

Key points:

  • Personal values and upbringing form the moral base.
  • Kohlberg's three levels: pre-conventional → conventional → post-conventional reasoning.
  • Ego strength: the courage to act on convictions under pressure.

Takeaway: Ethics is personal before it is organisational.

  • Synergistic Value: shared value (Porter & Kramer).
  • Multi-Stakeholder Value: benefit all stakeholders (Cisco).
Takeaway: CROSS the five approaches; integrate, don't donate.

Q4. Critically analyze the following theories of ethics: (a) Kantian Ethics (b) Utilitarianism theory of Ethics.

Answer: Kantian ethics judges duty and universalisability with no regard to consequences, while utilitarianism judges the greatest good for the greatest number — each has clear strengths and critical weaknesses.

Key points:

  • Kant: categorical imperative — act only on universalisable rules; treat people as ends, never means.
  • Kantian strength: protects rights and human dignity; weakness: rigidity and conflicts of duty, ignores outcomes.
  • Utilitarianism: greatest good for the greatest number (Bentham, Mill); act-utilitarianism vs rule-utilitarianism.
  • Utilitarian strength: practical and cost-benefit friendly; weakness: sacrifices minority rights, hard to measure utility.
  • Managers blend both: duties constrain, utility optimises.
Takeaway: Kant checks rights; Mill checks results — use both.

What is the marking scheme of the MMPC-20 Term-End Examination?

The paper is 100 marks over 3 hours with 70% weightage in the course. There are eight questions and you must attempt any five; each question carries equal marks (20 marks), with some split into sub-parts of 10 + 10 marks.
