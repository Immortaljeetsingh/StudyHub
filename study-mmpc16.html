Unit 1: Dynamics of International Business

📖 Definition

International Business: Commercial transactions between parties across national boundaries involving goods, services, capital, technology, and knowledge.

MNE/MNC: A firm that operates in multiple countries with significant business activities outside its home country.
📊 Domestic vs International Business
DimensionDomesticInternational
NationsOneMany
CurrenciesSingleMultiple
CulturesHomogeneousDiverse
RiskMinimumConsiderable
Legal systemsOneMultiple
💡 Real-World Example

Tata Steel acquired NatSteel (Singapore) and Corus (Europe) to overcome domestic competition from ArcelorMittal and POSCO, achieving economies of scale in the cyclical steel industry.

🧠 Memory Aid

"MRES" = Market, Resource, Efficiency, Strategic asset seeking (4 motives to go international)

🎯 Exam Tip

Frequently asked: "Explain the differences between domestic and international business" — cover at least 5 dimensions.

✅ Unit Summary

  • IB = business activities crossing national borders
  • 4 seeking motives: Market, Resource, Efficiency, Strategic Asset
  • Domestic vs International: single vs multiple currencies, cultures, legal systems, risk levels

Unit 2: Globalization and Evolving Paradigm

📖 Definition

Globalization: The process of integrating world economies through trade, investment, technology, and cultural exchange.

Glocalization: Adapting global strategies to local market conditions — "Think globally, act locally."
FDI: Investment by a firm in business operations in a foreign country.
📊 Three Waves of Globalization
WavePeriodKey Features
First1870-1914Trade expansion, colonialism, steamships, telegraph
Second1945-1980Bretton Woods, GATT, regional blocs, Cold War
Third1980-presentTechnology revolution, emerging markets, internet
💡 Real-World Example

McDonald's glocalizes — McAloo Tikki in India, Teriyaki Burger in Japan, McArabia in Middle East.

🧠 Memory Aid

"T-TRA" = Technology, Trade liberalization, Regional integration, MNC Activities (drivers of globalization)

✅ Unit Summary

  • 3 waves: 1870-1914, 1945-1980, 1980-present
  • Drivers: Technology, Trade liberalization, Regional integration, MNC activities
  • Glocalization = think globally, act locally

Unit 3: International Business Environment — PESTEL

📖 Definition

PESTEL Framework: A macro-environment analysis tool examining Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, and Legal factors.

Country Risk: The risk of investing or operating in a country due to political, economic, or transfer-related factors.
Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions: Six dimensions explaining cross-cultural differences.
📊 PESTEL Framework
FactorComponents
PoliticalGovernment stability, trade policies, political risk, corruption
EconomicGDP, inflation, exchange rates, economic systems, FDI policies
SocialCulture, demographics, education, lifestyle, religion
TechnologicalInfrastructure, R&D capacity, innovation, digital readiness
EnvironmentalClimate, sustainability regulations, green policies
LegalBusiness laws, IP protection, contract enforcement, labor laws
📊 Hofstede's 6 Cultural Dimensions
1. Power Distance
↓
2. Individualism vs Collectivism
↓
3. Masculinity vs Femininity
↓
4. Uncertainty Avoidance
↓
5. Long-term Orientation
↓
6. Indulgence vs Restraint
💡 Real-World Example

KFC faced massive resistance in India due to socio-cultural factors — Hindus protested believing KFC sold beef disguised as chicken, and vegetarian/non-vegetarian items shared cooking oil.

🧠 Memory Aid

"PIMMUL" = Power distance, Individualism, Masculinity, Masculinity, Uncertainty avoidance, Long-term orientation (Hofstede)

🎯 Exam Tip

PESTEL analysis is the most commonly tested framework. Always provide country-specific examples.

✅ Unit Summary

  • PESTEL = Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal
  • Hofstede's 6 dimensions explain cross-cultural differences
  • Country risk types: Political, Economic, Transfer

Unit 4: Trade Theories

📖 Definition

Comparative Advantage: A country's ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than others (David Ricardo).

OLI Framework (Dunning): Ownership, Location, and Internalization advantages determine FDI decisions.
📊 Trade Theories Timeline
Mercantilism (16th-18th C) ↓ Absolute Advantage (Adam Smith, 1776) ↓ Comparative Advantage (David Ricardo, 1817) ↓ Heckscher-Ohlin Model (1919/1933) ↓ FDI Theories: Hymer, Dunning, Vernon
📊 Theory Comparison
TheoryProponentCore IdeaLimitation
Mercantilism—Wealth = Gold; promote exportsZero-sum game
Absolute AdvantageSmithProduce what's cheapestCan't explain trade when one has all advantages
Comparative AdvantageRicardoSpecialize in lowest opportunity costAssumed labor only factor
Heckscher-OhlinH-OExport abundant-factor goodsLeontief Paradox
💡 Real-World Example

India's Ayurveda products first expand to culturally close South Asian markets (Uppsala model), then to Southeast Asia and Middle East.

🧠 Memory Aid

"A-C-O-H" = Absolute, Comparative, Opportunity cost, Heckscher-Ohlin (trade theories)
"O-L-I" = Ownership, Location, Internalization (Dunning)

✅ Unit Summary

  • Evolution: Mercantilism → Absolute → Comparative → H-O → FDI theories
  • OLI = Ownership + Location + Internalization advantages
  • Leontief Paradox challenged H-O model

Unit 5: WTO Agreements

📖 Definition

WTO: A permanent international body (established 1995) promoting free trade through a rules-based system.

MFN (Most Favoured Nation): A WTO principle requiring equal trade treatment to all member countries.
TRIPS: Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights.
📊 WTO Key Principles
PrincipleMeaning
Most Favoured NationEqual treatment to all WTO members
National TreatmentEqual treatment to foreign and domestic goods
Free Trade through NegotiationProgressive tariff reduction
Fair CompetitionAnti-dumping, countervailing measures
PredictabilityBound tariff rates, transparent policies
Encouraging DevelopmentSpecial treatment for developing countries
💡 Real-World Example

India used TRIPS flexibilities to produce generic medicines, making HIV treatment affordable for millions in Africa.

🧠 Memory Aid

"G-G-T" = GATT (goods), GATS (services), TRIPS (IP) — 3 pillars of WTO

✅ Unit Summary

  • GATT (1947-1994) → WTO (1995-present, 164 members)
  • 6 principles: MFN, National Treatment, Free Trade, Fair Competition, Predictability, Development
  • 3 pillars: GATT (goods), GATS (services), TRIPS (IP)

Unit 6: Regional Trade Blocs

📖 Definition

Trade Creation: New trade generated due to a bloc (efficient — shifts to lower-cost member producers).

Trade Diversion: Trade shifted from efficient non-members to less efficient members due to bloc preferences.
📊 Stages of Economic Integration
PTA → FTA → Customs Union → Common Market → Economic Union → Political Union ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓ Reduced No Common Free movement Harmonized Full tariffs internal tariff of factors policies integration tariffs external
📊 Major Trade Blocs
BlocMembersKey Features
EU27Single market, Euro, Schengen
USMCA3Eliminated tariffs, rules of origin
ASEAN10Southeast Asian cooperation
MERCOSUR4+South American customs union
AfCFTA54Continental free trade area
💡 Real-World Example

EU is the most advanced integration — 27 countries, single currency, common regulations, free movement of people via Schengen.

🧠 Memory Aid

"PFCCEP" = PTA, FTA, Customs Union, Common Market, Economic Union, Political Union

✅ Unit Summary

  • 6 stages: PTA → FTA → Customs Union → Common Market → Economic Union → Political Union
  • Trade creation (efficient) vs Trade diversion (inefficient)
  • Major blocs: EU, USMCA, ASEAN, MERCOSUR, AfCFTA

Unit 7: International Entry Strategies

📖 Definition

Entry Mode: The institutional arrangement by which a firm enters a foreign market.

Uppsala Model: Firms internationalize incrementally, starting with psychically close markets.
Joint Venture: A new entity created by two or more firms with shared ownership and control.
📊 Entry Modes (Least to Most Commitment)
ModeCommitmentRiskControlWhen to Use
ExportingLowLowLowTesting new markets
LicensingLow-MedLowLowLeveraging IP
TurnkeyMediumMediumMediumOne-time projects
JVMed-HighMediumSharedLocal knowledge needed
WOSHighHighFullMaximum control
💡 Real-World Example

Walmart entered India via JV with Bharti Enterprises (2007) due to FDI restrictions in multi-brand retail, later acquired Flipkart (2018) when regulations changed.

🧠 Memory Aid

"EL-JW" = Export, License, Joint venture, Wholly owned subsidiary

✅ Unit Summary

  • 6 theories: Uppsala, TCA, OLI, Network, Strategy, Bargaining
  • Entry modes: Export → License → Turnkey → JV → WOS
  • Factors: country attractiveness, risk, government policies, timing, capabilities

Unit 8: Organizational Structures for IB

📖 Definition

Matrix Structure: An organizational form combining two or more dimensions (e.g., geography AND product).

Centralization: Concentration of decision-making authority at headquarters.
📊 Evolution of Structures
1. Export Department
↓
2. International Division
↓
3. Global Structures (Geographic / Product / Functional / Matrix)
💡 Real-World Example

Unilever uses a matrix structure — organized by geography (Asia, Americas, Europe) AND by product categories (Food, Home Care, Beauty).

🧠 Memory Aid

"GPFM" = Geographic, Product, Functional, Matrix (structure types)

✅ Unit Summary

  • Evolution: Export Dept → International Div → Global Structures
  • 4 global types: Geographic, Product, Functional, Matrix
  • Matrix: dual focus but complex (conflict potential)

Unit 9: Strategic Alliances

📖 Definition

Strategic Alliance: A cooperative agreement between two or more firms to achieve mutual objectives while remaining independent.

Joint Venture: A new entity with shared ownership, risks, and profits.
Equity Alliance: Partners take equity stakes in each other (cross-shareholding).
📊 Types of Strategic Alliances
TypeDescriptionExample
Joint VentureNew entity, shared ownershipTata Starbucks
Equity AllianceCross-shareholdingRenault-Nissan
Non-equity AllianceContractual cooperationR&D partnerships
💡 Real-World Example

Maruti Suzuki started as JV between Suzuki (Japan) and Government of India (1982). Suzuki provided technology, India provided market access. Now Suzuki owns majority stake.

🧠 Memory Aid

"JEN" = Joint venture, Equity, Non-equity (alliance types)

✅ Unit Summary

  • 3 types: JV, Equity, Non-equity
  • Reasons: share costs, access markets, overcome restrictions
  • Success factors: clear objectives, mutual benefit, trust, flexibility

Unit 10: International Marketing

📖 Definition

EPRG Framework: Ethnocentric, Polycentric, Region-centric, and Geocentric orientations toward internationalization.

Standardization: Using the same marketing strategy across all markets.
Adaptation: Modifying the marketing mix to suit local conditions.
📊 EPRG Framework
OrientationApproachExample
EthnocentricHome country approach everywherePatanjali globally
PolycentricEach country treated separatelyApollo in Middle East
Region-centricRegional approach for similar marketsUnilever in South Asia
GeocentricGlobal integrated approachApple globally
💡 Real-World Example

IKEA adapts its product range — smaller furniture for Japanese apartments, brighter colors for India, different mattress sizes for UK.

🧠 Memory Aid

"E-P-R-G" = Ethnocentric, Polycentric, Region-centric, Geocentric
"S-PAM" = Standardization vs Adaptation in Product, Price, Place, Promotion

✅ Unit Summary

  • EPRG: Ethnocentric → Polycentric → Region-centric → Geocentric
  • Key decision: Standardization vs Adaptation
  • 9 drivers of international marketing

Unit 11: International Finance

📖 Definition

Foreign Exchange Market: A global marketplace for buying and selling currencies.

Hedging: Using financial instruments to reduce or eliminate foreign exchange risk.
PPP: Exchange rates should equalize the price of identical goods across countries.
📊 Exchange Rate Risk Types
RiskWhat It AffectsExample
Transaction RiskActual cash flowsExport payment in foreign currency
Translation RiskFinancial statementsConsolidating foreign subsidiary accounts
Economic RiskCompetitive positionCurrency change affecting export pricing
📊 Monetary System Evolution
Gold Standard (pre-1914) ↓ Bretton Woods (1944-1971) — Fixed rates, USD pegged to gold ↓ Floating Rates (1971-present) — Market-determined rates ↓ Current System — Managed float with intervention
💡 Real-World Example

Indian IT companies like Infosys hedge USD/INR exposure using forward contracts, as 60%+ revenue comes in USD.

🧠 Memory Aid

"T-T-E" = Transaction, Translation, Economic (FX risk types)
"F-FOM" = Forward, Futures, Options, Money market (hedging tools)

✅ Unit Summary

  • Gold Standard → Bretton Woods → Floating rates → Managed float
  • 3 FX risks: Transaction, Translation, Economic
  • Hedging: Forwards, Futures, Options, Money market

Unit 12: International Operations & Logistics

📖 Definition

Centralized Production: Concentrating production in one location to serve the entire world.

Rationalized Production: Specializing individual plants in specific components for global distribution.
📊 Production Strategies
StrategyDescriptionWhen Used
CentralizedOne location for worldHigh economies of scale
DecentralizedLocal production for local marketsHigh transport costs
RationalizedSpecialized plants for componentsComplex products, trade agreements
💡 Real-World Example

Apple's supply chain — design in California, chip fabrication in Taiwan (TSMC), assembly in China (Foxconn), distribution globally.

🧠 Memory Aid

"CDR" = Centralized, Decentralized, Rationalized (production strategies)

✅ Unit Summary

  • 3 production strategies: Centralized, Decentralized, Rationalized
  • Global supply chain: Sourcing → Manufacturing → Distribution
  • Challenges: coordination, quality control, disruptions

Unit 13: International Human Resource Management

📖 Definition

IHRM: Management of human resources in international context including expatriate management, cross-cultural training, and global compensation.

Ethnocentric Staffing: Filling key positions with home country nationals.
Geocentric Staffing: Hiring the best person regardless of nationality.
Expatriate: Employee sent to work in a foreign country for an extended period.
📊 Staffing Approaches
ApproachKey Positions Filled ByWhen Used
EthnocentricHome country nationalsEarly internationalization
PolycentricHost country nationalsMature operations
GeocentricBest person regardlessGlobal integration
RegiocentricManagers from same regionRegional strategy
📊 Expatriate Management Cycle
Selection (competence, adaptability, family)
↓
Training (language, cultural awareness)
↓
Compensation (balance sheet approach)
↓
Repatriation (career planning, reverse culture shock)
💡 Real-World Example

Japanese companies traditionally used ethnocentric approach — Japanese managers in all key positions worldwide. Now shifting toward geocentric as they globalize.

🧠 Memory Aid

"E-P-G-R" = Ethnocentric, Polycentric, Geocentric, Regiocentric (staffing approaches)

🎯 Exam Tip

Staffing approaches (EPRG) appear in both marketing and HRM contexts. Use the same framework but with HRM-specific examples.

✅ Unit Summary

  • 4 staffing approaches: Ethnocentric, Polycentric, Geocentric, Regiocentric
  • Expatriate cycle: Selection → Training → Compensation → Repatriation
  • Challenges: culture shock, dual careers, legal requirements, performance evaluation
