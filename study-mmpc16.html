Block-01: International Business Environment

Unit 1: Dynamics of International Business

📖 Core Concepts
  • International Business = business activities crossing national borders
  • History as old as civilization, started with barter trade
  • IB is a catalyst for technology diffusion, competition, higher living standards
International Business: Commercial transactions between parties across national boundaries involving goods, services, capital, technology, and knowledge.
MNE/MNC: A firm that operates in multiple countries with significant business activities outside its home country.

📋 Why Firms Go International (4 Seeking Motives)
  • Market Seeking — access new customers, follow clients abroad, saturate domestic market
  • Resource Seeking — access raw materials, skilled/unskilled labor, natural resources
  • Efficiency Seeking — reduce costs through economies of scale, factor cost arbitrage
  • Strategic Asset Seeking — acquire brands, technology, patents, distribution networks
📋 Barriers to International Business
  • Tariff Barriers: Import duties, anti-dumping duties, countervailing duties
  • Non-Tariff Barriers: Quotas, licensing requirements, technical standards, SPS measures
  • Cultural Barriers: Language, religion, customs, consumer preferences, business etiquette
  • Administrative Barriers: Bureaucratic delays, corruption, inconsistent regulations
  • Currency Risk: Exchange rate fluctuations affecting profitability
  • Political Risk: Government instability, policy changes, expropriation, war/conflict
📋 Indian MNCs Going Global — Key Motivations
  • Tata Group: Strategic asset seeking — acquired Corus (steel), JLR (auto), Tetley (tea) for brands and technology
  • Infosys/TCS: Market seeking + efficiency seeking — set up global delivery centers to serve clients in their time zones
  • ONGC Videsh: Resource seeking — invested in oil fields in Russia, Vietnam, Mozambique, Colombia for energy security
  • Dr Reddy’s: Market seeking — entered US/Europe generic drug markets where margins are higher than India
  • Bharat Forge: Strategic asset seeking — acquired Carl Dan Peddinghaus (Germany) for precision forging technology

Unit 2: Globalization and Evolving Paradigm

📖 Core Concepts
  • Globalization = process of integrating world economies, removing barriers
  • Not new — has occurred in waves throughout history
Globalization: The process of integrating world economies through trade, investment, technology, and cultural exchange.
Glocalization: Adapting global strategies to local market conditions — "Think globally, act locally."
FDI: Investment by a firm in business operations in a foreign country.
📊 Three Waves of Globalization
WavePeriodKey Features
First1870-1914Trade expansion, colonialism, steamships, telegraph
Second1945-1980Bretton Woods, GATT, regional blocs, Cold War
Third1980-presentTechnology revolution, emerging markets, internet
📋 Drivers of Globalization
  • Technology — internet, communication, containerization (shipping), air travel
  • Trade liberalization — WTO, FTAs, tariff reduction from avg 40% (1947) to 4% (2020)
  • Regional integration — EU, ASEAN, USMCA creating larger integrated markets
  • MNC activities — FDI, global supply chains, intra-firm trade (33% of world trade)
  • Financial liberalization — capital account convertibility, cross-border investment flows
  • Political changes — end of Cold War, China’s opening (1978), India’s LPG reforms (1991)
📋 India’s Globalization Journey
  • Pre-1991: License Raj, import substitution, limited FDI, inward-looking economy
  • 1991 LPG Reforms: Liberalization (deregulation), Privatization (disinvestment), Globalization (opening to FDI)
  • Post-1991: FDI increased from $100M (1991) to $84B (2022); IT exports from $100M to $194B; forex reserves from $1.2B to $600B+
  • Key Sectors Globalized: IT services (Infosys, TCS, Wipro), Pharmaceuticals (Sun, Dr Reddy’s), Auto components (Motherson, Bharat Forge), Steel (Tata, JSW)
  • Ongoing Challenges: Trade deficit, protectionist pressures, need for labor reforms, infrastructure gaps

Unit 3: International Business Environment — PESTEL

📖 Core Concepts
  • PESTEL = framework for scanning macro-environment
  • Country risk assessment is critical before market entry
  • Hofstede's 6 cultural dimensions explain cross-cultural differences
PESTEL Framework: A macro-environment analysis tool examining Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, and Legal factors.
Country Risk: The risk of investing or operating in a country due to political, economic, or transfer-related factors.
Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions: Six dimensions explaining cross-cultural differences.

📋 Hofstede's 6 Cultural Dimensions
  • Power Distance — acceptance of unequal power distribution
  • Individualism vs Collectivism — self vs group orientation
  • Masculinity vs Femininity — achievement vs caring values
  • Uncertainty Avoidance — tolerance for ambiguity
  • Long-term vs Short-term Orientation — future vs present focus
  • Indulgence vs Restraint — gratification of desires

📋 Cultural Dimensions — Business Implications
  • High Power Distance (India 77, China 80) — hierarchical org structures accepted, centralized decision-making expected
  • Collectivist (China 20, India 48) — group harmony valued, relationship-based business deals, family-owned conglomerates
  • High Uncertainty Avoidance (Japan 92) — detailed contracts, risk-averse, preference for proven methods
  • Long-term Oriented (China 87, Japan 88) — patient capital, strategic planning horizons of 10-20 years
  • High Masculinity (Japan 95) — competitive work culture, long working hours, achievement-driven
📋 Country Risk Types
  • Political Risk — government instability, policy changes, expropriation, nationalization
  • Economic Risk — recession, currency volatility, inflation, balance of payments crisis
  • Transfer Risk — restrictions on capital movement, foreign exchange controls
  • Operational Risk — bureaucratic delays, corruption, infrastructure gaps
📋 Country Risk Assessment Methods
  • BERI (Business Environment Risk Intelligence) — quantitative rating system for political, operational, and financial risk
  • Euromoney Country Risk Index — composite score based on political, economic, and structural factors
  • OECD Country Risk Classifications — 8-category system for export credit risk
  • Sovereign Credit Ratings — S&P, Moody’s, Fitch ratings reflect ability to repay debt
📋 PESTEL — India as an Investment Destination
  • Political: Stable democracy, but state-level policy variations, bureaucratic complexity
  • Economic: 5th largest GDP, 7%+ growth, but inflation volatility and fiscal deficit concerns
  • Social: 1.4 billion population, young demographics (median age 28), growing middle class of 300M+
  • Technological: IT powerhouse, UPI digital payments revolution, Aadhaar biometric ID system
  • Environmental: Air pollution challenges, Paris Agreement commitments, renewable energy push (500 GW target by 2030)
  • Legal: Complex tax system (post-GST simplification), strong IP courts, FDI limits in retail/insurance

Unit 4: Trade Theories

📖 Core Concepts
  • Trade theories explain why nations trade and what they trade
  • Evolution from mercantilism to modern FDI theories
  • Each theory builds on and critiques its predecessor
Mercantilism: An economic theory that a nation's wealth is measured by its gold/silver reserves; promote exports, restrict imports.
Absolute Advantage: A country's ability to produce a good more efficiently than any other country (Adam Smith).
Comparative Advantage: A country's ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than others (David Ricardo).
Heckscher-Ohlin Model: Countries export goods that use their abundant factors of production.
OLI Framework (Dunning): Ownership, Location, and Internalization advantages determine FDI decisions.
📊 Trade Theories Timeline
Mercantilism (16th-18th C) ↓ Absolute Advantage (Adam Smith, 1776) ↓ Comparative Advantage (David Ricardo, 1817) ↓ Opportunity Cost (Haberler, 1933) ↓ Heckscher-Ohlin Model (1919/1933) ↓ Leontief Paradox (1953) ↓ New Trade Theory (Krugman, 1979) ↓ Porter’s Diamond (1990) ↓ FDI Theories: Hymer, Dunning, Vernon

📋 New Trade Theory (Krugman, 1979)
  • Trade patterns are influenced by economies of scale and first-mover advantages, not just factor endowments
  • Countries may dominate exports in certain industries due to early entry and accumulated scale, even without natural advantage
  • Network effects and learning curves create path dependence in trade patterns
  • Explains why similar countries trade similar products (intra-industry trade) — e.g., Germany exports cars to Japan and Japan exports cars to Germany
  • Government intervention (industrial policy, subsidies) can help domestic firms achieve first-mover advantage
📋 Vernon’s Product Life Cycle Theory (1966)
  • New Product Stage: Innovation in developed country (e.g., USA), production for domestic market
  • Maturing Product Stage: Standardization begins, production moves to other developed countries (Europe, Japan)
  • Standardized Product Stage: Fully standardized, production shifts to developing countries (low-cost manufacturing)
  • Example: Smartphones — designed in USA/Korea (Apple, Samsung), now assembled in China/Vietnam/India
  • Limitation: Does not explain simultaneous global product launches or reverse innovation from emerging markets
📋 OLI Framework (Dunning) — Detailed
  • Ownership advantages — firm-specific assets (technology, brand, patents, management expertise)
  • Location advantages — country-specific factors (resources, market size, labor cost, infrastructure, policy incentives)
  • Internalization advantages — keeping advantages in-house vs licensing to avoid transaction costs, protect IP

When all three exist, FDI (WOS) is preferred. When only O exists, exporting works. When O+I but not L, licensing is optimal.

💡 OLI — Indian Example (Tata Motors → Jaguar Land Rover)

Ownership: Tata Motors had financial resources and management capability from its domestic success.
Location: UK provided access to premium brands, skilled engineering workforce, and European market access.
Internalization: Full acquisition (WOS) chosen over licensing to protect JLR’s brand equity and technology.
Result: Tata acquired JLR for $2.3B in 2008; JLR revenue grew from $10B to $28B by 2023.

📋 Uppsala Model — Detailed
  • Firms internationalize incrementally, starting with psychically close markets (similar culture, language, geography)
  • Market Commitment: Start with low-commitment modes (exporting), gradually increase (JV, WOS)
  • Experiential Knowledge: Knowledge gained from operating in a market reduces perceived risk
  • Psychic Distance: Cultural and geographic proximity determines market entry order
  • Indian Example: Indian IT firms (TCS, Infosys) first entered culturally similar UK/Australia, then expanded to continental Europe and Japan

Unit 5: WTO Agreements

📖 Core Concepts
  • GATT (1947-1994) was a multilateral treaty; WTO (1995) is a permanent body
  • WTO promotes free trade through rules-based system
  • Key principles: MFN, National Treatment, reciprocity
WTO: A permanent international body (established 1995) promoting free trade through a rules-based system.
GATT: A multilateral treaty (1947-1994) for trade liberalization through tariff reduction.
MFN (Most Favoured Nation): A WTO principle requiring equal trade treatment to all member countries.
National Treatment: A WTO principle requiring equal treatment of foreign and domestic goods after customs clearance.
GATS: General Agreement on Trade in Services — WTO agreement covering trade in services.
TRIPS: Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights — WTO agreement on IP protection.

📋 Doha Development Round

Launched 2001, focused on developing countries' concerns — agriculture, market access, TRIPS flexibility — largely stalled.

India's WTO Experience:

  • Benefits: IT services exports, pharmaceutical flexibilities
  • Challenges: Agriculture subsidies, market opening pressures
📋 WTO Dispute Settlement Mechanism
  • Dispute Settlement Body (DSB) — resolves trade disputes between member countries
  • Process: Consultation → Panel → Appellate Body → Implementation
  • Binding: Rulings are binding; non-compliance can lead to authorized retaliation (tariffs)
  • India’s Cases: India has been complainant in 24 cases and respondent in 32 cases (as of 2023)
  • Notable: India won case against US on steel tariffs (2014); lost case on solar panel domestic content requirement (2016)
  • Current Crisis: Appellate Body non-functional since 2019 (US blocking appointments), threatening WTO’s effectiveness
📋 TRIPS Agreement — Key Provisions
  • Patents: Minimum 20-year protection for inventions (pharmaceuticals, biotech)
  • Copyright: Minimum 50-year protection for literary, artistic works
  • Trademarks: Registration and protection of brand identifiers
  • Trade Secrets: Protection against unfair commercial use
  • Flexibilities for Developing Countries: Compulsory licensing (for public health emergencies), parallel imports, transition periods
  • India’s Stand: Used compulsory licensing for cancer drug Nexavar (2012); pushed for TRIPS waiver during COVID-19 vaccines

Unit 6: Regional Trade Blocs

📖 Core Concepts
  • Economic integration occurs in stages from PTA to Political Union
  • Trade creation vs Trade diversion effects
  • Major blocs: EU, NAFTA/USMCA, ASEAN, MERCOSUR, SAARC
Economic Integration: The process of reducing trade barriers between countries to form larger economic units.
PTA: An agreement giving preferential access to certain products from member countries.
FTA: A bloc where members eliminate internal tariffs but maintain independent external tariffs.
Customs Union: A trade bloc with no internal tariffs AND a common external tariff.
Trade Creation: New trade generated due to a bloc (efficient — shifts to lower-cost member producers).
Trade Diversion: Trade shifted from efficient non-members to less efficient members due to bloc preferences.

📋 Trade Creation vs Trade Diversion
  • Trade Creation: New trade that wouldn't exist without the bloc (efficient)
  • Trade Diversion: Trade shifted from efficient non-member to less efficient member (inefficient)
📋 India’s Regional Trade Agreements
  • SAFTA (South Asian FTA): India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, Maldives — tariff reduction on 5,000+ items, but Pakistan-India tensions limit effectiveness
  • ASEAN-India FTA: Signed 2009, covers goods (90% tariff lines), services (2014), investment — boosted trade from $43B (2008) to $110B (2022)
  • India-Japan CEPA: Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (2011) — 90% tariff elimination, Japanese FDI into India
  • India-UAE CEPA: Signed 2022, target $100B bilateral trade, zero tariff on 90% Indian exports
  • RCEP: India opted out in 2019 due to concerns about cheap Chinese imports flooding Indian markets
  • BIMSTEC: Bay of Bengal Initiative — 7 member countries, bridge between SAARC and ASEAN

Unit 7: International Entry Strategies

📖 Core Concepts
  • Entry mode decision depends on environmental analysis (PESTEL)
  • Six theories of internationalization explain different entry approaches
  • Entry modes range from low-commitment (exporting) to high-commitment (wholly owned)
Entry Mode: The institutional arrangement by which a firm enters a foreign market (exporting, licensing, JV, WOS).
Uppsala Model: A theory that firms internationalize incrementally, starting with psychically close markets and increasing commitment over time.
Joint Venture: A new entity created by two or more firms with shared ownership and control.
Wholly Owned Subsidiary (WOS): A foreign operation entirely owned by the parent company (greenfield or acquisition).
Licensing: An agreement granting a foreign firm the right to use intellectual property for a fee.

Block-03: International Management

Unit 8: Organizational Structures for International Business

📖 Core Concepts
  • Organizational structure evolves as firm internationalizes
  • Trade-off between centralization (control) and decentralization (responsiveness)
  • Four global structure types: Geographic, Product, Functional, Matrix
Organizational Structure: The formal arrangement of roles, responsibilities, and reporting relationships within a firm.
Geographic Structure: Organizing operations by regions (e.g., Asia, Americas, Europe).
Product Structure: Organizing operations by product lines or divisions.
Matrix Structure: An organizational form combining two or more dimensions (e.g., geography AND product).
Centralization: Concentration of decision-making authority at the top/headquarters.
Decentralization: Delegation of decision-making authority to lower levels or subsidiaries.

📋 Coordination Mechanisms
  • Direct supervision — centralized control from headquarters
  • Standardization — rules, procedures, policies across locations
  • Mutual adjustment — informal communication and collaboration

Factors Affecting Structure: Strategy, technology, environment, firm size

📋 Matrix Structure — Advantages & Disadvantages

Advantages:

  • Dual focus — product AND geographic responsiveness simultaneously
  • Efficient resource utilization across dimensions
  • Information flows in multiple directions, better decision-making
  • Develops managers with cross-functional expertise

Disadvantages:

  • Dual reporting lines create confusion and conflict
  • High coordination costs and management overhead
  • Power struggles between product and regional heads
  • Slow decision-making due to consensus requirements
💡 Indian Companies — Organizational Structures
  • Tata Group: Holding company structure (Tata Sons) with 30+ independently managed companies across sectors (steel, auto, IT, hospitality). Each company has its own board, but group-level synergies through shared brand, values, and cross-shareholdings.
  • Reliance Industries: Moved from functional structure to SBU (Strategic Business Unit) structure — separate verticals for O2C (Oil-to-Chemicals), Jio (Telecom/Digital), Retail. Each SBU has its own CEO and P&L accountability.
  • Infosys: Geographic structure with regional heads for Americas, Europe, Asia-Pacific + industry verticals (Banking, Manufacturing, Healthcare). Global delivery model with India as the hub.
  • Wipro: Matrix structure — organized by geography (4 regions) AND by service lines (Cloud, Cybersecurity, AI) AND by industry verticals. Three-dimensional matrix creates complexity but enables cross-selling.

Unit 9: Strategic Alliances

📖 Core Concepts
  • Strategic Alliance = cooperative agreement between firms
  • Three types: JV, equity, non-equity
  • Alliances help share costs, access markets, overcome restrictions
Strategic Alliance: A cooperative agreement between two or more firms to achieve mutual objectives while remaining independent.
Joint Venture: A new entity created by two or more firms with shared ownership, risks, and profits.
Equity Alliance: An alliance where partners take equity stakes in each other (cross-shareholding).
Non-Equity Alliance: A contractual cooperation without shared ownership (e.g., R&D partnerships, distribution agreements).
📊 Types of Strategic Alliances
TypeDescriptionExample
Joint VentureNew entity, shared ownershipTata Starbucks (Tata + Starbucks)
Equity AllianceCross-shareholdingRenault-Nissan
Non-equity AllianceContractual cooperationR&D partnerships, distribution agreements
📋 Reasons for Alliances
  • Share costs and risks of international operations
  • Access new markets and technologies
  • Gain competitive advantage
  • Overcome government restrictions (e.g., FDI caps)
📋 Challenges & Success Factors

Challenges:

  • Partner selection and trust building
  • Control and decision-making conflicts
  • Knowledge sharing vs protection
  • Cultural differences between partners

Success Factors:

  • Clear objectives and expectations
  • Mutual benefit for all partners
  • Trust and transparency
  • Flexibility to adapt
💡 Indian Strategic Alliances — Detailed Examples
  • Tata Starbucks (JV, 2012): Tata Consumer Products + Starbucks (50:50). Tata provided local supply chain (Tata Coffee beans), real estate knowledge, and regulatory expertise. Starbucks provided global brand, coffee expertise. 300+ stores, $200M+ revenue in India.
  • Hero Honda (JV, 1984 → Hero MotoCorp, 2011): Hero Cycles + Honda Motor (26:26). Honda provided 4-stroke engine technology, Hero provided distribution network (5,000+ dealers). Hero bought out Honda’s stake for $1.2B when Honda wanted independent operations.
  • Renault-Nissan Alliance: Cross-shareholding (15% each), shared platforms, joint manufacturing (Chennai plant produces for both brands). Saved $5B+ through shared R&D and procurement.
  • SpiceJet-Boeing: Aircraft purchase agreement (205 737 MAX planes, $22B) with pilot training and maintenance support — strategic partnership beyond simple procurement.
📋 Alliance Lifecycle
  • Formation: Partner selection, negotiation, agreement structuring
  • Operation: Day-to-day management, resource sharing, conflict resolution
  • Evolution: Scope expansion, restructuring, or scope reduction
  • Termination: Buyout (one partner acquires other), dissolution, or transformation to JV/WOS
  • Indian Example: Hero Honda → Hero MotoCorp (buyout); Tata Starbucks (ongoing JV)

Unit 10: International Marketing

📖 Core Concepts
  • International Marketing = marketing across national borders
  • EPRG framework defines 4 orientations toward internationalization
  • Standardization vs Adaptation is the key strategic decision
International Marketing: The application of marketing principles across national boundaries to satisfy global customer needs.
EPRG Framework: Ethnocentric, Polycentric, Region-centric, and Geocentric orientations toward internationalization.
Standardization: Using the same marketing strategy (product, price, place, promotion) across all markets.
Adaptation: Modifying the marketing mix to suit local market conditions in each country.
📋 9 Drivers of International Marketing
  1. Liberalization of markets
  2. Tax advantages (tax havens like Singapore, Cyprus)
  3. Free trade under trading blocs
  4. Growth in market share
  5. Limited domestic market saturation
  6. Technology and communication
  7. Cultural exchanges (Indian diaspora in Gulf)
  8. Improved transport and logistics
  9. Changing demographics
📊 EPRG Framework
OrientationApproachExample
EthnocentricHome country approach applied everywherePatanjali Ayurved globally
PolycentricEach country treated separatelyApollo Hospitals in Middle East
Region-centricRegional approach for similar marketsUnilever in South Asia
GeocentricGlobal integrated approachApple's global product strategy
📊 International Marketing Mix (4Ps Adaptation)
ElementStandardizationAdaptation
ProductSame product globallyAdapt to local tastes
PriceUniform pricingAdjust to local purchasing power
PlaceSame distributionLocal channel structures
PromotionSame messageCulturally adapted messaging
📋 Challenges of International Marketing
  • Technological changes (e-commerce, Industry 4.0)
  • World economy integration ("global village")
  • Consumer empowerment (information access)
  • Greater competition (local + global brands)
  • Retail boom (big retailers, e-commerce)
📋 Standardization vs Adaptation — Deep Dive

Arguments for Standardization:

  • Cost efficiency (economies of scale in production and advertising)
  • Consistent global brand image (Apple, Nike)
  • Simplified management and coordination

Arguments for Adaptation:

  • Cultural differences in consumer behavior and preferences
  • Legal and regulatory requirements vary by country
  • Different levels of economic development affect pricing
  • Distribution channel structures differ across markets

The Reality: Most MNCs pursue a glocalization strategy — standardized core product with localized marketing mix elements.

💡 Indian Companies — International Marketing
  • Amul: Uses ethnocentric approach — same dairy products marketed to Indian diaspora in 50+ countries
  • ITC Hotels: Adapts food menus to local tastes in overseas properties (Middle East halal, Western breakfast)
  • Tata Global Beverages (Tetley): Adapted tea blends for UK (strong black tea), Canada (orange pekoe), Russia (flavored teas)
  • Mahindra: Adapted SUV design for US market (larger size, left-hand drive, different safety standards)
  • Zomato: Acquired local food delivery companies (Uber Eats India, Urbanspoon Australia) for market-specific knowledge

Unit 11: International Finance

📖 Core Concepts
  • International Monetary System evolved from Gold Standard to floating rates
  • Foreign exchange market enables currency conversion
  • Exchange rate risk requires hedging strategies
Foreign Exchange Market: A global marketplace for buying and selling currencies.
Exchange Rate: The price of one currency expressed in terms of another.
Spot Market: A market for immediate delivery of currencies (typically T+2 settlement).
Forward Market: A market for future delivery of currencies at a pre-agreed rate.
Hedging: Using financial instruments to reduce or eliminate foreign exchange risk.
Purchasing Power Parity (PPP): A theory that exchange rates should equalize the price of identical goods across countries.
📊 Evolution of Monetary System
Gold Standard (pre-1914) ↓ Bretton Woods (1944-1971) — Fixed rates, USD pegged to gold ↓ Floating Rates (1971-present) — Market-determined rates ↓ Current System — Managed float with intervention
📋 Key International Financial Institutions
  • IMF — promotes international monetary cooperation, provides emergency lending
  • World Bank — provides development financing to developing countries
📊 Foreign Exchange Market
SegmentDescription

Forward MarketFuture delivery at agreed rate
FuturesStandardized contracts on exchanges
OptionsRight but not obligation to buy/sell

📋 IMF — Key Functions
  • Promotes international monetary cooperation and exchange rate stability
  • Provides emergency lending to countries facing balance of payments crises (e.g., Sri Lanka 2022, Pakistan 2023)
  • Conducts Article IV consultations to monitor member countries’ economic policies
  • Manages the Special Drawing Rights (SDR) — international reserve asset
  • Provides technical assistance on fiscal policy, monetary policy, and financial regulation
📋 World Bank Group Institutions
  • IBRD (International Bank for Reconstruction & Development) — loans to middle-income countries
  • IDA (International Development Association) — concessional loans/credits to poorest countries
  • IFC (International Finance Corporation) — private sector investment in developing countries
  • MIGA (Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency) — political risk insurance for investors
📋 Hedging Techniques
  • Forward contracts — lock in future exchange rate
  • Futures — standardized exchange-traded contracts
  • Options — right to buy/sell at strike price
  • Money market hedge — borrow/lend in foreign currency
📋 International Capital Markets
  • Eurobonds — bonds in currency other than issuer's home currency
  • Eurocurrency — deposits in currency outside home country
  • ADRs — American Depositary Receipts (foreign shares on US exchanges)
  • GDRs — Global Depositary Receipts

Unit 12: International Operations & Logistics Management

📖 Core Concepts
  • Location decisions balance costs, infrastructure, labor, markets, government
  • Production strategies: centralized, decentralized, rationalized
  • Global supply chain management spans sourcing to distribution
International Operations Management: The management of production and service delivery processes across national borders.
Centralized Production: Concentrating production in one location to serve the entire world (high economies of scale).
Decentralized Production: Producing locally in each market to reduce transport costs and adapt to local needs.
Rationalized Production: Specializing individual plants in specific components or products for global distribution.
Global Supply Chain: The network of suppliers, manufacturers, and distributors spanning multiple countries.
Outsourcing: Contracting out business activities to external providers, often in lower-cost countries.
📊 Production Strategies
StrategyDescriptionWhen Used
CentralizedOne location for worldHigh economies of scale, low transport costs
DecentralizedLocal production for local marketsHigh transport costs, local adaptation needed
RationalizedSpecialized plants for componentsComplex products, trade agreements
📋 Global Supply Chain Management
  • Sourcing — procurement from global suppliers
  • Manufacturing — production across countries
  • Distribution — delivery to global markets
  • Tools: JIT (Just-in-Time), TQM (Total Quality Management)
📋 Challenges
  • Coordination across time zones and cultures
  • Quality control at distant locations
  • Supply chain disruptions (pandemics, natural disasters)
  • Cultural differences in work practices
📋 Outsourcing vs Offshoring vs Nearshoring
  • Outsourcing: Contracting activities to external providers (may be domestic or foreign)
  • Offshoring: Moving activities to a distant foreign country (e.g., US IT services to India)
  • Nearshoring: Moving activities to a nearby country (e.g., US manufacturing to Mexico)
  • Reshoring: Bringing activities back to home country (post-pandemic trend)
  • India’s Outsourcing Dominance: $194B IT-BPM industry, 55% global outsourcing market share, 5M+ IT workers
📋 Location Decision Factors — Detailed
  • Factor Costs: Labor, raw materials, energy, land costs
  • Infrastructure: Transport, ports, telecom, power reliability
  • Market Proximity: Closeness to customers reduces delivery time and transport costs
  • Government Incentives: SEZs (India has 260+), tax holidays, subsidies, FDI-friendly policies
  • Agglomeration Effects: Industry clusters (Bangalore for IT, Detroit for auto, Shenzhen for electronics)
  • Risk Diversification: Multi-country production reduces dependence on single location
💡 Indian Manufacturing — Global Operations
  • Tata Motors: Manufacturing in India, Thailand, South Africa, UK; export to 100+ countries
  • Bajaj Auto: World’s largest 3-wheeler manufacturer; exports to 70+ countries from Indian plants
  • Motherson Sumi: 270+ plants across 41 countries; supplies wiring harnesses to all major auto OEMs
  • Infosys: Global delivery model with development centers in India, Poland, Mexico, China (“follow the sun” model)

Unit 13: International Human Resource Management (IHRM)

📖 Core Concepts
  • IHRM = managing people in international context
  • Four staffing approaches determine who manages foreign operations
  • Expatriate management is the core challenge
IHRM: Management of human resources in international context including expatriate management, cross-cultural training, and global compensation.
Ethnocentric Staffing: Filling key positions with home country nationals.
Polycentric Staffing: Filling key positions with host country nationals.
Geocentric Staffing: Hiring the best person regardless of nationality.
Expatriate: Employee sent to work in a foreign country for an extended period.
Culture Shock: Disorientation experienced when encountering unfamiliar cultural environment.
Repatriation: Process of returning expatriate to home country after international assignment.
📊 Staffing Approaches
ApproachKey Positions Filled ByWhen Used
EthnocentricHome country nationalsEarly internationalization, technology transfer
PolycentricHost country nationalsMature operations, local knowledge needed
GeocentricBest person regardless of nationalityGlobal integration strategy
RegiocentricManagers from same regionRegional strategy
📊 Expatriate Management
PhaseKey Activities
SelectionTechnical competence, cross-cultural adaptability, family situation
TrainingLanguage, cultural awareness, practical preparation

📋 Challenges of IHRM
  • Cultural adjustment and culture shock
  • Dual career couples (spouse employment)
  • Legal requirements (visas, work permits)
  • Performance evaluation across different cultural contexts
  • Different labor relations in each country
📋 Culture Shock — Four Stages
  • Honeymoon Stage: Excitement, fascination with new culture (first few weeks)
  • Crisis/Frustration Stage: Differences become frustrating, homesickness, anxiety (1-6 months)
  • Adjustment Stage: Gradual adaptation, developing coping strategies (6-12 months)
  • Acceptance Stage: Comfort in new culture, bicultural competence (12+ months)
  • Reverse Culture Shock: Difficulty readjusting to home culture upon return
📋 Expatriate Failure — Causes and Prevention

Common Causes of Expatriate Failure (15-40% failure rate):

  • Inability to adapt to new culture (spouse/family dissatisfaction is #1 cause)
  • Lack of cross-cultural training
  • Overemphasis on technical competence, underemphasis on soft skills
  • Inadequate compensation and support systems

Prevention Strategies:

  • Comprehensive cross-cultural training before departure
  • Include spouse/family in pre-departure orientation
  • Mentoring by former expatriates
  • Clear repatriation plan and career path
💡 Indian Companies — IHRM Practices
  • TCS: Uses geocentric staffing — hires local talent in 46 countries, sends Indian managers for leadership roles, “Global Internship Program” for cross-cultural exposure
  • Tata Steel: Post-Corus acquisition, retained UK management (polycentric), gradually integrated Indian leadership for knowledge transfer
  • Infosys: “Localization” strategy — hires 60%+ local workforce in US/Europe, reduces H-1B visa dependency
  • Mahindra: Ssangyong (Korea) acquisition used Korean CEOs initially (polycentric), shifted to Indian oversight after stabilization
  • Wipro: “Centers of Excellence” model — specialized teams in different countries (analytics in India, consulting in US, engineering in Germany)
