Unit 1: Business Ethics — An Overview

📖 Unit 1: Ethics Fundamentals

Business Ethics: The prescribed code of conduct for businesses; a set of guidelines for dealing with various procedures ethically.

"Business ethics begins where the Law ends" — Ethics goes beyond legal minimum requirements.

Ethics is contextual — conditioned by prevailing power structures in society. Globalization increases ethical complexity as corporates are sandwiched between state regulatory agencies and civil society vigilance.

▶
Ethics vs Law

Point 1: Ethics vs Law

AspectLawEthics
NatureCodified, enforceableNot codified, contextual

💡 Real-World Example

Volkswagen's emission scandal (Dieselgate) — the EPA's stringent emission norms led VW to resort to unethical practices, later recalling 11,000 cars. Shows how sustainability pressures can lead to ethical breaches.

🧠 Memory Aid

"EEL" — Ethics Evolves from Law (ethics goes beyond what law requires)

Point 2: Why Business Ethics Matters

📖 Deeper Understanding

Crane & Matten Definition: Business ethics is the study of business situations, activities, and decisions where issues of right and wrong are addressed.

Ethics is contextual — conditioned by prevailing power structures in society. What is considered ethical may vary across cultures and time periods.

Globalization Impact: Corporates are sandwiched between state regulatory agencies and civil society vigilance. Supply chain ethics across borders adds complexity.

Sustainability Link: Environmental concerns are driving new regulatory frameworks. Companies face pressure to balance profit with planet.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Business ethics begins where the Law ends
  • Ethics is contextual, evolving, not codified
  • Globalization increases ethical complexity
  • Sustainability concerns are driving new ethical frameworks

Unit 2: Concepts and Theories of Business Ethics

▶
Ethical Theories Explained

Point 1: Two Schools of Ethical Theories

📖 Ethical Theories (DUTCH)
TheoryPrincipleApplication
Utilitarianism (Bentham/Mill)Greatest good for greatest numberCost-benefit analysis
Deontology (Kant)Duty-based; categorical imperativeCompliance, contracts
Virtue Ethics (Aristotle)Character and moral virtuesLeadership by example
Social Contract (Rawls)Justice as fairnessBehind "veil of ignorance"
Stakeholder Theory (Freeman)Balance all stakeholder interestsCorporate governance

Consequentialism (Teleology): Actions judged by outcomes/consequences

Non-consequentialism (Deontology): Actions judged by inherent rightness, regardless of outcomes

Categorical Imperative: Act only according to rules you could will to be universal laws (Kant)

🎯 Exam Tip

Compare and contrast teleological vs deontological approaches with examples. Examiners love this comparison. Always mention Kant's categorical imperative.

💡 Real-World Example

A pharmaceutical company pricing AIDS drugs affordably in Africa (utilitarian approach) vs. maintaining high prices to fund R&D (consequentialist approach to long-term innovation).

🧠 Memory Aid

"DUTCH" — Deontology, Utilitarianism, Teleology, Character (virtue), Hypothetical (social contract)

Point 2: Ethical Relativism

📖 Ethical Relativism

Ethical Relativism: Ethics vary by culture and context. What is considered ethical in one culture may not be in another.

Stakeholder Theory (Freeman): Balance interests of all stakeholders — employees, customers, suppliers, community, environment.

Freeman's Four Propositions: Separation Fallacy, Integration Thesis, Open Question Argument, Responsibility Principle.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Consequentialism (outcomes) vs Non-consequentialism (inherent rightness)
  • 5 major theories: Utilitarianism, Deontology, Virtue Ethics, Social Contract, Stakeholder
  • Ethical relativism: ethics vary by culture and context
  • Freeman's stakeholder theory balances all stakeholder interests

Unit 3: Ethical Dilemmas

Point 1: Ethical Dilemma Frameworks

📖 Key Frameworks

Ethical Navigation Wheel (Kvalnes & Ovarenget): Framework for navigating dilemmas systematically

Moral Compass (Lynn Paine): Guide for ethical decision-making based on moral principles

Ethical Check Points (Rushworth Kidder): Systematic approach to resolving dilemmas

Social Accounting: Measuring and reporting social and environmental impact

Ethical Navigation WheelMoral CompassEthical Check PointsSocial Accounting
🎯 Exam Tip

Present ethical dilemmas using a structured framework: identify stakeholders, apply ethical theories, evaluate alternatives, recommend action.

💡 Real-World Example

Online gaming companies designing addictive games — balancing profit maximization against potential harm to youth mental health. Is it ethical to create products designed to be addictive?

🧠 Memory Aid

"NICE" — Navigation wheel, Identify stakeholders, Check points, Evaluate alternatives

Point 2: Types of Ethical Dilemmas

TypeDescriptionExample
Truth vs LoyaltyTelling truth vs being loyal to organizationWhistleblowing on company fraud

📖 Social Accounting

Social Accounting: The process of communicating the social and environmental effects of an organization's actions to particular interest groups and to society at large.

It measures triple bottom line performance: economic, social, and environmental impact.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Ethical dilemma = all options are ethically problematic
  • Use structured frameworks: Navigation Wheel, Moral Compass, Check Points
  • 4 types: Truth vs Loyalty, Individual vs Community, Short-term vs Long-term, Justice vs Mercy
  • Social Accounting measures triple bottom line performance

Unit 4: Ethics in Business (IBS Framework)

Point 1: IBS Framework

📊 IBS Framework (Individual-Business-Society)
Individual Level
Personal values, moral development, cognitive moral development
Business Level
Organizational culture, ethical climate, codes of conduct
Society Level
Social norms, cultural values, legal frameworks
📖 Key Definitions

Corporate Governance: System of rules, practices, and processes by which a company is directed and controlled

Code of Ethics: Formal document outlining the principles and values that guide an organization's behavior

Stakeholder: Any individual or group that can affect or is affected by the achievement of the organization's objectives

Point 2: Shareholder vs Stakeholder

AspectFriedman (Shareholder)Freeman (Stakeholder)
GoalMaximize shareholder valueBalance ALL stakeholder interests
Quote"Business of business is to do business"Purpose connected to all affected parties
FocusProfit onlyEmployees, customers, community, environment
💡 Real-World Example

The Enron scandal (2001) — failure of corporate governance, auditor independence, and ethical leadership led to one of the largest corporate bankruptcies in history.

🧠 Memory Aid

"IBS" — Individual, Business, Society (three levels of ethical analysis)

Point 3: Individual Level — Moral Development

📖 Cognitive Moral Development (Kohlberg)

Level 1 — Pre-conventional: Self-interest, avoiding punishment

Level 2 — Conventional: Conforming to social norms, law and order

Level 3 — Post-conventional: Universal ethical principles, social contract

✅ Unit Summary

  • IBS Framework: Individual, Business, Society levels
  • Friedman (shareholder) vs Freeman (stakeholder) debate
  • Corporate governance bridges ethics and CSR
  • Kohlberg's 3 levels of moral development

Point 3: Key CSR Concepts

📖 Important Definitions

Corporate Citizenship: The extent to which businesses meet legal, ethical, and economic responsibilities as established by shareholders.

Ethical Consumerism: Consumers making purchasing decisions based on human rights, social justice, and environmental concerns.

Socially Responsible Investment (SRI): Investment considering financial returns AND social/environmental impact.

Howard Bowen (1953): "Social obligations of businessmen" — first formal definition of CSR.

✅ Unit Summary

  • CSR evolved from voluntary (1950s) to mandatory (India, 2013)
  • 4 types of activism driving CSR: Consumer, CSO, Shareholder, Employee
  • Corporate Citizenship = meeting legal, ethical, economic responsibilities
  • Ethical Consumerism and SRI are growing trends

Ethical Leadership: Leaders setting the tone for ethical organizational culture through their actions and decisions.

Social Audit: A systematic evaluation of an organization's social impact and ethical performance.

Unit 10: CSR Legislations and Policy Guidelines

Point 1: Section 135 — The 2-3-5 Rule

📖 Companies Act 2013, Section 135

Threshold Criteria (any one triggers CSR obligation):

Net worth ≥ ₹500 croreTurnover ≥ ₹1,000 croreNet profit ≥ ₹5 crore

2% Rule: Must spend 2% of average net profits of last 3 years on CSR

CSR Committee: Minimum 3 directors, at least 1 independent director

Point 2: Schedule VII Activities

EducationHealthcareEnvironmentGender EqualityRural DevelopmentSwachh Bharat

Point 3: 2021 Amendments

Admin overheads cap (5%)Impact assessmentCSR-1 registrationExcess spend carry-forward (3 years)
📐 Case Study: India's Unique CSR Law

India made history by becoming the first country to give statutory recognition to CSR in corporate legislation. The CSR provisions evolved from voluntary guidelines (2009) to mandatory provisions (2013) to strengthened rules (2021). India is one of ONLY 3 countries with mandatory CSR (along with Mauritius and one other).

🧠 Memory Aid

"2-3-5 Rule" — 2% of profits, 3-year average, ₹5 Cr net profit threshold

🎯 Exam Tip

Section 135 of Companies Act 2013 is the most examable topic in CSR. Always mention the 2-3-5 Rule and Schedule VII. India is one of ONLY 3 countries with mandatory CSR.

📖 2021 Amendments

Admin overheads: Capped at 5% of total CSR expenditure

Impact assessment: Required for companies with CSR obligation ≥ ₹10 crore

CSR-1 registration: Required for implementing agencies

Excess spend: Can be set off against CSR obligations for next 3 years

✅ Unit Summary

  • Section 135: 2% of avg net profits for eligible companies
  • Threshold: ₹500Cr net worth OR ₹1000Cr turnover OR ₹5Cr profit
  • India = first country with mandatory CSR law

Point 2: BRR (Business Responsibility Reporting)

📖 BRR Framework

9 principles for listed companies to report on social, environmental, and governance performance.

CSR reporting = disclosure; CSR audit = verification. Know the difference between BRR (for listed companies) and CSR reporting (for all eligible companies).

  • Impact assessment required for companies with CSR obligation ≥ ₹10 crore

Unit 13: Roles and Responsibilities of CSR Department

Point 1: CSR Committee Composition

📖 Key Rules

Minimum 3 directors, 1 independent

Administrative overheads: Capped at 5% of total CSR expenditure (2021 amendment)

Excess CSR spend: Can be set off against CSR obligations for next 3 years

Make vs Buy Decision: In-house CSR implementation vs implementing agencies

🎯 Exam Tip

CSR Committee composition is a common exam question: 3 directors minimum, 1 independent director mandatory.

💡 Real-World Example

Mahindra & Mahindra's Nanhi Kali programme for girls' education; Lifeline Express (hospital on train); Mahindra Hariyali (0.95 million trees planted).

🧠 Memory Aid

"3-1-5" — 3 directors minimum, 1 independent, 5% admin overhead cap

Point 2: Make vs Buy Decision

AspectIn-House (Make)Implementing Agency (Buy)
ControlDirect control over activitiesDelegated to agency
ExpertiseMay lack specialized knowledgeAgency has established track record
CostHigher overhead costsMore cost-effective
RegistrationNot requiredMust file CSR-1 with MCA

✅ Unit Summary

  • CSR Committee: 3 directors, 1 independent
  • Make vs Buy decision: in-house vs implementing agencies
  • Implementing agencies must have 3+ years track record and CSR-1 registration
  • Tax issues: CSR expenditure generally not deductible as business expense

Unit 14: CSR and Sustainable Development

▶
Sustainable Development

Point 1: Sustainable Development

📖 Brundtland Commission (1987)

"Meeting present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet their own needs."

Circular Economy: Reduce, reuse, recycle approach to minimize waste

Carbon Neutrality: Balancing carbon emissions with carbon removal

Social Audit: Systematic evaluation of an organization's social impact

Point 2: Triple Bottom Line

📊 Triple Bottom Line (Elkington)
👥 People (Social Impact)
🌍 Planet (Environmental)
💰 Profit (Financial)

Point 3: ESG and Circular Economy

ESG FrameworkCircular EconomyCarbon NeutralitySocial Audit
💡 Real-World Example

Mark & Spencer became UK's greenest retailer, pledging carbon neutrality and zero waste to landfill. Toyota Prius pioneered hybrid electric vehicles for mainstream consumers.

🧠 Memory Aid

"PPP" — People, Planet, Profit (Triple Bottom Line); "ESG" — Environmental, Social, Governance

🎯 Exam Tip

Link sustainable development to SDGs and Triple Bottom Line. Use ESG framework to demonstrate understanding of modern CSR evaluation.

📐 Case Study: Amul — India's Greatest CSR Story

Amul (GCMMF) is arguably India's most impactful CSR success. Economic: 3.6 million dairy farmers earn livelihood. Legal: Follows FSSAI standards, cooperative laws. Ethical: Pays farmers 80% of consumer price (vs. 40-50% in private dairies). Philanthropic: Dr. Verghese Kurien's "White Revolution" made India the world's largest milk producer. Amul proves that a business can be supremely profitable AND deeply ethical — the living embodiment of Carroll's CSR Pyramid.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Sustainable development = meet present needs without compromising future
  • Triple Bottom Line: People + Planet + Profit
  • ESG framework connects ethics, CSR, and governance
