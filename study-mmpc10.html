Block-01: Scope of Managerial Economics & Demand Analysis

Unit 1: Scope of Managerial Economics

📖 Core Definition

Managerial Economics = Applied Microeconomics for business decisions. It bridges the gap between economic theory and business practice, focusing on how firms react to economic environment changes. It addresses three central economic problems: What to produce, How to produce, and For whom to produce.

Scope: Resource allocation, inventory management, pricing decisions, and capital budgeting. It uses economic concepts like demand, supply, elasticity, and market structure to guide business decisions.

⚠ Key Insight

Managerial Economics is normative (prescriptive) — it tells managers what they should do, unlike pure economics which is positive (descriptive). It combines microeconomic theory with decision sciences like operations research and statistics.

⚠ Four Types of Market Failure

1. Monopoly — Deadweight loss from reduced output. 2. Public Goods — Non-excludable (national defense, clean air). 3. Externalities — Pollution costs borne by third parties. 4. Information Asymmetry — "Market for lemons" problem.

🧠 Memory Aid: RIP-I

4 problem groups: Resource allocation, Inventory & Queuing, Pricing, Investment.

💡 Indian Example

Reliance Jio’s entry into telecom is a classic case of managerial economics — they analyzed demand elasticity, pricing strategy, and market structure before launching free voice + cheap data, disrupting the entire industry.

✅ Unit 1 Summary

  • Three central problems: What, How, For whom to produce
  • Market mechanism: Supply ∩ Demand = Equilibrium price
  • Four market failures: Monopoly, Public Goods, Externalities, Info Asymmetry
  • Four problem groups: Resource, Inventory, Pricing, Investment (RIP-I)

⚠ Law of Demand: Price ↑ → Quantity ↓

Income Effect: Price drop → real income ↑ → buy more. Substitution Effect: Price drop → relatively cheaper → substitute toward it. Exceptions: Giffen goods (inferior, income effect > substitution effect), Veblen goods (status signaling).

⚠ Shift vs Movement — Critical Distinction

Movement along curve: Only when own price changes. Shift of curve: When any other determinant changes. Remember: "Move on Price, Shift on the rest."

💡 Indian Example

When onion prices surged to ₹150/kg in India, demand fell sharply — households switched to alternatives like cabbage and beetroot. Government had to impose export bans and import onions from Turkey and Egypt.

📋 Case Study: Jio’s Impact on Telecom Demand

When Reliance Jio launched in 2016 with free voice + ₹50/GB data, the entire telecom demand curve shifted right — millions of new users entered the market. Existing operators (Airtel, Vodafone) had to match prices. This demonstrates: 1) Price elasticity of demand in India’s price-sensitive market, 2) How a new entrant can shift industry demand, 3) Substitution effect as users switched from 2G/3G to 4G.

✅ Unit 2-3 Summary

  • Demand function: Qd = f(Po, Pc, Ps, Yd, T, A, CR, R, E, N, O)
  • Law of Demand: Price ↑ → Quantity ↓ (Ceteris Paribus)
  • Income and Substitution effects explain the Law
  • Exceptions: Giffen goods, Veblen goods, Speculative goods
  • Market demand = Horizontal summation of individual demands
  • Types: Individual, Market, Joint, Derived, Competitive demand

💡 Diamond-Water Paradox

Value in Use vs Value in Exchange explains the paradox: Water has high use value (essential for life) but low exchange value (abundant). Diamonds have low use value but high exchange value (scarce). Resolution: Price depends on marginal utility, not total utility. The marginal unit of water is cheap because water is abundant.

💡 Indian Example

During COVID-19, demand for face masks shifted massively to the right (taste/awareness change), while within each day, higher prices caused movement along the demand curve as fewer people bought at inflated prices.

✅ Unit 4 Summary

  • Value in Use vs Value in Exchange: Diamond-water paradox
  • Effective Demand = Willingness + Ability to pay
  • Movement = own price change; Shift = other determinants change
  • Demand always measured over a time period

💡 Elasticity in Action

Petrol in India: Highly inelastic (|Ed| < 0.5) — even when prices cross ₹100/litre, commuters still buy because alternatives are limited. iPhones: Elastic demand (|Ed| > 1) — a 10% price increase leads to >10% drop in sales because Android alternatives exist. Life-saving drugs: Perfectly inelastic (|Ed| = 0) — patients pay any price.

🧠 Memory Aid: ENUP

Elasticity determinants: Expenditure share, Necessity vs Luxury, Ubstitutes availability, Period of time.

Arc Elasticity Formula: Ed = [(Q2-Q1)/(Q2+Q1)] / [(P2-P1)/(P2+P1)] — Use midpoint method for accuracy between two points.

Point Elasticity Formula: Ed = (dQ/dP) × (P/Q) — Elasticity at a specific point on the demand curve.

Income Elasticity: Ey = (ΔQ/ΔY) × (Y/Q) — Ey > 0 = normal good, Ey < 0 = inferior good.

Cross Elasticity: Exy = (ΔQx/ΔPy) × (Py/Qx) — Exy > 0 = substitutes, Exy < 0 = complements.

💡 Indian Example

Petrol in India is inelastic — even when prices cross ₹100/litre, people still buy because there are few substitutes for commuting. But luxury goods like iPhones have elastic demand — a price hike significantly reduces sales.

📋 Case Study: Elasticity of Indian Goods

Inelastic goods in India: Petrol (|Ed| ~0.3), Salt (|Ed| ~0.1), Insulin (|Ed| ~0.0),自来水 (|Ed| ~0.2). These are necessities with few substitutes. Elastic goods in India: Air travel (|Ed| ~1.5), Restaurant meals (|Ed| ~1.2), Branded clothing (|Ed| ~1.8), Electronics (|Ed| ~1.4). These are luxuries with many substitutes. Policy implication: Government can tax inelastic goods (petrol, tobacco) without significantly reducing consumption, generating more revenue.

Key Formulas: Arc Ed = [(Q2-Q1)/(Q2+Q1)] / [(P2-P1)/(P2+P1)] • Point Ed = (dQ/dP) × (P/Q) • TR = P × Q • MR = dTR/dQ

✅ Unit 5 Summary

  • Three elasticities: Price, Income, Cross
  • Elastic (|Ed|>1): Price ↑ → Revenue ↓
  • Inelastic (|Ed|<1): Price ↑ → Revenue ↑
  • Determinants: Substitutes, Necessity, Income share, Time (ENUP)
  • Arc vs Point elasticity methods for calculation

Unit 7: Production Function (Critical Unit)

📖 Production Function

Q = f(L, K) — Maximum output from given input combination. Technical Efficiency: Max output from given inputs. Economic Efficiency: Given output at lowest cost (considers input prices). Short Run: At least one input fixed. Long Run: All inputs variable.

▶
Production Function TP AP MP
▶
Law of Diminishing Returns
▶
Isoquants & Returns to Scale
MeasureFormulaBehavior
TP (Total Product)Total outputRises, peaks, then falls
AP (Average Product)TP / LRises, peaks, then falls
MP (Marginal Product)ΔTP / ΔLRises first, then falls (diminishing returns)
⚠ Critical MP-AP Relationships

MP > AP → AP rising. MP < AP → AP falling. MP = AP → AP at maximum. MP = 0 → TP at maximum. MP < 0 → TP falling. Cricket Analogy: If Sachin averages 50 and scores 100, his average rises. If he scores 0, average falls.

⚠ Optimal Input Hiring Rule

A firm should hire inputs until: Value of Marginal Product (VMP) = Input Price. For labor: VMP = P × MPL = w (wage rate). For capital: VMP = P × MPK = r (rental rate). This ensures the last unit of input hired generates revenue exactly equal to its cost.

Key Formulas: AP = TP/L • MP = ΔTP/ΔL • MRTS = MPL/MPK • Optimal: MRTS = w/r • VMP = P × MP = w

🧠 Memory Aid: TAM-IRS

Production concepts: Total product, Average product, Marginal product, Isoquants, Returns to scale, Stages of production.

💡 Indian Example

Indian farmers experience diminishing returns when adding more fertilizer to a fixed plot of land — initial increases boost yield, but excessive fertilizer actually reduces crop output (Stage III).

📋 Case Study: Indian IT Industry Production Function

TCS Infosys Production Analysis: Indian IT companies show clear production function behavior. Stage I: When TCS hires first 1000 developers, each additional developer adds more output (specialization benefits). Stage II: Between 1000-500000 developers, MP is positive but declining — communication overhead increases. Stage III: Beyond 500000 developers, coordination costs become so high that additional hires reduce total output. Optimal: TCS maintains ~500000 employees — in Stage II, where MP > 0 but MP < AP. MRTS: TCS substitutes capital (AI tools) for labor (developers) as AI becomes cheaper — MRTS = MPL/MPK = w/r.

Key Formulas: AP = TP/L • MP = ΔTP/ΔL • MRTS = MPL/MPK • Optimal: MRTS = w/r • VMP = P × MP = w

✅ Unit 7 Summary

  • Production function: Q = f(L, K) — max output from inputs
  • Three stages: Stage II is optimal (MP>0, MP
  • Law of Diminishing Returns: MP eventually declines
  • Isoquants show same-output input combinations; MRTS = MPL/MPK
  • Returns to Scale: Increasing, Constant, Decreasing

⚠ Critical MC-ATC-AVC Relationships

MC < ATC → ATC falling. MC > ATC → ATC rising. MC = ATC → ATC at minimum. MC crosses both ATC and AVC at their minimum points. AVC reaches minimum at lower output than ATC.

⚠ Break-Even & Shutdown

Break-even: TR = TC → Q_BE = FC / (P - AVC). Shutdown point: P < AVC → stop production. Below Q_BE: Loss (but continue if P > AVC). Above Q_BE: Profit.

💡 Operating Leverage

Operating Leverage = Proportion of fixed costs in total costs. High fixed costs mean high operating leverage. Example: Airlines have high operating leverage (aircraft leases, crew salaries are fixed). When passenger load is high, profits soar. During COVID with low loads, losses were catastrophic because fixed costs remained. IndiGo's fixed costs are ~60% of total costs.

🧠 Memory Aid: FAVM-B

Cost concepts: Fixed, Average, Variable, Marginal, Break-even.

💡 Indian Example

Indian airlines like IndiGo have high operating leverage — aircraft leases are fixed costs. When passenger load is high, profits soar. During COVID with low loads, losses were catastrophic because fixed costs remained. IndiGo’s break-even load factor is ~75%.

📋 Case Study: Indian Steel Industry Cost Structure

Tata Steel vs JSW Steel: Both have high fixed costs (blast furnaces, mines, land). Fixed Costs: ~60% of total costs. Break-even: Tata Steel’s break-even is ~₹45,000/tonne. When steel prices fall below this, losses accumulate. Operating Leverage: When steel prices rose from ₹40,000 to ₹70,000/tonne (2020-2021), Tata Steel’s profit increased 10x. Shutdown: Even during price dips, production continues as long as P > AVC (₹30,000/tonne).

Key Formulas: TC = FC + VC • ATC = TC/Q • MC = ΔTC/ΔQ • Break-even: Q = FC/(P-AVC) • Profit = (P - ATC) × Q

✅ Unit 8 Summary

  • TC = FC + VC; ATC = AFC + AVC; MC = ΔTC/ΔQ
  • MC crosses ATC and AVC at their minimum points
  • Break-even: Q = FC/(P-AVC); Shutdown: P < AVC
  • AFC always declining (rectangular hyperbola)
  • Operating leverage = proportion of fixed costs

Unit 9: Long Run Cost Analysis

📖 Long Run Average Cost (LRAC)

Envelope Curve — Envelopes all short run AC curves. U-shaped due to economies then diseconomies of scale. Unique Point: At minimum LRAC: SRAC = SRMC = LRAC = LRMC. Cost-Output Elasticity: Ec = MC/AC; Ec < 1 = Economies of Scale.

⚠ Learning Curve & Scope

Learning Curve: C(n) = C(1) × n^(-b) — Costs decrease with cumulative production. b = learning parameter, n = cumulative units. Economies of Scope: TC(Q1, Q2) < TC(Q1,0) + TC(0,Q2) — producing together is cheaper than separately.

Cost-Output Elasticity: Ec = MC/AC • Ec < 1 → Economies of Scale • Ec = 1 → Constant Returns • Ec > 1 → Diseconomies of Scale

Learning Curve: C(n) = C(1) × n^(-b) where b = learning parameter, n = cumulative units produced

Economies of Scope: TC(Q1, Q2) < TC(Q1, 0) + TC(0, Q2) — Joint production is cheaper than separate production

⚠ Diseconomies of Scale

Causes: 1) Morale problems — larger workforce leads to less personal connection. 2) Communication breakdown — more management layers distort messages. 3) Coordination complexity — harder to align large teams. 4) Bureaucracy — excessive rules slow decisions.

⚠ Cost-Output Elasticity

Ec = MC / AC. When Ec < 1 → Economies of Scale (costs rise slower than output). When Ec = 1 → Constant Returns. When Ec > 1 → Diseconomies of Scale (costs rise faster than output).

Unit 11: Perfect Competition & Monopoly (Critical Unit)

📖 Profit Maximization Rule

MR = MC — Universal rule across ALL market structures. In Perfect Competition: P = MR = AR (horizontal demand). In Monopoly: MR < P, MR curve lies below demand curve.

💡 Price Discrimination Conditions

For price discrimination to work, three conditions must be met:

1) Market Power — firm can set price (not a price taker).

2) Market Segmentation — identifiable groups with different willingness to pay.

3) No Arbitrage — can't resell product between segments.

Example: Airlines charge business travelers 3-5x more than leisure travelers for the same seat because business travelers can't resell their tickets. Student discounts, senior citizen pricing, and geographic pricing are all forms of 3rd degree price discrimination.

Indian pharmaceutical companies practice 3rd degree price discrimination — same medicine sold at lower prices in India than in the US. Ola/Uber use surge pricing (1st degree) during peak hours.

📋 Case Study: Indian Railways — A Natural Monopoly

Why Monopoly? Indian Railways is a natural monopoly — massive capital requirements (tracks, stations, rolling stock) create insurmountable barriers to entry. Pricing: Railways practices 3rd degree price discrimination — Sleeper class (₹0.34/km), AC 3-tier (₹1.25/km), AC 2-tier (₹1.86/km), AC First (₹3.36/km). Different prices for different segments. MR = MC: Railways sets prices where marginal revenue from each class equals marginal cost of providing service. Welfare Issue: P > MC creates deadweight loss, but government subsidizes to make rail affordable. Recent Change: Private trains (Tejas Express) introduce competition, moving toward monopolistic competition.

✅ Unit 11 Summary

  • MR = MC is universal profit maximization rule
  • Perfect Comp: P = MC, zero LR profit, efficient
  • Monopoly: P > MC, supernormal LR profit, deadweight loss
  • Price Discrimination: 1st, 2nd, 3rd degree
  • Conditions: Market power, Segmentation, No arbitrage

Unit 12: Monopolistic & Oligopolistic Competition

📖 Monopolistic Competition

Many sellers, differentiated products, free entry. Short run: Like monopoly (MR = MC, can earn profits). Long run: Entry eliminates profits (P = ATC). Excess capacity: Produces left of minimum ATC. Non-price competition: Advertising, branding, quality.

⚠ Kinked Demand Curve (Sweezy)

Competitors match price cuts but don’t match price increases → price rigidity. Firms face different elasticity above and below current price.

⚠ Monopolistic Competition: Short Run vs Long Run

Short Run: Like monopoly — MR = MC, can earn supernormal profits. Long Run: Entry of new firms eliminates profits → P = ATC (tangent to demand curve). Excess Capacity: Produces left of minimum ATC — this is the cost of product variety. Non-price competition: Advertising, branding, quality differentiation become key strategies.

🧠 Memory Aid: COPS-G

Oligopoly: Collusion, Oligopolistic interdependence, Price rigidity, Strategic behavior, Game theory.

💡 Indian Example

Indian telecom (Jio, Airtel, Vi) is a classic oligopoly. When Jio launched free data, competitors had to match. OPEC is a global cartel example. Restaurants in Connaught Place operate in monopolistic competition — each offers a differentiated experience.

Concentration Measures:

CR4 = Market share of top 4 firms — simple measure of concentration

HHI = ∑(si²) where si = market share of firm i — more sophisticated measure

HHI < 1500: Unconcentrated • 1500-2500: Moderately concentrated • > 2500: Highly concentrated

📋 Case Study: Indian Telecom Oligopoly

Jio-Airtel-Vi Oligopoly: India’s telecom market is a textbook oligopoly with 3 major players. CR4: Top 3 firms control ~95% of market (highly concentrated). HHI: ~3500 (highly concentrated). Kinked Demand: When Airtel tried to raise prices in 2019, Jio didn’t follow — Airtel had to reverse. Game Theory: Jio’s entry in 2016 was a dominant strategy — free data forced competitors to match or lose subscribers. Cartel Attempt: In 2023, all three raised prices simultaneously (tacit collusion) — no formal cartel but coordinated behavior. Result: Prices rose 20-25% without losing subscribers (inelastic demand for data).

✅ Unit 12 Summary

  • Monopolistic Comp: Differentiated products, free entry, zero LR profit, excess capacity
  • Oligopoly: Few firms, interdependent, kinked demand, price rigidity
  • Models: Cournot (quantities), Bertrand (prices), Stackelberg (leader-follower), Sweezy (kinked)
  • Cartel: Firms collude to act as monopoly (OPEC)
  • Concentration: CR4, HHI measures

📖 Pricing Strategies

⚠ Transfer Pricing Rules

Competitive market: Transfer price = Market price (selling division can sell externally at market price).

No external market: Transfer price = Marginal cost of producing division (no alternative, so use cost).

Imperfect market: Negotiate between MC and market price (compromise between divisions).

Cost-Plus Pricing Formula: P = AVC + (Markup% × AVC) • Or: P = ATC + Target Profit Margin

Two-Part Pricing: Total Price = Fixed Fee (access) + Per-Unit Usage Fee • Optimal: Set usage fee = MC, extract surplus via fixed fee

Peak Load Pricing: Higher prices during peak demand, lower during off-peak to stimulate demand (electricity, airlines, hotels)

⚠ Bundling Strategy

Bundling is effective when customer demands are negatively correlated — one values product A highly but not B, while another values B but not A. By bundling, you extract more surplus from both. Example: Microsoft Office bundle (Word + Excel + PowerPoint) vs selling individually.

⚠ Two-Part Tariff Optimization

Optimal two-part tariff: Set usage fee = MC (marginal cost), then extract consumer surplus via fixed fee. Example: Amusement parks charge entry fee (fixed) + per-ride fee (usage). Clubs charge membership (fixed) + drink prices (usage). The fixed fee captures surplus that would otherwise go to consumers.

🧠 Memory Aid: CBT-PT

Pricing strategies: Cost-plus, Bundling, Two-part tariff, Peak load, Transfer pricing.

💡 Indian Example

Jio uses two-part tariff — monthly fixed fee + data charges. Airlines use peak load pricing (Diwali flights cost more). McDonald’s uses bundling (McAloo Tikki meal = burger + fries + drink cheaper than buying separately). Reliance uses transfer pricing between Refining and Retail divisions.

⚠ 8 Key Relationships

💡 Numerical Problem Solving

Break-Even Example: FC = ₹10 lakh, AVC = ₹500/unit, Price = ₹800/unit. Q_BE = 10,00,000 / (800 - 500) = 3,333 units. Elasticity Example: Price rises from ₹100 to ₹120, demand falls from 1000 to 800 units. Ed = [(800-1000)/(800+1000)] / [(120-100)/(120+100)] = [-200/1800] / [20/220] = -0.111 / 0.091 = -1.22 (elastic).
