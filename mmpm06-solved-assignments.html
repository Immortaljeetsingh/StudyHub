Q1. Define marketing research and explain the steps of the marketing research process.

Answer: Marketing research is the systematic and objective search for and analysis of data relevant to the identification and solution of any problem in the field of marketing (Green & Tull). The American Marketing Association adds that it links the consumer, customer, and public to the marketer through information. Research is needed whenever decision-makers face uncertainty and risk, provided the cost of research is less than the cost of a wrong decision and time is available. The research process has eight steps: 1) Problem definition, the most critical step, since "a problem well defined is a problem half-solved"; 2) Research objectives, specific measurable goals and hypotheses; 3) Research design, the exploratory, descriptive, or causal blueprint; 4) Sampling plan, defining the population, sample size, and method; 5) Data collection, through surveys, interviews, and observation; 6) Data preparation, coding, editing, and cleaning; 7) Analysis, using univariate, bivariate, and multivariate techniques; and 8) Report and presentation, with conclusions, recommendations, and an executive summary. Feedback loops exist at every stage. Amul's entry into the chocolate market illustrates the process: research on consumption patterns, price points, and distribution preceded the launch decision.

Q2. What are the types of research designs? Differentiate exploratory, descriptive, and causal research.

Answer: A research design is the framework or blueprint for conducting a marketing research project; it details the procedures for obtaining the information needed to address the research problem. There are three types. Exploratory research is used when the problem is unclear; it is flexible and low-structure, using literature review, expert interviews, focus groups, and pilot studies to discover insights, and answers "what is happening?". Descriptive research describes market characteristics and functions with pre-planned structure and specific hypotheses, typically using surveys, panels, and observation, and answers "how much, how many, who, and when?". Causal research establishes cause-and-effect relationships with the highest structure, using laboratory and field experiments and A/B testing, and answers "does X cause Y?". For example, interviews with managers to understand why market share is falling are exploratory; a customer satisfaction survey is descriptive; and testing whether extended store hours increase revenue, comparing experimental stores with control stores, is causal. The design choice depends on the research problem, the precision required, and cost and time constraints. Remember the mnemonic EDC: Exploratory, Descriptive, Causal.

Q3. What is a hypothesis? Explain the null and alternative hypotheses and the errors in hypothesis testing.

Answer: A hypothesis is a conjectural statement of the relationship between two or more variables (Kerlinger), written so it can be proven or disproven by valid and reliable data. A good hypothesis is simple and declarative, measurable, grounded in existing knowledge, and testable. The null hypothesis (H0) states that there is no relationship between variables; for example, "extended store hours do not significantly increase revenue". The alternative hypothesis (H1) states that there is a relationship; for example, "extended hours significantly increase revenue". Testing follows five steps: state H0 and H1, choose the significance level (alpha), select the test statistic, calculate and compare, and accept or reject H0. Two errors are possible. Type I error (alpha error) is rejecting a true null hypothesis, a false positive, like concluding a new ad campaign works when it does not; it is like convicting an innocent person. Type II error (beta error) is accepting a false null hypothesis, a false negative, like concluding the campaign fails when it actually works; it is like letting a guilty person go free. In the Indian retail case in the notes, H0 was rejected at 95% confidence after revenue rose 18%.

Tip: mention that hypotheses give focus, prevent delusion, and translate research objectives into testable propositions.

Q4. Discuss the challenges of conducting marketing research in India.

Answer: India's marketing research industry is the fourth largest globally, worth about $2.1 billion, but researchers face distinctive challenges. Linguistic diversity is foremost: with 22 official languages and 400+ dialects, questionnaires must be translated into at least 5-6 languages and back-translated to verify accuracy. Rural accessibility is difficult: roughly 70% of the population is rural, and rural markets grow faster than urban ones, yet face-to-face fieldwork is costly and slow. High attrition is a problem: about 30% annual turnover in MR firms, driven by low salaries, undermines continuity and quality. Quality concerns persist, with 58% of businesses feeling research quality falls short of expectations, and interviewers may "straightline" or steer interviews for quick completion, creating fraud risk. Time delays matter: 42% of managers feel research takes too long to be of real use. The five rules for research in India are: speak the local language, do not get lost in translation, respect culture and customs, do not always rely on technology in rural areas, and beware of fraud by implementing quality checks, GPS tracking, and call-back verification.

Tip: government sources such as NSSO, CMIE, and RBI surveys are valuable secondary data for research projects.
