Solved Answers - All Questions

Q1. What do you mean by 'strategy'? Discuss the features of strategy with examples.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the concept and definitions, 5 marks for features with examples.

Answer: Strategy is the grand design through which an organisation achieves its long-term objectives by deploying its resources to meet the challenges of a changing environment.

Strategy is the grand design that an organisation uses to achieve its objectives by deploying its resources in a changing environment. The word comes from the Greek strategos (army + to lead) - originally military, later adapted to business. Glueck calls it "a unified, comprehensive and integrated plan relating strategic advantage to environmental challenges"; Chandler says it is the determination of basic long-term goals plus the courses of action and resources to reach them; Porter adds that strategy is "the creation of a unique and valued position involving a different set of activities".

  • Features: long-term orientation, organisation-wide scope, resource allocation, fit with the external environment, flexibility, and the choice of a unique position.
  • Deliberate strategy is planned and executed exactly - Apple's iPhone launch plan; emergent strategy evolves step by step - Amazon's journey from books to everything.
  • Mintzberg's 5 Ps: Pattern (consistent behaviour over time), Plan (intended course), Position (market placement), Perspective (the organisation's vision) and Ploy (a tactic to outwit rivals).
  • Strategy vs policy vs tactics: strategy is action-oriented and flexible, policy is a fixed guideline, tactics are short-term moves.

Example: Apple's premium differentiation with its ecosystem (iPhone + Mac + iPad + Services) is a consistent pattern strategy that has created a unique position for decades. A firm without strategy is, in the classic phrase, "a ship without a rudder".

Key points to remember: strategy = long-term plan for objectives using resources; deliberate vs emergent; Mintzberg's 5 Ps (PaPPeP); flexible and action-oriented, unlike policy; Porter - unique position.

Takeaway: A firm without a consistent, unique strategy is "a ship without a rudder".

Q2. Write short notes on: (a) Core values (b) Strategic intent

Marks guide: 5 marks for each short note, with meaning, importance and an example.

Answer: Core values are the fundamental beliefs guiding behaviour (the 7S centre), while strategic intent is an ambitious stretch goal beyond current resources.

(a) Core values are the fundamental beliefs that guide an organisation's behaviour and decisions, even when they cost money or convenience. In the McKinsey 7S framework, shared values sit at the centre - the foundation that holds Strategy, Structure, Systems, Style, Staff and Skills together. Core values are stable over time and shape culture: Tata Group's values of integrity, excellence and responsibility have guided it for over 150 years; Infosys's values of client value, integrity and fairness built trust in global markets. They matter because values drive daily decisions that strategy documents cannot cover - a company that genuinely values safety will stop production for a faulty batch even at a loss.

(b) Strategic intent (Hamel & Prahalad, 1989) is the ambitious, obsessive long-term goal that stretches the organisation beyond its current resources and capabilities, guiding resource allocation, effort and motivation. Examples: Komatsu's "Encircle Caterpillar", Canon's "Beat Xerox", Honda's "crush Yamaha" and Infosys's "Win in the flat world". Strategic intent sits on the hierarchy of Vision → Mission → Business Definition → Objectives → Goals: the vision answers "where do we want to be?" and the mission answers "what business are we in?", while intent turns them into a compelling stretch target.

Key points to remember: core values are the 7S centre and guide behaviour (Tata, Infosys); strategic intent is a stretch ambition (Komatsu, Canon, Infosys); hierarchy - vision, mission, objectives, goals.

Takeaway: Values answer "who are we?"; strategic intent answers "how far will we stretch?".

Q3. Explain various modes of entry in global markets with examples.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the entry modes with examples, 5 marks for motives and adaptation.

Answer: Firms enter global markets along a ladder from exporting to FDI, choosing the mode that matches commitment, risk and control.

Firms enter global markets along a ladder of increasing commitment, risk and control:

  • Exporting: selling goods made at home into foreign markets. Lowest risk. Example: Indian textile exporters selling to the EU.
  • Licensing: a licensor allows a foreign firm to use its technology or brand for a fee. Example: Indian pharma licensing drug formulations abroad.
  • Franchising: the franchisor grants a complete business format - brand, processes, support. Example: McDonald's uses franchising worldwide, adapting locally (McAloo Tikki in India, Teriyaki Burger in Japan) - a polycentric approach.
  • Joint venture: two firms create a new entity sharing ownership and risk. Example: Maruti-Suzuki JV created India's largest car maker.
  • Strategic alliance: cooperation without ownership - sharing technology, markets or research. Example: Star Alliance in airlines.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): highest commitment - wholly owned plants or subsidiaries. Example: Hyundai's manufacturing plant in India.

Motives include market expansion, scale economies, location advantages, extending the product life cycle and low-cost labour. The EPRG framework (Perlmutter) describes the orientation behind these choices - ethnocentric (home-first), polycentric (adapt to each host), regiocentric or geocentric (global). Companies climb the ladder as they learn a market and grow more confident.

Key points to remember: entry modes - exporting, licensing, franchising, JV, alliance, FDI (low to high commitment); McDonald's franchise + local adaptation; Maruti-Suzuki JV; EPRG orientations shape the choice.

Takeaway: Climb the entry-mode ladder as confidence and knowledge of the market grow.

Q4. Briefly summarize, what you understand by the general environment and its relevance for business.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the seven dimensions and the I/O view, 5 marks for relevance with examples.

Answer: The general environment is the broad set of external forces (seven dimensions) that indirectly shape every organisation's opportunities and threats.

The general environment is the broad set of external forces that affect all organisations indirectly. It has seven dimensions - remember DEPSTG-GP: Demographic, Economic, Political/Legal, Sociocultural, Technological, Global and Physical.

  • Demographic: population, age, urbanisation. India's young population (65% under 35) is an opportunity for consumer goods, education and tech.
  • Economic: GDP growth, inflation, interest rates. India's 6-7% growth and rising middle class expand markets.
  • Political/Legal: policies, regulations, taxation - Make in India, PLI schemes, GST.
  • Sociocultural: values and lifestyles - health consciousness, digital adoption, premiumisation.
  • Technological: UPI payments, AI, 5G and cloud computing are reshaping every industry.
  • Global and Physical: trade flows, climate, water and energy resources.

Relevance: the I/O model says the external environment determines strategy more than internal resources - industry attractiveness matters most. Firms scan these dimensions (environmental scanning) and use the EFE matrix to weight opportunities and threats; a total above 2.5 means a strong external position. COVID-19 was an external shock that forced rapid restructuring, and the pandemic's acceleration of e-commerce shows how environmental change creates winners and losers.

Key points to remember: general environment = 7 dimensions (DEPSTG-GP); scanning + EFE matrix convert it into strategy; I/O view - environment drives strategy; India examples - young population, UPI, Make in India.

Takeaway: Scan the environment continuously - external change makes winners and losers.

Q5. Explain the concept of 'Resource Based View' of strategic management. Further, discuss the relationship among resources, capabilities and competitive advantage.

Marks guide: 10 marks for RBV and the VRIN test, 5 marks for resources-capabilities-advantage logic.

Answer: RBV is the inside-out view - competitive advantage comes from VRIN resources and capabilities, not just industry attractiveness.

The Resource Based View (RBV) is the inside-out perspective: competitive advantage comes from a firm's internal resources and capabilities, not just from industry attractiveness. Where the I/O model says "choose an attractive industry", RBV says "build resources competitors cannot copy".

  • Resources: tangible (plants, cash, equipment), intangible (brand, patents, culture) and human capabilities (skills to use assets).
  • Capabilities: what the firm can do by combining resources - e.g., Honda's engine technology, Apple's design and ecosystem integration.
  • Core competencies (Prahalad & Hamel): the unique strengths that provide sustainable advantage - the glue of the organisation.
  • VRIN test (Barney): a resource yields sustained advantage only if it is Valuable, Rare, Inimitable and Non-substitutable. Tata Group's brand trust passes all four - valuable, rare, impossible to replicate in 150 years of history, and with no substitute.

The relationship: resources are the raw inputs, capabilities are what the firm does with them, and competitive advantage follows when those capabilities rest on VRIN resources. Dynamic capabilities - sensing, seizing and transforming (Teece) - let firms reconfigure resources as the environment changes. Value chain analysis complements RBV by showing where value and cost are created activity by activity.

Key points to remember: RBV = inside-out, resources drive advantage; VRIN = Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Non-substitutable; resources → capabilities → core competencies → sustainable advantage; Tata trust as the classic example.

Takeaway: Build VRIN resources - advantage that rivals can neither copy nor substitute.

Q6. What is the role of cost in business growth? Explain Porter's 'cost leadership' with respect to generic strategies.

Marks guide: 10 marks for cost leadership - sources, prerequisites and risks, 5 marks for the generic strategy framework.

Answer: Cost leadership makes the firm the lowest-cost producer with acceptable quality, letting it price below rivals and fund growth.

Cost is central to growth: the lowest-cost producer can price below rivals while keeping margins, win price-sensitive customers, survive price wars and reinvest savings into expansion. That is the logic of cost leadership, one of Porter's three generic strategies (with differentiation and focus).

  • Cost leadership: become the lowest-cost producer in the industry while maintaining acceptable quality. Prerequisites: efficient scale, experience-curve gains, tight cost control and avoiding marginal accounts. Sources: economies of scale, learning effects, technology and supply-chain optimisation.
  • Risks: technology changes can make cost advantages obsolete; competitors imitate cost-cutting methods; obsession with cost may ignore quality and service.
  • Indian examples: IndiGo (fleet standardisation and operational efficiency), Jio (data at the lowest price through massive scale) and Maruti Suzuki (economies of scale in small cars).

In the generic strategies matrix, cost leadership covers the broad market at low cost (Walmart, Jio); differentiation serves the broad market with uniqueness (Apple, BMW); cost focus and differentiation focus serve narrow niches (dollar stores, Rolex). A firm that achieves none - neither low cost nor differentiation - is "stuck in the middle" and performs poorly, as pre-Tata Air India showed: high costs with average service.

Key points to remember: cost leadership = lowest-cost producer with acceptable quality; sources - scale, learning curve, supply chain; examples - IndiGo, Jio, Maruti; generic strategies - cost leadership, differentiation, focus; stuck in the middle = failure.

Takeaway: Be the cheapest, be unique, or own a niche - never sit "stuck in the middle".

Q7. What is competitive strategy? Discuss dimension for formulation of competitive strategy with illustrations.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the concept and dimensions, 5 marks for illustrations.

Answer: Competitive strategy is how a firm wins customers against rivals in its chosen business - decided through competitor analysis along cost, quality, flexibility, delivery and service.

Competitive strategy is how a firm competes in its chosen business to win customers against rivals - Porter describes it as the goals (ends) plus the policies (means) for competing in a specific industry. It is formulated at the business (SBU) level, while corporate strategy decides which businesses to be in.

  • Cost: competing on price through operational efficiency - IndiGo keeps costs low with a single aircraft type.
  • Quality: reliability and performance - Maruti's reputation for dependable, low-maintenance cars.
  • Flexibility: quick adaptation and customisation - Zara-style fast fashion reaching Indian stores in weeks.
  • Delivery: speed and reliability - Amazon India's next-day delivery promise.
  • Service: after-sales support - Tata Motors' wide service network supporting commercial vehicle buyers.

Formulation uses the competitor analysis framework - future objectives, current strategy, assumptions, capabilities and response profile of rivals - and the wheel of competitive strategy, which aligns every functional area (finance, production, marketing, HR) with the chosen strategy. Industry context matters: in a fragmented industry (restaurants) firms consolidate or specialise; in an emerging industry (EVs) they seek first-mover advantage; in a mature industry they defend share; in a declining industry they lead, niche, harvest or divest.

Porter's five forces determine industry attractiveness (Jio's 2016 entry reshaped all five forces in telecom).

Key points to remember: competitive strategy = how to compete (ends + policies); five dimensions - cost, quality, flexibility, delivery, service; analyse rivals (future, current, assumptions, capabilities, response); match strategy to industry type.

Takeaway: Profile rivals and industry forces first - only then choose how to compete.

Q8. What is diversification strategy? Explain the alternative routes to diversification.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the concept and routes, 5 marks for examples and the Ansoff matrix.

Answer: Diversification is entering new businesses beyond current products and markets - the riskiest Ansoff cell - pursued through internal development, M&A, JVs and alliances.

Diversification is a corporate-level growth strategy: entering new businesses beyond the firm's current products and markets. It answers the corporate question "what businesses should we be in?" and is chosen when the current business has limited growth or when new businesses offer better returns or risk-spreading.

  • Related diversification: entering businesses that share technology, marketing or distribution with existing ones - Tata Motors' EVs share the Tata engineering and brand platform.
  • Unrelated diversification (conglomerate): entering businesses with no common thread - Tata Group spans steel, software, autos and hotels; Reliance spans petrochemicals, telecom (Jio) and retail.

Routes to diversification:

  • Internal development: building the new business from scratch - Jio was built internally by Reliance.
  • Mergers and acquisitions: buying existing firms - horizontal (same industry: Vodafone + Idea), vertical (different value-chain stages: Reliance acquiring content platforms for Jio) and conglomerate (unrelated: Tata buying Tetley and Corus).
  • Joint ventures and alliances: sharing the new venture with partners - Maruti-Suzuki.
  • Vertical integration is the narrowest form of expansion - forward (toward the customer, like Apple Stores) or backward (toward raw materials, like Tata Steel's coal mines).

The Ansoff matrix places diversification as the most risky option - new products in new markets - against safer choices like market penetration, product development and market development. The BCG matrix then helps manage the diversified portfolio: stars, cash cows, question marks and dogs.

After diversifying, the BCG matrix decides funding: cash cows feed stars; question marks get selective bets; dogs exit.

Key points to remember: diversification = new products + new markets (Ansoff's riskiest cell); related vs unrelated; routes - internal, M&A, JVs, alliances; vertical integration is forward/backward expansion; BCG manages the portfolio.

Takeaway: Diversify with a logic (related or unrelated), then run the BCG portfolio actively.

June 2022 - Term-End Paper (8 questions)
Note: The June 2022 MMPC-012 PDF is not present in the StudyHub collection (the 2022-June folder has no MMPC-012 paper). The questions below are reconstructed from the standard IGNOU MMPC-012 pattern of that period and the syllabus - use them as practice; the wording is not verbatim.

Q1Explain the concept of strategy and describe its main features with examples.

Answer: Strategy is a long-term, organisation-wide, resource-based design to reach objectives, marked by uniqueness, flexibility and environmental fit.

Key points:

  • Long-term orientation and organisation-wide scope; allocates resources toward objectives.
  • Action-oriented and flexible, unlike fixed policy; matches external environment with internal capability.
  • Deliberate vs emergent: Apple's planned iPhone launches vs Amazon's evolution from books to everything.
  • Mintzberg's 5 Ps: Pattern, Plan, Position, Perspective, Ploy (PaPPeP).
  • Porter: strategy is the creation of a unique and valued position.

Takeaway: Strategy = long-term unique direction, flexible and environment-fit.

Q2Explain the strategic management process with the help of a diagram.

Answer: The strategic management process runs from strategic intent through analysis and formulation to implementation, evaluation and control - as a continuous loop.

Key points:

  • Strategic intent: vision, mission and long-term objectives set direction.
  • Environmental analysis: external (PESTLE, opportunities/threats) and internal (SWOT, VRIN).
  • Strategy formulation: business-level (generic) and corporate-level (growth/stability/retrenchment) strategies.
  • Implementation: structure, resource allocation, policies, programs and change management.
  • Evaluation and control: performance review, standards, corrective action (BSC, KPIs); feedback loops back.

The five stages of the strategic management process - evaluation feeds back into formulation.

Takeaway: Formulation is only the start - the loop must close through evaluation.

Q3What is the BCG matrix? Explain its four quadrants with examples.

Answer: The BCG matrix classifies a firm's businesses by market growth and market share into stars, question marks, cash cows and dogs to guide portfolio investment.

Key points:

  • Star (high growth, high share): market leader in a growing industry - invest heavily (Jio 2016-19, Reliance Retail).
  • Cash cow (low growth, high share): mature leader generating surplus cash - milk it to fund others (TCS, Maruti).
  • Question mark (high growth, low share): attractive industry, weak position - invest selectively or divest (Tata Motors' EV business).
  • Dog (low growth, low share): weak in a stagnant market - divest or liquidate (landline telephony).
  • Cash flows move: cows fund stars and question marks; the portfolio must stay balanced.

Portfolio logic: cash cows fund stars and question marks; dogs are divested.

Takeaway: Balance the portfolio - grow stars, milk cows, prune dogs.

Q4Explain Porter's five forces model of industry competition.

Answer: Five forces - new entrants, suppliers, buyers, substitutes and rivalry - determine industry attractiveness and profit potential.

Key points:

  • Threat of new entrants: high when entry barriers (scale, brand, capital, regulation) are low.
  • Bargaining power of suppliers: high when few suppliers, no substitutes, high switching costs.
  • Bargaining power of buyers: high when products are undifferentiated, buyers concentrated and price-sensitive.
  • Threat of substitutes: high when alternatives are attractively priced and easy to switch to.
  • Competitive rivalry: intense with many balanced rivals, slow growth and high exit barriers.
  • Strong forces = low industry profitability; Jio's 2016 entry changed all five forces in Indian telecom.

Use the five forces to judge industry attractiveness before entering or investing.

Takeaway: Five forces set the ceiling on industry profits - read them first.

Q5What do you understand by corporate governance? Discuss the basic models of corporate governance.

Answer: Corporate governance is the system by which organisations are directed and controlled - its basic models are Anglo-American, German, Japanese and Indian.

Key points:

  • Meaning: Cadbury (1992) - "the system by which organisations are directed and controlled"; pillars: Accountability, Fairness, Transparency, Integrity, Social Responsibility (AFTIS).
  • Anglo-American (UK/US): single board, dispersed shareholders, strong disclosure; SOX 2002 - CEO/CFO certification, audit committees, PCAOB.
  • German: two-tier board (management + supervisory), bank financing, employee co-determination.
  • Japanese: keiretsu cross-shareholding, long-term bank relationships, consensus management.
  • Indian: mixed model - Companies Act 2013, mandatory independent directors and audit committees (strengthened after Satyam 2009).

Takeaway: All models rest on the same pillars - accountability, fairness and transparency.

Real IGNOU paper, December 2022. Time: 3 hours, Maximum Marks: 100. Attempt any five questions; all carry equal marks.

Q1Explain the features of any company's vision. Discuss briefly the purpose of vision statement with the help of a diagram.

Answer: A vision is a brief, clear, inspiring picture of the desired future - it answers "where do we want to be?" and anchors the whole strategy hierarchy.

Key points:

  • Features of a good vision: brief, clear, inspiring, stable over time, future-oriented - Google's "organize the world's information"; Amazon's "most customer-centric company".
  • Purpose: gives direction, motivates people, guides resource allocation and decision-making, sets the benchmark for strategic intent.
  • Vision sits at the top of the hierarchy: Vision → Mission → Objectives → Goals.
  • Vision differs from mission: vision = future destination; mission = present purpose ("what business are we in?").

Vision → Mission → Objectives → Goals: the hierarchy of strategic intent.

Takeaway: Vision is the destination; everything else is the route map.

Q2Explain the meaning of global business environment. Why is there a need for internationalization?

Answer: The global business environment is the sum of economic, political, cultural and technological forces across countries that shape international operations - internationalisation lets firms exploit markets, scale and location advantages.

Key points:

  • Meaning: external forces in home and host countries plus international institutions (WTO, IMF) that affect cross-border business.
  • Market expansion: access new customers beyond the domestic market.
  • Scale economies: larger volumes reduce per-unit cost.
  • Location advantages and low-cost labour: raw materials, cheap wages, favourable regulations.
  • PLC extension: revive mature products in newer markets.
  • EPRG orientations (Perlmutter): ethnocentric, polycentric, regiocentric, geocentric; example - Indian IT services (TCS, Infosys) and pharma exports internationalise for scale and talent.

Takeaway: Go global when foreign markets, costs or resources beat home options.

Q3Briefly analyse Porter's five forces framework. Diagrammatically explain Porter's five forces model of competition.

Answer: The five forces - new entrants, suppliers, buyers, substitutes and rivalry - determine how attractive and profitable an industry is.

Key points:

  • Threat of new entrants: low entry barriers (scale, brand, capital, regulation) invite new rivals.
  • Supplier power: few suppliers, no substitutes and high switching costs give suppliers leverage.
  • Buyer power: concentrated, price-sensitive buyers with cost information press margins.
  • Threat of substitutes: attractively priced alternatives cap industry prices.
  • Rivalry: many balanced competitors, slow growth and high exit barriers intensify competition.
  • Application: strong forces = low profit potential; Jio's 2016 entry reshaped all five forces in telecom.

The standard diagram to reproduce in the exam - centre box plus four forces around it.

Takeaway: Draw the five forces, then judge the industry's profit ceiling.

Q4Describe value chain framework. How does it analyze organization's strengths and weaknesses?

Answer: The value chain splits the firm into primary and support activities that create value and cost - strengths and weaknesses surface activity by activity.

Key points:

  • Primary activities: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing & sales, service.
  • Support activities: firm infrastructure, HR management, technology development, procurement.
  • Each activity adds margin; value and cost are created at every link of the chain.
  • Strengths: activities done better than rivals become sources of competitive advantage (core competencies).
  • Weaknesses: high-cost or low-value activities are targets for improvement, outsourcing or divestment.
  • Example: IndiGo's operational excellence = lowest-cost value chain in Indian aviation.

Value chain maps strengths and weaknesses activity by activity.

Takeaway: Advantage lives in the details of the chain - audit every activity.

Q5Describe the formulation of competitive strategy. What are the factors to develop realistic competitive strategy?

Answer: Competitive strategy is formulated by profiling rivals and aligning functional policies with goals - realistically, it must fit the industry, rivals, customers, capability and environment.

Key points:

  • Competitive strategy = goals (ends) + policies (means) for competing in a specific industry (Porter).
  • Competitor analysis framework: future objectives, current strategy, assumptions, capabilities, response profile.
  • Wheel of competitive strategy: goals at the centre; finance, production, marketing and HR as spokes that must align.
  • Factors for a realistic strategy: industry structure, competitor moves, customer needs, the firm's own resources and environment trends.
  • Competitive dimensions to choose among: cost, quality, flexibility, delivery, service.
  • Match strategy to industry type: fragmented, emerging, mature or declining.

Takeaway: A realistic strategy reads industry, rivals, customers and capability before choosing.

Q6Examine the nature and scope of corporate strategies. Discuss the types of generic corporate strategies.

Answer: Corporate strategy decides which businesses the firm should be in and how resources flow between them - through growth, stability, retrenchment or combination strategies.

Key points:

  • Nature and scope: corporate-level decisions on portfolio, resource allocation across SBUs and synergy (BCG, Ansoff guide choices).
  • Growth: concentration, integration (horizontal/vertical), diversification, internationalisation.
  • Stability: pause, no-change, profit, harvesting - chosen when the environment is stable and performance is fine.
  • Retrenchment: turnaround, divestiture, liquidation - when performance declines.
  • Combination: growth + stability + retrenchment across different units (Reliance, Tata, Adani).

Reliance and Tata run combination strategies across their SBU portfolios.

Takeaway: Pick growth, hold steady, retrench the weak - or combine all three.

Q7Explain the basic models of corporate governance. Discuss the important provisions of different models.

Answer: The basic governance models - Anglo-American, German, Japanese and Indian - differ in board structure, ownership and stakeholder focus, each with distinctive provisions.

Key points:

  • Anglo-American (UK/US): single board, dispersed shareholders, market discipline; SOX 2002 provisions - CEO/CFO certification (s.302), internal-controls assessment (s.404), PCAOB oversight, criminal penalties for false reporting.
  • German: two-tier board (management board + supervisory board with employee co-determination), bank-dominated financing.
  • Japanese: keiretsu cross-shareholding, long-term bank relationships, consensus-based direction.
  • Indian: mixed model - Companies Act 2013: mandatory independent directors, audit committees, board evaluation, CSR spending, stronger minority protection (post-Satyam 2009).
  • Common thread: audit committees, transparency of reporting and protection of minority shareholders in every model.

Takeaway: One-tablet revision: Anglo-American = shareholder; German = two-tier; Japanese = cross-holding; India = mandatory independents.

Q8Describe balanced score card. How are critical success factors determined?

Answer: The balanced scorecard translates strategy into measures across four perspectives - financial, customer, internal processes and learning & growth - with CSFs as the few things the firm must get right.

Key points:

  • Four perspectives: Financial (profit, ROI), Customer (satisfaction, share), Internal Processes (quality, cycle time), Learning & Growth (skills, innovation).
  • Balances lagging indicators (financial results) with leading indicators (processes and learning).
  • CSFs: the handful of areas where superior performance determines success - determined from strategy and vision.
  • Determination: industry analysis (five forces), competitor benchmarking (CPM), customer needs and internal capability analysis.
  • Each CSF gets objectives, measures, targets and initiatives - e.g., airlines: on-time performance, turnaround time, yield per seat.
  • Benefits: strategy communication, alignment of departments and people, monitoring and corrective action.

The BSC links vision to measures in four perspectives.

Takeaway: Measure all four perspectives - money follows people, process and customers.

June 2023 - Term-End Paper (8 questions)

Real IGNOU paper, June 2023. Time: 3 hours, Maximum Marks: 100. Attempt any five questions; all carry equal marks.

Q1Describe the different features of strategy giving examples.

Answer: Strategy is long-term, organisation-wide, resource-based and environment-fit - flexible in action yet stable in direction, with examples from Apple, Amazon and Jio.

Key points:

  • Long-term orientation: looks years ahead, not quarterly (Amazon's decade-long ecosystem bets).
  • Organisation-wide scope: covers all functions and units, unlike tactics.
  • Resource allocation: deploys money, people and technology toward objectives.
  • Environmental fit: matches external opportunities/threats with internal strengths (I/O + RBV).
  • Deliberate vs emergent: Apple's planned iPhone launches vs Amazon's books-to-everything evolution.
  • Uniqueness and flexibility: Porter's "unique position"; Jio's free-data ploy as a deliberate competitive move.

Takeaway: Features to write: long-term, broad, resource-based, flexible, unique, environment-fit.

Q2What are the different traits of an effective objective? Explain each of them giving examples.

Answer: Effective objectives are SMART - specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound - plus aligned to the mission.

Key points:

  • Specific: clear and precise - "raise market share to 25%" rather than "grow".
  • Measurable: quantified - profit %, units sold, satisfaction score.
  • Achievable: challenging but within reach of resources and capability.
  • Realistic: consistent with the mission and environment (a small carmaker targeting 50% share next year is not realistic).
  • Time-bound: has a deadline - "by March 2026" - enabling review and accountability.
  • Derived from mission and cascaded to departments and individuals (Maruti's per-model targets).

Takeaway: No SMART traits = a wish, not an objective.

Q3How can one analyze the external environment? Describe the process of analyzing the external environment.

Answer: External environment analysis scans and interprets outside forces through four steps - identify factors, select key variables, analyse them and position strategically.

Key points:

  • Step 1: identify environmental factors - PESTLE and the seven general-environment dimensions (DEPSTG-GP).
  • Step 2: select the key variables that actually affect the firm (industry-specific shortlist).
  • Step 3: analyse variables - impact and likelihood, trends, forecasts and scenarios.
  • Step 4: strategic positioning - convert findings into strategy via the EFE matrix (weighted opportunities/threats; score above 2.5 = strong external position).
  • Tools: environmental scanning, PESTLE, Porter's five forces, EFE matrix.

Identify → select → analyse → position: the analysis process in four boxes.

Takeaway: Analyse the outside first - strategy follows the environment.

Q4Explain the concept of scenario planning giving examples.

Answer: Scenario planning builds several plausible futures and stress-tests strategy against each, so the firm is ready for different worlds instead of one forecast.

Key points:

  • Meaning: a systematic method to think about alternative futures under uncertainty.
  • Process: identify key uncertainties → build 2-4 plausible scenarios → test current strategy in each → prepare flexible responses and early-warning signals.
  • Not prediction: scenarios are "what if" worlds - optimistic, pessimistic and base case.
  • Examples: oil majors plan for crude at $40 vs $120; auto firms plan fast and slow EV adoption; firms prepared both remote-work and office worlds after COVID.
  • Benefits: strategic flexibility, resilience, better contingency decisions.

Scenario planning: prepare for several worlds, watch early-warning signals.

Takeaway: Under uncertainty, plan for several futures - hedge and stay flexible.

Q5Write short notes on the following: (a) Critical success factors (b) Cost leadership

Answer: CSFs are the few things a business must get right to succeed; cost leadership makes the firm the lowest-cost producer with acceptable quality.

Key points:

  • (a) CSFs: the handful of areas where superior performance determines success - on-time performance for airlines, network coverage for telecom.
  • CSFs are derived from industry analysis, competitor benchmarks, customer needs and the firm's strategy; they feed the Competitive Profile Matrix (CPM).
  • (b) Cost leadership: lowest-cost producer at acceptable quality; sources - economies of scale, learning curve, technology, supply-chain optimisation.
  • Prerequisites: efficient scale, tight cost control, avoiding marginal accounts; risks - technology shifts, imitation, ignoring quality.
  • Examples: IndiGo (fleet standardisation), Jio (massive scale), Maruti Suzuki (economies of scale).

Generic strategies: cost leadership vs differentiation, broad vs narrow scope.

Takeaway: CSFs tell you what to win on; cost leadership is one winning formula.

Q6Explain the concept of differentiation strategy and its different types. Support your answer with suitable examples.

Answer: Differentiation creates unique, valued products that justify premium prices - through features, brand, service, design or technology.

Key points:

  • Meaning: uniqueness that customers value → price premium, loyalty and margins.
  • Types/sources: product features (Dyson), technology innovation (Apple), brand image (BMW), customer service (Tata), distribution (Nykaa), design (IKEA).
  • Benefits: reduced price sensitivity, higher margins, competitive barriers.
  • Risks: premium too high, basis of differentiation loses importance, imitation by rivals.
  • Examples: Apple's ecosystem, Amul's trust, Titan's design, Maruti's low-maintenance reliability.

Takeaway: Differentiate on something customers value and rivals cannot copy cheaply.

Q7How can 'Harvest Strategy' become a competitive strategy for declining industries? Explain.

Answer: Harvest strategy turns a declining business into a cash generator - cut costs, raise prices and stop reinvestment - which keeps it profitable when industry demand is shrinking.

Key points:

  • Declining industries: demand falling, excess capacity, weak profitability (landline telecom, DVD rental).
  • Harvest: maximise short-term cash flow; cut costs, raise prices, stop reinvestment, accept market-share loss.
  • Why competitive: avoids a costly market-share war in a shrinking pie; converts the business into a cash cow.
  • Conditions: decline is certain and remaining demand is price-inelastic or loyal.
  • Alternatives in declining industries: leadership, niche, harvest or divest - harvest suits firms whose strengths lie elsewhere.
  • Example: firms harvesting legacy products (Nokia exiting legacy handsets to fund network equipment).

Harvest is one of the four classic responses to a declining industry.

Takeaway: When the industry shrinks, harvest the cash - and reinvest it elsewhere.

Q8Describe the Katz Model - Skills of an effective manager with respect to corporate culture.

Answer: Katz's model says managers need technical, human and conceptual skills - in proportions that shift with management level, and which shape corporate culture at every level.

Key points:

  • Technical skills: job-specific knowledge and methods - most important at lower management.
  • Human skills: working with, motivating and communicating with people - critical at every level.
  • Conceptual skills: seeing the organisation as a whole and thinking strategically - most important at top management.
  • The mix shifts as managers rise: technical declines, conceptual grows, human stays constant.
  • Corporate culture link: top managers shape culture through conceptual vision; middle managers transmit it; lower managers model technical standards and daily values.

Katz pyramid: conceptual at the top, technical at the base.

Takeaway: Skills follow the level - conceptual for leaders, human for all, technical for executors.

Q1What are the different stages of strategy formulation? Explain each of them in brief.

Answer: Strategy formulation moves through strategic intent, environmental analysis, strategy development, and evaluation and choice - before implementation takes over.

Key points:

  • Stage 1 - Strategic intent: vision, mission and long-term objectives set the direction.
  • Stage 2 - Environmental analysis: external (PESTLE, five forces, opportunities/threats) and internal (SWOT, VRIN) scanning.
  • Stage 3 - Strategy development: business-level generic strategies and corporate-level options (growth/stability/retrenchment).
  • Stage 4 - Evaluation and choice: select the best-fit strategy using EFE/IFE matrices, portfolio tools (BCG, Ansoff) and feasibility checks.
  • Formulation then feeds implementation - structure, resources, programs - and evaluation feeds back (the process is iterative).

Four stages of formulation - the front half of the strategic management process.

Takeaway: Formulate in order: intent, analysis, development, choice.

Q2(a) Elaborate on the global business environment. (b) How do the organization do environmental analysis in the global context? Discuss.

Answer: The global business environment is the web of international economic, political, cultural, technological and legal forces; in the global context, analysis means scanning many environments and comparing them market by market.

Key points:

  • (a) Elaborate: economic forces (exchange rates, trade pacts, global growth), political/legal (tariffs, sanctions, WTO rules), sociocultural (values, language - McDonald's localisation), technological (platforms, digital rails), plus global institutions (WTO, IMF, World Bank).
  • (b) Global analysis: run PESTLE per country and compare across markets.
  • Use the EPRG orientation to decide adaptation vs standardisation (polycentric = adapt locally).
  • Monitor global trends - China+1 supply chains, UPI cross-border payments, geopolitical risk.
  • Weight opportunities and threats per market (country-level EFE matrix) before entering.

Takeaway: Global strategy = read many environments, then adapt or standardise deliberately.

Q3Write short notes on the following: (a) Strategic groups (b) Resource Based View

Answer: Strategic groups are clusters of firms following similar strategies in an industry; RBV says VRIN internal resources, not just industry position, drive competitive advantage.

Key points:

  • (a) Strategic groups: firms in the same group compete more intensely with each other; groups are mapped on two strategic dimensions (price vs quality) and separated by mobility barriers.
  • Use: identify true direct rivals, spot empty positions, understand industry structure.
  • (b) RBV: inside-out view - resources and capabilities create advantage; VRIN test - Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Non-substitutable.
  • Logic: resources → capabilities → core competencies → sustainable advantage.
  • Example: Tata's 150-year brand trust; complement to the I/O outside-in view.

A strategic group map reveals who the firm's real competitors are.

Takeaway: Find your group, know your true rivals, spot the empty gaps.

Q4Describe the concept of value chain framework.

Answer: The value chain framework decomposes a firm into primary and support activities that together create value and margin.

Key points:

  • Primary activities: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing & sales, service.
  • Support activities: firm infrastructure, HR management, technology development, procurement.
  • Margin = customer value created minus the cost of all activities.
  • Purpose: locate cost drivers, differentiation sources and strengths/weaknesses.
  • Example: Maruti's supply chain and operations excellence across the whole chain.

The classic value chain - five primary, four support activities, margin.

Takeaway: Break the firm into activities - each one adds cost or value.

Q5What are the factors determining the formulation of competitive strategy? Explain them in brief.

Answer: Competitive strategy is determined by industry structure, competitor behaviour, customer needs, the firm's own capabilities and the wider environment.

Key points:

  • Industry structure: five forces and stage of evolution (fragmented, emerging, mature, declining).
  • Competitor analysis: future objectives, current strategy, assumptions, capabilities and likely responses.
  • Customer needs: which value dimensions matter - cost, quality, flexibility, delivery, service.
  • Firm capabilities: internal strengths and resources (VRIN, value chain analysis).
  • Environment: macro trends, technology shifts and regulation.
  • Consistency requirement: all functional policies must align with the chosen strategy (wheel of competitive strategy).

Takeaway: Five inputs decide how to compete - industry, rivals, customers, capability, environment.

Q6Describe the elements of structural environment in Emerging Industries.

Answer: Emerging industries - newly formed or reformed - have a structural environment marked by technological and strategic uncertainty, high initial costs and unformed rules.

Key points:

  • Technological uncertainty: competing technologies and no settled standard yet.
  • Strategic uncertainty: no proven winning strategy; firms experiment.
  • High initial costs but steep cost declines with volume and learning.
  • Small entry barriers at first - but rising as scale and standards build.
  • First-time buyers, unestablished supplier/buyer relationships, and regulatory rules still evolving.
  • Strategies: first-mover advantage, technological leadership, shaping industry standards, building barriers, educating buyers (Indian EV market as an example).

Takeaway: In emerging industries, shape the rules before rivals do.

Q7Why do the organization opt for expansion strategies? Discuss the Ansoff's Product Market Expansion Grid with respect to expansion strategies.

Answer: Firms expand for growth, scale economies and risk spreading; Ansoff's grid ranks the four expansion routes - penetration, product development, market development and diversification - by risk.

Key points:

  • Why expand: growth in sales and profit, scale economies, market power, risk spreading and survival.
  • Ansoff axes: products (existing/new) x markets (existing/new).
  • Market penetration: existing products in existing markets - lowest risk (Coca-Cola's ad push).
  • Product development: new products in existing markets (Apple Watch).
  • Market development: existing products in new markets (Starbucks entering India).
  • Diversification: new products + new markets - highest risk (Reliance's Jio).

Ansoff's grid: the expansion menu ranked by risk.

Takeaway: Stay close to home until you can afford the leap.

Q8How can the organization structure to be matched to strategy? Explain.

Answer: Structure follows strategy (Chandler): the organisation must adopt the structure - functional, divisional, matrix or network - that fits its strategic needs.

Key points:

  • Chandler's principle: strategy change must be followed by structure change.
  • Functional structure: suits single-product firms and stable strategies - specialisation and efficiency.
  • Divisional structure: suits diversified firms - accountability per product/region (Reliance's SBUs).
  • Matrix structure: dual reporting for complex projects (consulting, engineering).
  • Network structure: outsourced, agile organisations (tech platforms).
  • Mismatch symptoms: slow decisions, duplication, poor coordination - review structure as strategy evolves.

Strategy sets the direction; structure makes it executable.

Takeaway: When strategy changes, structure must follow - or execution stalls.

June 2024 - Term-End Paper (8 questions)

Real IGNOU paper, June 2024. Time: 3 hours, Maximum Marks: 100. Attempt any five questions; all carry equal marks.

Q1"Strategy formulation, implementation, evaluation and control are integrated." Elaborate on the statement.

Answer: The four stages of strategic management form one continuous loop - each feeds and reshapes the others.

Key points:

  • Formulation sets direction; implementation executes it through structure, resources and programs.
  • Evaluation measures performance against standards; control takes corrective action.
  • Integration: feedback from evaluation reshapes formulation - the loop never closes.
  • Change in one stage forces adjustment in others (a new strategy demands a new structure and new controls).
  • Example: Jio's 2016 launch - formulation (free data), implementation (massive network build), evaluation (market share), control (pricing revisions).
  • The balanced scorecard operationalises the link between all four stages.

The four stages are integrated - feedback closes the loop.

Takeaway: Strategy is a cycle, not a one-time plan - close the loop.

Q2What are the different factors which are analysed under PESTLE? Explain any two in detail.

Answer: PESTLE analyses six external factor groups - Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal and Environmental - for opportunities and threats.

Key points:

  • Political: government policy, stability, taxation, trade rules - Make in India, PLI schemes.
  • Economic: GDP growth, inflation, interest and exchange rates - India's 6-7% growth and rising middle class.
  • Social: demographics, culture, lifestyles - young population, health consciousness, digital adoption.
  • Technological: innovation, automation, digital infrastructure - UPI, AI, 5G, cloud.
  • Legal: laws and regulations - GST, labour codes, data protection rules.
  • Environmental: climate, natural resources, sustainability regulations - renewables, water scarcity.

PESTLE: six macro forces feeding the external analysis.

Takeaway: Name all six, then go deep on any two in the exam.

Q3How does Porter's Five Forces Framework help in evaluating a competitor's strategy? Explain.

Answer: The five forces reveal where competition presses hardest, exposing a rival's pressure points, profit exposure and likely moves.

Key points:

  • Rivalry: how intense the competitor's fight is - price vs differentiation battle.
  • New entrants: the barriers a competitor must cross to expand - feasibility of its growth plans.
  • Supplier power: the rival's input-cost exposure and dependence on suppliers.
  • Buyer power: pressure on the competitor's prices, margins and negotiating position.
  • Substitutes: alternatives that cap the whole industry's prices - including the rival's.
  • Application: assess each force's effect on the rival, predict its responses, and attack where pressure is highest.

Use the five forces to read rivals: their costs, their customers, their threats.

Takeaway: Find where pressure hurts the rival most - that is your opening.

Q4Explain the competitive strategy of Fragmented Industries.

Answer: Fragmented industries - many small players, no dominant firm - are handled through consolidation, specialisation, value creation and managed fragmentation.

Key points:

  • Characteristics: many small firms, low entry barriers, localised demand, diseconomies of scale (restaurants, local retail, accounting firms).
  • Consolidation: merge or acquire to build scale and market power (hotel chains absorbing independent properties).
  • Specialisation: focus on a niche segment and serve it better than anyone (boutique clinics, premium dining).
  • Value creation/branding: standardise the fragmented offer - franchise models (McDonald's, Apollo Clinics).
  • Managed fragmentation: keep local units but centralise buying, marketing and systems.
  • Example: Indian restaurants - franchise chains standardising, Zomato/Swiggy consolidating demand.

Takeaway: In fragmented industries - scale up, specialise or standardise.

Q5Describe expansion through integration. Illustrate your answer with the help of example.

Answer: Integration expands the firm within its industry - horizontally at the same stage, or vertically forward toward customers and backward toward suppliers.

Key points:

  • Horizontal integration: acquiring same-stage competitors - Vodafone + Idea merger.
  • Backward integration: moving toward raw materials/suppliers - Tata Steel's coal mines, Reliance's content acquisitions for Jio.
  • Forward integration: moving toward distributors/customers - Apple Stores, Tata Cliq.
  • Benefits: cost control, quality assurance, supply security, market power, coordination.
  • Risks: high capital needs, reduced flexibility, bureaucratic costs, regulatory scrutiny.
  • Example: Amul's cooperative owns the chain from farmers to consumers.

Three directions of integration around the firm.

Takeaway: Integrate to own the chain - but only where control beats flexibility.

Q6Write short notes on the following: (a) Resource Based View (b) The Value Chain Framework

Answer: RBV says VRIN internal resources drive competitive advantage; the value chain shows where those advantages live in the firm's activities.

Key points:

  • (a) RBV: inside-out perspective; resources (tangible, intangible, human) → capabilities → core competencies → sustainable advantage.
  • VRIN test: Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Non-substitutable (Tata's brand trust); dynamic capabilities - sensing, seizing, transforming (Teece).
  • (b) Value chain: primary activities - inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing & sales, service.
  • Support activities: firm infrastructure, HR, technology development, procurement; margin = value minus cost.
  • Each activity can be a strength (source of advantage) or a weakness (cost target, outsourcing candidate).
  • The two tools combine: RBV says what to build, the value chain says where.

Value chain + RBV: activity map and resource logic together.

Takeaway: RBV tells you what to build; the value chain tells you where.

Q7What are the different models of Corporate Governance? Explain any two.

Answer: The main governance models are Anglo-American, German, Japanese and Indian - differing in board structure, ownership and stakeholder emphasis.

Key points:

  • Anglo-American (UK/US): single board, dispersed shareholders, strong disclosure and market discipline; SOX 2002 - CEO/CFO certification, audit committees, PCAOB.
  • German: two-tier board (management board + supervisory board), bank financing, employee co-determination - stakeholder orientation.
  • Japanese: keiretsu cross-shareholding, long-term bank relationships, consensus management.
  • Indian: hybrid model - Companies Act 2013: mandatory independent directors, audit committees, board evaluation, minority protection (strengthened after Satyam 2009).
  • Common provisions across models: board oversight, audit committees, transparency, minority shareholder protection.

Takeaway: Explain any two fully - board structure and provisions - for full marks.

Q8Differentiate between Hofstede's Cultural Dimension Model and Hall's Cultural Model.

Answer: Hofstede measures national cultures on scored value dimensions; Hall contrasts communication cultures - high-context vs low-context, and time orientation.

Key points:

  • Hofstede: Power Distance, Individualism vs Collectivism, Masculinity vs Femininity, Uncertainty Avoidance, Long-term Orientation (plus Indulgence) - survey-based national scores.
  • Hall: high-context cultures (Japan, India) - implicit messages, relationships, non-verbal cues; low-context (US, Germany) - explicit, direct, written communication.
  • Hall's time orientation: monochronic (one thing at a time, punctuality - Germany) vs polychronic (many things at once, flexible time - India, Latin America).
  • Use: cross-cultural management, negotiation, global marketing and staffing.
  • India: high power distance, collectivist, long-term oriented, high-context and polychronic.
  • Difference in a line: Hofstede scores values; Hall explains how people communicate and use time.

Takeaway: Hofstede = value scores; Hall = communication and time styles.

Q1Describe the role of strategic management in achieving organizational goals. Explain with the help of examples.

Answer: Strategic management aligns direction, resources and actions with the environment - turning goals into coordinated, achievable outcomes.

Key points:

  • Provides direction: vision, mission and objectives focus the whole organisation.
  • Guides resource allocation: money, people and technology go where strategy says.
  • Improves performance: proactive, environment-fit strategies outperform ad hoc moves.
  • Creates competitive advantage: deliberate positioning (generic strategies, VRIN resources).
  • Coordinates units: SBUs aligned to corporate goals (Reliance: Jio, Retail, Petrochemicals).
  • Examples: Maruti's small-car strategy met India's market need; Amazon's customer-centricity compounds goals.

The process that converts intent into achieved goals.

Takeaway: Strategic management turns intent into coordinated action.

Q2Evaluate the contribution of environmental analysis in strategic decision-making. Discuss.

Answer: Environmental analysis supplies the information - opportunities and threats - on which every sound strategic decision is built.

Key points:

  • Identifies opportunities to exploit and threats to counter (external side of SWOT).
  • Reduces uncertainty: scanning turns an unknown environment into manageable data (EFE matrix).
  • Informs entry and exit decisions: which markets and industries to enter (five forces, PESTLE).
  • Enables timing: first-mover windows (EV adoption, UPI payments).
  • Provides early warning: shifts in regulation, technology or demand.
  • Examples: Jio read the data-democratisation opportunity; Kodak missed the digital threat.

Takeaway: Decisions are only as good as the environment reading behind them.

Q3How is a strategic plan formulated? Explain with the help of examples.

Answer: A strategic plan is built by setting intent, analysing the environment, choosing strategies and translating them into budgets, programs and controls.

Key points:

  • Step 1: vision, mission and objectives (strategic intent).
  • Step 2: external and internal analysis - PESTLE, five forces, SWOT, VRIN.
  • Step 3: generate and evaluate strategic options - generic and corporate strategies.
  • Step 4: choose the strategy and set long-range targets.
  • Step 5: convert into action - budgets, policies, programs, milestones, controls.
  • Example: Maruti's EV roadmap - intent (electrification), analysis (policy + market), choice (e-Vitara), budgets and rollout plan.

Takeaway: A plan is intent + analysis + choice + budget - in that order.

Q4Does Strategic Management differ in various sectors? Elucidate with respect to Automobile and Pharmaceutical Sectors.

Answer: Yes - the same frameworks adapt to each sector's environment, cost structure and regulation, as the automobile and pharmaceutical sectors show.

Key points:

  • Automobile: capital-intensive, long product cycles, brand and scale competition, cyclical demand, government EV push.
  • Pharmaceutical: R&D-heavy, patent-driven, strict regulation (CDSCO/DCGI, USFDA), long approval timelines, knowledge-intensive.
  • Strategy focus differs: autos - cost leadership/differentiation on platforms; pharma - R&D pipeline, patents, licensing, global registrations.
  • Examples: Maruti (scale and cost) vs Dr Reddy's/Sun Pharma (molecules and patents); Hyundai EV plants vs Indian pharma's generics and vaccine exports.
  • Implication: generic frameworks apply everywhere, but sector context decides which strategy wins.

Takeaway: Frameworks are universal; sector context decides the winning strategy.

Q5What are the challenges that an organization faces while implementing strategic plans? Discuss.

Answer: Implementation fails mainly from resource gaps, structural misfit, resistance to change, weak communication and poor control.

Key points:

  • Resource constraints: insufficient finance, talent or technology for the plan.
  • Structural mismatch: organisation structure not aligned with strategy (Chandler).
  • Resistance to change: fear, loss of status, broken routines - overcome via communication, participation, education (Kotter's 8 steps).
  • Leadership and communication gaps: vision not cascaded to units and people.
  • Weak evaluation and control: no KPIs or feedback loop - strategy drift.
  • External surprises: environment changes faster than implementation (COVID-19).

Takeaway: Execution beats formulation - budget, structure, people and controls decide outcomes.

Q6Write short notes on the following: (a) Stability Strategy (b) Turnaround Strategy

Answer: Stability strategy holds the current course when the environment is stable; turnaround aggressively cuts costs and rebuilds revenue to reverse a decline.

Key points:

  • (a) Stability: pause/proceed with caution (consolidate before growth), no-change (continue as-is), profit (maximise cash from the existing position), harvesting (maximise cash flow, accept share loss).
  • Stability suits successful firms in stable environments needing consolidation.
  • (b) Turnaround: retrenchment for a declining firm - diagnose the decline, cut costs aggressively, improve revenue, restructure assets.
  • Stages: diagnosis → cost reduction → revenue focus → restructuring (leadership change if needed).
  • Example: Tata's Air India turnaround; Kingfisher's collapse shows what happens without one.

Two sides of the corporate strategy coin.

Takeaway: Hold steady when stable; act fast when declining.

Q7What do you understand by Balanced Score Card (BSC)? Explain as to how it is helpful to the organizations.

Answer: The balanced scorecard converts strategy into measures across four perspectives - financial, customer, internal processes and learning & growth - aligning the whole organisation on execution.

Key points:

  • Four perspectives: Financial (profit, ROI), Customer (satisfaction, share), Internal Processes (quality, cycle time), Learning & Growth (skills, innovation).
  • Translates vision into objectives, measures, targets and initiatives per perspective.
  • Balances lagging indicators (financial results) with leading indicators (processes, learning).
  • Helpful because it: communicates strategy, aligns departments and people, monitors execution and drives continuous improvement.
  • Example: airlines - financial (yield per seat), customer (on-time), process (turnaround time), learning (pilot training).

The BSC links vision to measures in four perspectives.

Takeaway: Score all four perspectives - money follows people, process and customers.

Q8Describe the concept of scenario planning with the help of examples.

Answer: Scenario planning builds several plausible futures and stress-tests strategy against each, building preparedness instead of betting on one forecast.

Key points:

  • Meaning: alternative "what if" futures - optimistic, pessimistic and base case.
  • Process: identify key uncertainties → build 2-4 scenarios → test current strategy in each → prepare flexible responses with early-warning signals.
  • Not prediction: it is planning for different worlds.
  • Examples: oil majors plan for crude at $40 vs $120; auto firms plan fast and slow EV adoption; telecom planned 3G/4G/5G paths.
  • Benefits: strategic flexibility, resilience and better contingency decisions under uncertainty.

Scenario planning: prepare for several worlds, watch early-warning signals.

Takeaway: Under uncertainty, plan for several worlds - hedge and stay flexible.
