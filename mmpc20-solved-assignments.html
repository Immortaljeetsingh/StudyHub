Q1. "Business ethics begins where the law ends." Explain the statement and the difference between ethics and law.

Answer: Business ethics is the prescribed code of conduct that guides businesses in dealing ethically with procedures, people and society. The statement "business ethics begins where the law ends" means that law fixes the common minimum acceptable standard, while ethics demands a higher standard of right and wrong. Law is codified, enforceable through legal penalties and narrow in scope, whereas ethics is not codified, is contextual and is enforced through social sanctions and reputation. Ethical behaviour therefore goes beyond compliance: a firm may be legally correct yet ethically questionable. Ethics is contextual, conditioned by the prevailing power structures in society. Globalisation adds complexity, as multinational corporates are sandwiched between state regulatory agencies and vigilant civil society, and supply chains spread ethical decisions across borders. The Volkswagen Dieselgate scandal illustrates this: the firm cheated emission tests rather than plainly violating the law, yet the deception was profoundly unethical. Sustainability pressures are likewise driving new ethical frameworks, reinforcing that responsible business must exceed the legal minimum.

Key points:

  • Business ethics is the prescribed code of conduct that guides businesses in dealing ethically with procedures, people and society.
  • The statement "business ethics begins where the law ends" means that law fixes the common minimum acceptable standard, while ethics demands a higher standard of right and wrong.
  • Sustainability pressures are likewise driving new ethical frameworks, reinforcing that responsible business must exceed the legal minimum.

Takeaway: Sustainability pressures are likewise driving new ethical frameworks, reinforcing that responsible business must exceed the legal minimum.

Q2. Discuss the major theories of business ethics with suitable examples.

Answer: Ethical theories fall into two schools: consequentialism (teleology), which judges actions by their outcomes, and non-consequentialism (deontology), which judges actions by their inherent rightness. Utilitarianism, associated with Bentham and Mill, seeks the greatest good for the greatest number and is typically applied through cost-benefit analysis; a pharmaceutical company pricing AIDS drugs affordably in Africa is a utilitarian choice. Deontology, associated with Kant, holds that actions must follow duty and the categorical imperative: act only according to rules you could will to be universal laws, regardless of consequences. Virtue ethics, from Aristotle, focuses on the character and moral virtues of the decision-maker rather than the act itself, making leadership by example its practical expression. Two further frameworks are relevant: Rawls's social contract theory, which derives justice as fairness from behind a "veil of ignorance", and Freeman's stakeholder theory, which requires balancing the interests of all stakeholders - employees, customers, suppliers, community and environment. The mnemonic DUTCH - Deontology, Utilitarianism, Teleology, Character (virtue) and Hypothetical (social contract) - helps recall these theories in assignment answers.

Key points:

  • Ethical theories fall into two schools: consequentialism (teleology), which judges actions by their outcomes, and non-consequentialism (deontology), which judges actions by their inherent rightness.
  • Virtue ethics, from Aristotle, focuses on the character and moral virtues of the decision-maker rather than the act itself, making leadership by example its practical expression.
  • Utilitarianism, associated with Bentham and Mill, seeks the greatest good for the greatest number and is typically applied through cost-benefit analysis; a pharmaceutical company pricing AIDS drugs affordably in Africa is a utilitarian choice.

Q3. Discuss the CSR provisions of Section 135 of the Companies Act 2013. Explain the "2-3-5 rule".

Answer: India became the first country to mandate CSR by law through Section 135 of the Companies Act 2013. A company is covered if it meets any one of three thresholds: net worth of Rs 500 crore or more, turnover of Rs 1,000 crore or more, or net profit of Rs 5 crore or more. Eligible companies must spend at least 2% of the average net profits of the preceding three years on CSR activities listed in Schedule VII, which includes education, healthcare, environment, gender equality, rural development and Swachh Bharat. A CSR committee of at least three directors, including one independent director, must formulate and monitor the policy. This is the "2-3-5 rule": 2% of average profits, a 3-year average and the Rs 5 crore net profit threshold. The 2021 amendments added further discipline: administrative overheads capped at 5% of CSR expenditure, mandatory impact assessment for companies with obligations of Rs 10 crore or more, CSR-1 registration for implementing agencies, and permission to carry forward excess spend against obligations for the next three years. The law makes CSR a board-level, accountable process rather than voluntary charity.

Key points:

  • India became the first country to mandate CSR by law through Section 135 of the Companies Act 2013.
  • A company is covered if it meets any one of three thresholds: net worth of Rs 500 crore or more, turnover of Rs 1,000 crore or more, or net profit of Rs 5 crore or more.
  • The law makes CSR a board-level, accountable process rather than voluntary charity.

Takeaway: The law makes CSR a board-level, accountable process rather than voluntary charity.

Q4. Describe the evolution of CSR in India and the four models of CSR given by TERI.

Answer: CSR in India has evolved through four phases. Phase 1 (1850-1914) was charity and philanthropy driven by culture and religion, exemplified by the Tatas and Birlas. Phase 2 (1914-1960) shifted to social development under Gandhian trusteeship theory, which held that wealth must be used for social good. Phase 3 (1960-1980) was the mixed-economy era, when state-led development and the licence raj made CSR largely an extension of government programmes. Phase 4 (1980 onwards) is the interface phase, where liberalisation encouraged firms to blend philanthropic and business motives. TERI identifies four models of CSR in India: the Ethical model, based on Gandhian trusteeship; the Statist model, reflecting Nehru's state-ownership approach; the Liberal model, following Friedman's view that business should maximise profits within the law; and the Stakeholder model, following Freeman's demand that the welfare of all stakeholders be served. Indian examples include the Tata Chemicals Society for Rural Development (1980), the Infosys Foundation (1996) and HUL's Project Shakti, which empowers rural women as entrepreneurs, showing CSR moving from charity to strategy.

Key points:

  • CSR in India has evolved through four phases.
  • Phase 1 (1850-1914) was charity and philanthropy driven by culture and religion, exemplified by the Tatas and Birlas.
  • Phase 2 (1914-1960) shifted to social development under Gandhian trusteeship theory, which held that wealth must be used for social good.
