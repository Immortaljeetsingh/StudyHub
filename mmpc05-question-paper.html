Q1. Random variable, probability distribution and their usefulness in decision-making

Answer: A random variable is a function that assigns a numerical value to each outcome of a random experiment — it converts uncertain events into numbers we can calculate with. A probability distribution lists every possible value of that variable with its probability (each between 0 and 1, all summing to exactly 1), and this pair powers expected value, risk and quality decisions.

If the values are countable — like the number of defective items in a batch of 50 from Tata Steel — it is a discrete random variable. If the values can take any number in a range — like battery life in months — it is a continuous random variable. Examples: the Binomial distribution (n independent trials, two outcomes, fixed probability p) and the Normal distribution (bell curve described by mean and standard deviation).

Usefulness in decision-making:

  • Expected value: E(X) = Σx × p(x) gives the long-run average — e.g., an insurer estimates average claims per policy.
  • Risk measurement: the distribution shows not just the average but how spread out outcomes are (variance/SD).
  • Expected Monetary Value (EMV): in decision theory, managers multiply each payoff by its probability and choose the action with the highest EMV — e.g., Reliance’s decision to launch Jio with free data was based on the expected subscriber payoff.
  • Quality control: the normal distribution tells what fraction of products will fail — e.g., how many batteries will be replaced under guarantee.

Key points

  • Random variable = outcomes turned into numbers; discrete = countable, continuous = measurable.
  • Probabilities must lie between 0 and 1 and must sum to 1.
  • E(X) = Σx × p(x) is the long-run average used in EMV.
  • Distributions (Binomial, Normal, Poisson) power risk analysis, insurance and quality decisions.
  • Memory aid: “Binomial = batches, Poisson = points, Normal = bell”.

Takeaway: A probability distribution is the decision-maker’s map of uncertainty — it converts “what might happen” into measurable expected values.

Q2. Solved numerical: Calculate Q2 and D6 for the profits of 100 companies

Answer: Q2 (median) = ₹56.67 lakh and D6 = ₹60 lakh; 50% of the companies earn ₹56.67 lakh or less, and 60% earn ₹60 lakh or less.

Step 1 – Build the cumulative frequency table: Total = 4 + 8 + 18 + 30 + 15 + 10 + 8 + 7 = 100 companies.

Step 2 – Locate Q2: Q2 is the median, i.e., the n/2 = 50th item. Cumulative frequencies: 4, 12, 30, 60, 75, 85, 93, 100. The 50th item falls in the class 50-60 (L = 50, cf = 30, f = 30, h = 10).

Q2 = L + [(n/2 − cf)/f] × h = 50 + [(50 − 30)/30] × 10 = 50 + 6.67 = 56.67

Interpretation of Q2: Q2 = ₹56.67 lakh is the median profit. It means 50% of the companies earn a profit of ₹56.67 lakh or less, and the other 50% earn more.

Step 3 – Locate D6: D6 is the 6th decile, i.e., the 6n/10 = 60th item. The 60th item also falls in the class 50-60 (cf = 30, f = 30, L = 50, h = 10).

D6 = L + [(6n/10 − cf)/f] × h = 50 + [(60 − 30)/30] × 10 = 50 + 10 = 60

Interpretation of D6: D6 = ₹60 lakh means 60% of the companies earn a profit of ₹60 lakh or less, and only 40% earn more. Since Q2 (56.67) is less than D6 (60), the distribution is slightly positively skewed — a few high-profit companies pull the upper tail.

Key points

  • Q2 = median = the n/2th item; D6 = the 6n/10th item of the ordered data.
  • Formula: L + [(k − cf)/f] × h — always build cumulative frequencies first.
  • Q2 = ₹56.67 lakh: 50% of companies earn less than this; D6 = ₹60 lakh: 60% earn less.
  • Q2 < D6 means slight positive skew — a few high-profit companies stretch the upper tail.

Takeaway: Quartiles and deciles read straight off the cumulative column — locate the position, identify the class, interpolate.

Q3. Why sampling is desirable — and often the only course — for inference

Answer: Sampling — selecting a subset from a larger population to draw conclusions about the whole — is desirable because it is cheaper, faster and often more accurate than a census, and it is the only practical course for destructive testing, infinite populations and huge scattered populations.

A well-designed sample of a few thousand households can represent 1.4 billion Indians. Sampling is desirable for the following reasons:

  • Lower cost: a sample survey costs a fraction of a census. India’s decennial Census costs ₹8,000+ crore, while NSSO sample surveys cost far less.
  • Less time: a sample can be collected and analysed in weeks; a census takes years and its results are outdated by publication.
  • Greater accuracy: with a smaller team, better-trained enumerators, closer supervision and deeper follow-up, sample data can actually be more reliable than hurried census data.
  • Destructive testing: when testing destroys the item — like testing matchsticks, bulbs or the crushing strength of cement — you cannot test the whole population; sampling is the only course.
  • Infinite or inaccessible populations: the population of possible outcomes (e.g., all future production of a machine) is unlimited, so a census is impossible.
  • Feasibility: when the population is huge and scattered, like all consumers of HUL’s shampoos, complete enumeration is physically impractical.
  • Statistical soundness: with probability sampling (simple random, stratified, cluster), sampling error can be measured and controlled, so conclusions remain valid.

Key points

  • Sampling saves cost and time while keeping conclusions valid.
  • It is the only option for destructive testing (bulbs, matchsticks, cement) and infinite populations.
  • Probability sampling gives measurable, controllable sampling error.
  • NSSO’s lakh-household samples represent the whole country reliably.

Takeaway: When you cannot measure everyone, measure a well-chosen few — the savings in cost and time usually come with better accuracy.

Q4. Solved numerical: applicants with a post-graduate degree or work experience

Answer: P(A or B) = 0.80, so out of 300 applicants about 240 have either a post-graduate degree or professional work experience (or both).

This is a direct application of the addition rule of probability. Let A = applicant has a post-graduate degree and B = applicant has work experience as a chief financial accountant.

Given: P(A) = 0.30, P(B) = 0.70, and P(A and B) = 0.20 (both).

Because the two events are not mutually exclusive (an applicant can have both), we subtract the overlap:

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B) = 0.30 + 0.70 − 0.20 = 0.80

Out of 300 applicants: Expected number = 300 × 0.80 = 240 applicants.

Check the logic: if we simply added 0.30 + 0.70 = 1.00, we would double-count the 0.20 who have both. Subtracting the overlap gives the correct 0.80. This is exactly how a company estimates the size of a qualified candidate pool before designing its recruitment drive.

Key points

  • Addition rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B).
  • Subtract the overlap only when events are not mutually exclusive.
  • Expected count = probability × total = 300 × 0.80 = 240.
  • Venn diagrams are the fastest way to spot double-counting.

Takeaway: When two events can happen together, subtract the intersection once — otherwise the union exceeds 1.

Q5. Testing for difference between proportions, hypotheses and pooled estimate of p

Answer: To test whether two population proportions differ, we set H0: p1 = p2 against H1: p1 ≠ p2, compute a Z statistic from the two sample proportions, and — because H0 assumes one common proportion — use the pooled estimate p̂ = (x1 + x2)/(n1 + n2) to calculate the standard error.

Procedure for testing the difference between two proportions (p1 − p2):

  • Step 1 – State the hypotheses: Null hypothesis H0: p1 = p2 (no difference between the two population proportions). Alternative hypothesis H1: p1 ≠ p2 (two-tailed), or p1 > p2 / p1 < p2 (one-tailed).
  • Step 2 – Choose the significance level: usually α = 0.05.
  • Step 3 – Select the test statistic: since proportions follow a normal distribution for large samples, use the Z-test: Z = (p1 − p2) ÷ SE(p1 − p2), where p1 and p2 are the sample proportions.
  • Step 4 – Calculate and decide: if the calculated |Z| exceeds the critical value (±1.96 at 5%), reject H0 and conclude the proportions differ significantly.

Use of the pooled estimate of p: when H0 assumes p1 = p2, the best estimate of that single common proportion is the pooled estimate: p̂ = (x1 + x2) ÷ (n1 + n2), where x1, x2 are successes and n1, n2 the sample sizes. The pooled p̂ is used to compute the standard error SE = √[p̂(1 − p̂)(1/n1 + 1/n2)], which makes the test more efficient because it uses all the information from both samples. Example: an HR manager comparing the promotion rates of men and women in two branches would pool the promoted counts across both branches under H0.

Key points

  • H0: p1 = p2; H1: p1 ≠ p2 (two-tailed) or one-sided.
  • Use the Z-test for large samples: Z = (p1 − p2)/SE.
  • Pool only under H0: p̂ = (x1 + x2)/(n1 + n2).
  • Reject H0 if |Z| > 1.96 at 5% significance.
  • The pooled estimate gives a more efficient (smaller) standard error.

Takeaway: Under “no difference”, both samples describe the same proportion — so merge them (pool) to get the tightest estimate of the standard error.

Q6. Solved numerical: batteries replaced under guarantee and net profit

Answer: (i) 122 batteries out of 10,000 will have to be replaced under the guarantee; (ii) the net profit is ₹4,87,800 after paying for replacements.

Given: Guarantee period = 24 months; Average life μ = 33 months; Standard deviation σ = 4 months; annual sales = 10,000 batteries; profit = ₹50 per battery; replacement cost = ₹100 per battery.

(i) How many batteries will be replaced? A battery is replaced if its life is less than 24 months. Convert to a Z score:

Z = (X − μ)/σ = (24 − 33)/4 = −9/4 = −2.25

Given: area between X = 24 and X = 33 is 0.4878 (the area between Z = −2.25 and the mean). The total left half of the normal curve is 0.5, so the area below X = 24 is:

P(X < 24) = 0.5 − 0.4878 = 0.0122 (1.22%)

Number replaced = 10,000 × 0.0122 = 122 batteries.

(ii) Net profit: Gross profit = 10,000 × ₹50 = ₹5,00,000. Replacement cost = 122 × ₹100 = ₹12,200.

Net profit = 5,00,000 − 12,200 = ₹4,87,800

Managerial insight: if the guarantee cost (₹12,200) is too high, the company can improve quality (raise mean life or lower S.D.), which shrinks the tail below 24 months — exactly how normal distribution analysis supports quality and warranty decisions.

Key points

  • Z = (X − μ)/σ = (24 − 33)/4 = −2.25.
  • Tail area = 0.5 − given area = 0.0122 (1.22%).
  • Expected replacements = probability × total = 10,000 × 0.0122 = 122.
  • Net profit = gross profit − replacement cost = ₹4,87,800.
  • 68-95-99.7 rule: 24 months is about 2.25σ below the mean, so the tail is tiny.

Takeaway: Every warranty is a bet against the left tail of the normal curve — compute the Z score, find the tail probability, then price the guarantee.

Q7. Short notes: Discrete vs Continuous data, Relative skewness, Cluster vs Stratum, Type I and Type II errors, Least square criterion

Answer: Five compact notes — on data types, skewness measures, the two sampling designs, the two hypothesis-testing errors, and the logic behind the line of best fit.

  • (a) Discrete and Continuous data: discrete data can only take countable, separate values (e.g., number of cars sold); continuous data can take any value within a range (e.g., weight). Bar charts suit discrete data; histograms suit continuous data.
  • (b) Relative skewness: skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution; relative skewness expresses it as a unit-free measure. Karl Pearson: SK = 3(Mean − Median)/SD; Bowley uses quartiles. Positive skew: Mean > Median > Mode (income data); negative skew: Mean < Median < Mode.
  • (c) Cluster vs Stratum: stratified sampling splits the population into homogeneous strata and samples every stratum proportionally — like a slice from each layer of a cake. Cluster sampling randomly selects whole groups and studies all units inside them — like picking whole grape bunches. Stratified is more precise; cluster is cheaper for scattered populations.
  • (d) Type I and Type II errors: Type I error (α) is rejecting a true null hypothesis — a false positive, like convicting an innocent person. Type II error (β) is accepting a false null hypothesis — a false negative, like letting a guilty person go free. The power of a test = 1 − β.
  • (e) Least square criterion: the line of best fit is the one that minimises the sum of squared vertical deviations Σ(Y − Ɔ)2 between actual and fitted Y. This gives the regression line Y = a + bX used in forecasting. Squaring stops positive and negative errors cancelling out.

Key points

  • Discrete = countable, continuous = measurable; bar chart vs histogram.
  • SK = 3(Mean − Median)/SD (Pearson); Bowley uses quartiles; both are unit-free.
  • Stratified = sample every layer; cluster = whole groups chosen randomly.
  • Type I = innocent convicted (α); Type II = guilty freed (β); power = 1 − β.
  • Least squares minimises Σ(Y − fitted Y)² — squaring stops sign cancellation.

Takeaway: Short notes are won by crisp contrast pairs — discrete/continuous, cluster/stratum, Type I/II — plus one formula each.

Q1 (15M). “The standard deviation is the most widely used and important measure of variation.” Comment. Also discuss the properties of a good measure of variation.

Answer: The statement is correct — the SD is preferred over range and quartile deviation because it uses every observation, is expressed in the original units, lends itself to further algebraic treatment, and forms the basis of the coefficient of variation, the normal curve and Z-tests.

Why SD is the most widely used measure: it is rigidly defined and based on all observations; deviations from the mean are squared, so positive and negative deviations do not cancel out; it is the square root of variance, so it stays in the original units (e.g., “months”, “rupees”); two variances can be combined mathematically, which range and quartile deviation cannot; it powers the empirical 68-95-99.7 rule and all Z-based tests.

Properties of a good measure of variation:

Key points:

  • Rigidly defined: the formula gives one unambiguous value for a given data set.
  • Based on all observations: it must use every item, not just two (range fails here).
  • Easy to understand and compute: should be simple enough for routine use.
  • Least affected by sampling fluctuations: should be stable across samples.
  • Capable of further algebraic treatment: variances can be added, combined and manipulated.
  • Less affected by extreme values: extreme items should not distort the measure.

Takeaway: SD wins because it uses all data, keeps original units and supports further statistics — its one weakness (sensitivity to extremes) is inherited from squaring.

Q3 (15M). Find the coefficient of correlation between X and Y: X = 1 to 9; Y = 12, 11, 13, 15, 14, 17, 16, 19, 18.

Answer: r = 0.933 — a very strong positive linear relationship between X and Y.

Working (n = 9): ΣX = 45, ΣY = 135, ΣXY = 731, ΣX² = 285, ΣY² = 2085.

r = [nΣXY − ΣXΣY] / √[(nΣX² − (ΣX)²)(nΣY² − (ΣY)²)]

  = [9(731) − 45(135)] / √[(9×285 − 45²)(9×2085 − 135²)]

  = 504 / √(540 × 540) = 504/540 = 0.933

Key points

  • Product-sum formula: r = [nΣXY − ΣXΣY] / √[...].
  • Both denominators (540) are equal here — a coincidence of this data, not a rule.
  • r = 0.933 → very strong positive; r² = 0.87 of variance explained.

Takeaway: r is unit-free and always between −1 and +1 — 0.933 means “strong upward linear association”.

Q4 (15M). “Time series analysis is one of the most powerful methods in use, especially for short-term forecasting purposes.” Comment. Also explain the decomposition method.

Answer: Time series analysis uses the past pattern of a variable (sales, demand, prices) to project it forward — ideal for short-term forecasting because the recent past is the best guide to the near future; the decomposition method splits the series into trend, seasonal, cyclical and irregular components.

Key points:

  • Comment on the statement: short-term forecasts (weeks to a year) are needed for inventory, cash budgets, production scheduling and sales quotas, and time series methods such as moving averages, exponential smoothing and trend projection deliver these cheaply and quickly. They work best when the pattern is stable; they fail in structural breaks (new competitor, pandemic, policy shock).
  • Decomposition method: assume the observed series Y is built from components — T (trend: long-run direction), S (seasonal: repeating within the year), C (cyclical: multi-year business cycles) and I (irregular: random shocks).
  • Multiplicative model: Y = T × S × C × I — used when seasonal swings grow with the level (e.g., ice-cream sales).
  • Additive model: Y = T + S + C + I — used when swings stay constant in size.
  • Procedure: estimate T (moving average/least squares) → divide (or subtract) to get S × C — average by season to isolate S → remove S and T to expose C → residual is I. Forecasts are then rebuilt as T × S.

Takeaway: Decompose first, forecast second — a seasonal index plus a trend line is the classic short-term forecasting engine.

Q5 (15M). What are the various key issues in decision theory? Explain the decision tree approach.

Answer: Decision theory is the formal study of choosing among alternatives when the outcomes are uncertain; its key issues are the decision-maker’s alternatives, states of nature, payoffs, probabilities, criteria and attitude to risk, and the decision tree is the branch diagram that structures these choices for evaluation.

Key points:

  • Key issues: (1) alternatives (the courses of action available); (2) states of nature (future conditions outside the manager’s control — boom, recession); (3) payoffs (consequences, usually money, of each alternative-state pair); (4) probabilities of each state (known in risk, unknown in uncertainty); (5) decision criteria (EMV, maximax, maximin, minimax regret); (6) risk attitude of the decision-maker.
  • Decision tree approach: a tree drawn left to right with square nodes (decisions, where the manager chooses) and circle nodes (chance, where a state of nature occurs with its probability).
  • Evaluation (roll-back): at each chance node compute the expected payoff Σ(payoff × probability); at each decision node choose the branch with the highest expected value; work from the rightmost branches back to the root.
  • Advantages: forces explicit listing of alternatives and states; handles sequential decisions (decide now, learn, decide again); visualises probabilities and payoffs; can incorporate the cost of gathering more information.

Takeaway: A decision tree makes an uncertain problem visible — roll back from the right, choosing the branch with the best expected payoff.

Q6 (15M). Short notes on any three: (a) Coefficient of variation (b) Bayes’ theorem (c) Probability sampling method (d) Correlation coefficient (e) Chi-square distribution.

Answer: Five compact notes covering the relative variability measure, the inverse-probability rule, sampling with known chance, the strength of linear association and the square-of-normal distribution.

Key points:

  • (a) Coefficient of variation: CV = (SD/Mean) × 100 — a unit-free relative measure that compares variability of two series with different means or units (e.g., prices of gold vs shares). Lower CV = more consistent. It is also used for risk per unit of return in investments.
  • (b) Bayes’ theorem: revises prior probabilities using new evidence: P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) / ΣP(B|Ai)·P(Ai). The prior is updated to a posterior after observing data — the engine of modern spam filters and medical diagnostics.
  • (c) Probability sampling method: every unit has a known, non-zero chance of selection — simple random (lottery), systematic (every kth), stratified (proportional slices) and cluster (whole groups). It allows sampling error to be measured, so inferential statistics are valid.
  • (d) Correlation coefficient: r measures the strength and direction of linear association, between −1 and +1; r = 0 means no linear relation. It does not imply causation.
  • (e) Chi-square distribution: the distribution of the sum of squares of independent standard normals, with df = k − 1 etc.; used for goodness-of-fit and independence tests — compare computed χ² with the table value at the chosen significance level.

Takeaway: CV for comparing spread, Bayes for updating beliefs, probability sampling for measurable error, r for association, χ² for categorical tests.

Q8 (20M, Section B). What do you understand by “hypothesis”? Explain null and alternate hypothesis with the help of examples.

Answer: A hypothesis is a tentative statement or assumption about a population parameter (mean, proportion, variance) that is tested against sample data; the null hypothesis H0 is the default “no effect / no difference” claim to be disproved, while the alternative H1 is the research claim accepted when H0 is rejected.

Key points:

  • Hypothesis: a formal, testable statement — e.g., “the average life of this battery is 33 months”. We never test the whole population; we draw a sample and decide whether the sample evidence contradicts the hypothesis.
  • Null hypothesis (H0): the hypothesis of no change or no difference, always stated with equality (μ = μ0, p = p0). Example: H0: μ = 24 months (the guarantee life). It is presumed true until evidence says otherwise — like “innocent until proven guilty”.
  • Alternative hypothesis (H1 or Ha): the claim we accept if the sample evidence is strong enough — μ ≠ μ0 (two-tailed), μ > μ0 or μ < μ0 (one-tailed). Example: H1: μ ≠ 24 months.
  • Examples: (i) a cola bottler claims 500 ml per bottle — H0: μ = 500 ml, H1: μ ≠ 500 ml; (ii) a training programme claims to raise scores — H0: μ1 ≤ μ2, H1: μ1 > μ2; (iii) a coin is fair — H0: p = 0.5, H1: p ≠ 0.5.
  • Decision logic: we reject H0 only when the sample statistic falls in the rejection region (beyond the critical value), at a chosen significance level α.

Takeaway: H0 is the sceptic’s default, H1 is the researcher’s claim — the test decides which the sample supports.

Q1 (15M). “The arithmetic mean is the most commonly used and readily understood measure of central tendency.” Do you agree? Comment. Also explain the mathematical properties of the arithmetic mean.

Answer: Yes — the mean is the most widely used average because it is easy to calculate, uses every observation and has a clear interpretation; its mathematical properties (algebraic sum of deviations zero, minimisation of squared deviations, and its role in combining groups) make it the natural foundation for further statistics.

Key points:

  • Comment: everyone understands “the average”; it is rigidly defined and based on all items; it supports arithmetic like adding several years’ averages. Its limitation: extreme values distort it (e.g., one billionaire raises the mean income of a village), which is why median and mode exist.
  • Mathematical properties:
  • Sum of deviations: Σ(X − X̄) = 0 — positive and negative deviations cancel.
  • Minimum squared deviations: Σ(X − X̄)² is less than Σ(X − A)² for any A ≠ X̄ — the mean is the least-squares centre of the data.
  • Combination of groups: overall mean of two groups = weighted average: X̄ = (n1X̄1 + n2X̄2)/(n1 + n2).
  • Effect of change of origin/scale: adding a constant to every value adds it to the mean; multiplying every value multiplies the mean.

Q4 (15M). What do you understand by ‘correlation coefficient’? Discuss the different types of association between variables.

Q5 (15M). Explain the ‘chi-square distribution’. Explain how you would use it in testing independence of categorised data and testing the goodness of fit.

Answer: The chi-square (χ²) distribution is the probability distribution of the sum of squares of independent standard normal variables, indexed by degrees of freedom (df); it is the standard tool for tests on categorical data — independence of classifications and goodness of fit.

Key points:

  • Nature of the distribution: defined for non-negative values, skewed right for small df, approaching a normal shape as df grows; mean = df, variance = 2(df). The test statistic is χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E, where O are observed and E expected frequencies.
  • Test of independence: H0: the two attributes are independent (e.g., smoking status and lung condition). Build a contingency table; expected frequency for each cell = (row total × column total)/grand total; compute Σ(O − E)²/E with df = (rows − 1)(columns − 1); reject H0 if the computed value exceeds the table χ² at the chosen level.
  • Test of goodness of fit: H0: the data follow a specified distribution (Binomial, Normal, uniform). Divide the range into classes, compute expected frequencies from the hypothesised distribution, then χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E with df = k − 1 (k = number of classes, minus parameters estimated); a large value means the data do not fit the hypothesised distribution.
  • Conditions: observations independent; expected frequencies should generally not be below 5 (merge adjacent classes if needed).

Takeaway: χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E is the single formula behind both independence and goodness-of-fit tests — only the expected frequencies and df change.

Q8 (20M, Section B). Explain the concept of the Bernoulli process along with the Binomial distribution in detail.

Answer: The Bernoulli process is a sequence of independent trials with exactly two outcomes (success/failure) and a constant success probability p; the Binomial distribution counts the number of successes X in n such trials, with P(X = x) = C(n, x) px qn−x.

Key points:

  • Bernoulli process conditions: (1) n identical trials; (2) each trial has exactly two outcomes — success or failure; (3) trials are independent — the outcome of one does not affect another; (4) probability of success p is constant trial after trial (q = 1 − p).
  • Binomial random variable: X = number of successes in n trials; possible values 0, 1, ..., n.
  • Probability function: P(X = x) = C(n, x) px qn−x, where C(n, x) = n!/[x!(n−x)!] counts the ways of choosing which x trials succeed.
  • Mean and variance: E(X) = np; Var(X) = npq. Example: 6 tosses of a fair coin, p = 0.5, mean = 3 heads.
  • Real examples: number of defective items in a batch (defective/not); number of patients cured by a drug (cured/not); number of applicants who pass an interview.
  • Shape: symmetric when p = 0.5; skewed right when p < 0.5; skewed left when p > 0.5; approaches the normal curve as n grows.

Takeaway: Bernoulli = one trial, Binomial = n Bernoulli trials — the binomial counts successes with mean np and variance npq.

Q4. Explain the various criteria that are helpful in taking decisions, when the probability of occurrence of outcomes are not known.

Answer: Under uncertainty (probabilities unknown) managers use decision criteria based on the payoff matrix alone — maximax (optimism), maximin (pessimism), minimax regret, Laplace (equal likelihood) and Hurwicz (weighted optimism) — each reflecting a different attitude to risk.

Key points:

  • Maximax (optimistic): for each alternative note the best possible payoff; choose the alternative with the largest of these — “best of the best”. For risk lovers; ignores worst cases.

Takeaway: No probabilities → choose a criterion that matches your risk attitude: maximax hopes, maximin fears, regret minimises “what if”, Laplace splits evenly.

Q5 (15M). Explain the concept of sampling distribution. Also state why we need to study sampling distributions.

Answer: The sampling distribution of a statistic is the probability distribution of all its possible values over repeated samples of the same size from the same population — and studying it is how we measure sampling error and make probability statements about unknown population parameters.

Key points:

  • Concept: draw every possible sample of size n; compute the statistic (mean, proportion) from each; the histogram of these values is the sampling distribution. For the mean, the Central Limit Theorem says it is approximately normal with mean μ and standard error σ/√n.
  • Why study them: (1) to quantify sampling error — how far sample estimates typically deviate from the population value; (2) to make probability statements — e.g., P(X̄ > 66) is computable because the distribution of X̄ is known; (3) to build confidence intervals — the 95% interval is estimate ± 1.96 SE; (4) to design hypothesis tests — Z and t tests compare a statistic with its sampling distribution under H0; (5) to choose sample size — the SE shrinks with √n, so required precision dictates n.
  • Key idea: the statistic is a random variable with its own distribution — different from the population distribution and from the sample’s.

Takeaway: Every inference — interval or test — is a statement about the sampling distribution; SE = σ/√n is its spread.

Q6 (15M). Short notes on any three: (a) Mathematical properties of Median (b) Pascal Distribution (c) Hypothesis Testing Procedure (d) Linear Regression (e) Stratified Sampling.

Answer: Five compact notes on the positional average, the negative binomial, the standard test sequence, the least-squares line and the proportional-slices design.

Key points:

  • (a) Mathematical properties of Median: the median is the positional average (n+1)/2th item; Σ|X − Median| is a minimum — the median is the least-absolute-deviation centre; it is not affected by extreme values; it can be found for open-ended classes; the combined median of groups cannot be computed from group medians alone (needs the full data), unlike the mean.
  • (b) Pascal Distribution: negative binomial — number of trials needed to get r successes: P(X = x) = C(x − 1, r − 1) pr qx−r; the waiting-time cousin of the binomial (r = 1 gives the geometric distribution).
  • (c) Hypothesis Testing Procedure: state H0 and H1 → choose α → pick the test statistic (Z, t, χ², F) → compute it from sample data → compare with the critical value → reject or do not reject H0 → state the conclusion in business language.
  • (d) Linear Regression: models a dependent variable as a linear function of an independent one: Y = a + bX; coefficients come from the normal equations or b = Σ(X − X̄)(Y − ŷ)/Σ(X − X̄)²; b is the slope — the expected change in Y per unit of X; used for prediction, unlike correlation which only measures association.
  • (e) Stratified Sampling: divide the population into homogeneous strata (age, income, region) and draw a proportional random sample from each; guarantees representation of every stratum and usually lowers sampling error for the same cost — the NSSO’s design.

Q8 (20M, Section B). Explain the term probability theory. What are the different approaches to probability theory?

Answer: Probability theory is the mathematics of chance — assigning a number between 0 and 1 to the likelihood of an event — and it is approached in three main ways: classical (equally likely outcomes), relative frequency (long-run proportions) and subjective (personal degree of belief).

Key points:

  • Terminology: an experiment has outcomes; the sample space is the set of all outcomes; an event is a subset of outcomes. P(A) = 0 means impossible, P(A) = 1 certain, and P(A) + P(A’) = 1 always.
  • Classical (a priori) approach: P(A) = favourable outcomes/total outcomes when all outcomes are equally likely — e.g., a fair die: P(even) = 3/6 = 0.5. No experimentation needed; fails when outcomes are not equally likely.
  • Relative frequency (empirical) approach: P(A) = number of times A occurs/total trials, as trials repeat; e.g., a machine’s defect rate = defects/total produced last year. Objective and data-driven; needs a long run of stable conditions.
  • Subjective approach: probability as a personal degree of belief, revised with experience — e.g., “75% chance the rupee strengthens next quarter”. Used in business when data are scarce; the basis of Bayesian updating.
  • Axiomatic approach (Kolmogorov): probability is a function satisfying three axioms — non-negativity, certainty of the sample space, and additivity for disjoint events; all other rules (addition, multiplication, Bayes) are theorems from these.

Takeaway: Classical counts equally likely cases, frequency counts long-run rates, subjectivity encodes judgement — and the axioms unify them all.

Q1 (15M). “Different issues arise while analysing decision problems under uncertain conditions of outcomes.” Explain the concept of decision theory and certain key issues involved in decision theory.

Answer: Decision theory is the systematic analysis of choosing among alternatives when outcomes are uncertain; its key issues are alternatives, states of nature, payoffs, probabilities, decision criteria and the decision-maker’s risk attitude — and each interacts differently depending on whether probabilities are known (risk) or unknown (uncertainty).

Key points:

  • Concept: structure the problem as a payoff matrix — rows = alternatives (what the manager can choose), columns = states of nature (what the market/ environment can do), cells = payoffs. Then apply a criterion to select the best row.
  • Key issues:
  • Alternatives: the courses of action available — launch the product or not, expand or not.
  • States of nature: future conditions outside managerial control — high/low demand, boom/recession.
  • Payoffs: the consequence (usually profit/cost) of each alternative under each state.
  • Probabilities: known (decision under risk) or unknown (decision under uncertainty — then maximax/maximin/regret criteria apply).
  • Criteria and risk attitude: EMV and expected utility for risk; maximax (optimist), maximin (pessimist), minimax regret, Laplace and Hurwicz for uncertainty.
  • Information and sequencing: whether information can be bought (expected value of perfect information, EVPI) and whether decisions are sequential (decision trees).

Takeaway: Uncertain outcomes force a choice of criterion — the “right” decision depends on how much you fear the worst case.

Q4 (15M). What do you understand by non-probability sampling methods? Explain the various types of non-probability sampling.

Answer: Non-probability sampling selects units without known probabilities of selection — based on convenience, judgement, quotas or referrals — which makes it cheap and fast but unable to measure sampling error; the main types are convenience, purposive (judgement), quota and snowball sampling.

Key points:

  • Convenience sampling: whoever is easiest to reach — shoppers in a mall, students in a canteen. Fastest and cheapest; highest risk of bias.
  • Purposive (judgement) sampling: the researcher hand-picks units believed representative — e.g., interviewing 20 dealers for an industry view; quality depends on the researcher’s expertise.
  • Quota sampling: population is divided into groups (age, gender, region) and interviewers fill fixed quotas per group — an “unrandomised” stratified sample; ensures group representation without random selection.
  • Snowball sampling: initial respondents recruit further respondents from their networks — ideal for rare or hidden populations (drug users, professionals in niche fields); chain referral can quickly saturate a segment.
  • Limitation: since selection probabilities are unknown, statistical inference (confidence intervals, tests) is not valid — results describe the sample only.

Takeaway: Non-probability designs trade representativeness for speed — fine for exploration and pilot studies, unsafe for formal inference.

Q5 (15M). “Time series analysis is one of the most powerful methods in use, especially for short-term forecasting purposes.” Comment. Also explain in brief the decomposition method.

Answer: The statement is right for stable, patterned series — time series methods exploit the recent pattern of the variable itself, which is exactly what short-term forecasting needs; the decomposition method separates the series into trend, seasonal, cyclical and irregular components and forecasts by projecting the systematic parts.

Key points:

  • Comment: short-term forecasts (inventory, cash, scheduling, sales quotas) need quick, cheap, data-only projections — moving averages, exponential smoothing and trend lines deliver these well. Time series methods assume “the past pattern continues”; they weaken after structural breaks and cannot forecast turning points caused by new policies or competition.
  • Decomposition method: model Y = T × S × C × I (multiplicative) or Y = T + S + C + I (additive), where T = trend (long-run direction), S = seasonal (within-year pattern), C = cyclical (multi-year business cycle), I = irregular (random shocks).
  • Procedure: compute a centred moving average to extract T + C; divide Y by it to get S × I; average the same months/quarters to isolate the seasonal index S; smooth out I; then forecast as (trend projection) × (seasonal index).
  • Output: seasonal indices (e.g., December sales = 1.3 × trend) used directly in production and inventory planning.

Takeaway: Trend tells you where the series is heading; the seasonal index tells you where it is this month — multiply them to forecast.

Q6 (15M). Short notes on any three: (a) Skewness (b) Binomial Distribution (c) Type 1 error (d) The Correlation Coefficient (e) Cluster Sampling.

Answer: Five compact notes on asymmetry, the success-counting distribution, the false-positive error, the linear-association measure and the whole-groups design.

Key points:

  • (a) Skewness: asymmetry of a distribution around its centre. Positive skew: long right tail, Mean > Median > Mode (income, profits); negative skew: long left tail, Mean < Median < Mode. Measures: Pearson SK = 3(Mean − Median)/SD; Bowley via quartiles. Symmetric distributions (normal) have SK = 0.
  • (b) Binomial Distribution: counts successes in n independent two-outcome trials with constant p: P(X = x) = C(n, x) px qn−x; mean np, variance npq; symmetric at p = 0.5, skew otherwise; approximates normal for large n.
  • (c) Type 1 error: rejecting a true null hypothesis (α) — a false alarm or false positive, e.g., declaring a good batch defective; probability = α (the significance level chosen, usually 0.05); reducing α raises the Type II risk (β).
  • (d) The Correlation Coefficient: r between −1 and +1 measuring strength/direction of linear association; r² = proportion of shared variation; r = 0 means no linear relation; correlation is not causation.
  • (e) Cluster Sampling: divide the population into clusters (villages, schools, wards), sample clusters at random, and enumerate all units in the chosen clusters — cheaper for scattered populations, but less precise than stratified sampling when clusters are heterogeneous.

Takeaway: Skewness shows which tail is longer; binomial counts successes; Type I = false alarm; r measures linearity; clusters cut travel costs.

Q8 (20M, Section B). Explain the various diagrams and graphs that can be used for charting a frequency distribution.
