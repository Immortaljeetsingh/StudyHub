Q1. Explain the TELOS framework of project feasibility analysis.

Answer: Feasibility analysis assesses whether a proposed project is practical and viable before resources are committed, and decides whether the project should proceed, be modified or be abandoned. The TELOS framework evaluates five dimensions. Technical feasibility asks “can we build it?” - the availability of technology and skilled labour. Economic feasibility asks “should we build it?” - using investment appraisal methods such as NPV, IRR, payback period and ROI. Legal feasibility asks “are we allowed to?” - permits, regulations and compliance. Operational feasibility asks “will it work?” - whether the output integrates with existing systems and processes. Schedule feasibility asks “can we do it on time?” - whether deadlines are realistic. NPV is the difference between the present value of cash inflows and outflows; accept when NPV is positive. IRR is the discount rate at which NPV equals zero; accept when IRR exceeds the required rate. The payback period is the simplest method - the time taken to recover the initial investment - while ROI = (net profit / cost of investment) x 100. Together these tools give management a complete picture of project viability before sanction.

Key points:

  • Feasibility analysis assesses whether a proposed project is practical and viable before resources are committed, and decides whether the project should proceed, be modified or be abandoned.
  • Economic feasibility asks “should we build it?” - using investment appraisal methods such as NPV, IRR, payback period and ROI.
  • The TELOS framework evaluates five dimensions.

Takeaway: Together these tools give management a complete picture of project viability before sanction.

Q2. What is the critical path in project network analysis and how is it determined?

Answer: In project network analysis, the critical path is the longest sequence of dependent activities through the network. It determines the minimum project duration - any delay in a critical activity delays the whole project, and critical activities have zero float. The critical path is found through two passes on the network. The forward pass calculates the earliest start (ES) and earliest finish (EF) of every activity, starting with ES = 0 at the first node and setting each successor’s ES to the maximum of its predecessors’ EF values. The backward pass calculates the latest start (LS) and latest finish (LF), working back from the project end date and setting each predecessor’s LF to the minimum of its successors’ LS values. The float of an activity is LS - ES (or LF - EF); activities with zero float lie on the critical path. In PERT, times are probabilistic: te = (O + 4M + P)/6 with variance ((P - O)/6)², while CPM uses deterministic single estimates. Identifying the critical path tells managers which activities need the tightest control and which have flexibility, and it is the basis for crashing decisions.

Key points:

  • In project network analysis, the critical path is the longest sequence of dependent activities through the network.
  • It determines the minimum project duration - any delay in a critical activity delays the whole project, and critical activities have zero float.
  • Identifying the critical path tells managers which activities need the tightest control and which have flexibility, and it is the basis for crashing decisions.

Takeaway: Identifying the critical path tells managers which activities need the tightest control and which have flexibility, and it is the basis for crashing decisions.

Q3. Explain Earned Value Analysis (EVA) and its key formulas.

Answer: Earned Value Analysis integrates scope, schedule and cost data to assess project performance, answering “are we on time?” and “are we on budget?”. It compares three values: Planned Value (PV) - the budgeted cost of work scheduled; Earned Value (EV) - the budgeted cost of work actually performed; and Actual Cost (AC) - what was actually spent. Cost Variance CV = EV - AC (positive means under budget); Schedule Variance SV = EV - PV (positive means ahead of schedule). CPI = EV/AC measures cost efficiency - greater than 1 is efficient - and SPI = EV/PV measures schedule efficiency. Forecasts use BAC (budget at completion): EAC = BAC/CPI estimates the final cost, ETC = EAC - AC the remaining cost, VAC = BAC - EAC the expected variance, and TCPI = (BAC - EV)/(BAC - AC) the efficiency needed to finish within budget. Example: BAC = Rs 500 lakh with PV = 200, EV = 180 and AC = 220 gives CV = -40, CPI = 0.82 and EAC = Rs 609.76 lakh, signalling an overrun requiring corrective action. EVA is the highest-frequency numerical topic in IGNOU exams.

  • Earned Value Analysis integrates scope, schedule and cost data to assess project performance, answering “are we on time?” and “are we on budget?”.

Q4. What are the strategies for responding to project risks?

Answer: Project risk is an uncertain event that, if it occurs, affects project objectives - negatively as a threat or positively as an opportunity. The four strategies for negative risks are remembered as ATMA. Avoid eliminates the threat by changing the plan or approach. Transfer shifts the impact to a third party, for example buying insurance or outsourcing a risky component. Mitigate reduces the probability or impact of the risk, such as using proven technology or adding schedule buffer. Accept acknowledges the risk and takes no action, passively or with a contingency reserve. The four strategies for positive risks are ESEA. Exploit ensures the opportunity happens, for instance fast-tracking a beneficial phase. Share allocates ownership to a third party best able to capture the benefit, as in a joint venture. Enhance increases the probability or impact of the opportunity. Accept again means taking no action. Risks are recorded in a risk register with probability and impact scores, prioritized through the probability-impact matrix, and responses are monitored throughout the project. Contingency reserves cover identified risks while management reserves cover unknown risks.

Key points:

  • Project risk is an uncertain event that, if it occurs, affects project objectives - negatively as a threat or positively as an opportunity.
  • Transfer shifts the impact to a third party, for example buying insurance or outsourcing a risky component.
  • Avoid eliminates the threat by changing the plan or approach.

Takeaway: The four strategies for negative risks are remembered as ATMA.
