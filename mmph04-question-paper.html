  • Q1. Explain the concept of industrial and employment relations. Describe the systems approach to industrial relations.

    Answer: Industrial relations are the relationships between management, workers and government in the world of work; the systems approach (Dunlop, 1958) sees three actors interacting in technology, market and power contexts to generate a web of rules.

    Industrial relations (IR) means the relationships between management, workers, and the government in the world of work - covering wages, working conditions, disputes, unions, and collective bargaining. Employment relations is the wider, modern term: it covers not only unionised industrial workers but every form of employer-employee relationship, including contract, gig, and white-collar employment. IR has three main perspectives: the unitary approach, which sees the organisation as one family with common interests (so conflict is treated as an aberration); the pluralist approach, which accepts that management and workers have different interests that must be balanced through rules and negotiation; and the Marxist approach, which sees conflict as inevitable under capitalism. The systems approach to IR was given by John T. Dunlop (1958). It sees the IR system as a subsystem of society with three actors - workers and their unions, management and its organisations, and the government - interacting in three contexts: the technology context (how work is done), the market context (product and labour market pressures), and the power context (where power lies in society). These actors and contexts together produce a web of rules - laws, agreements, and customs - that govern the workplace. Example: India's IR system has all three actors (unions like INTUC and CITU, employer bodies like CII, and the government through the Ministry of Labour) generating rules through the Industrial Disputes Act and collective agreements. The systems approach is valued because it explains why each country's IR looks different - its rules reflect its contexts.

    Key points to remember: IR = relations among management, workers, government; employment relations = broader, includes all employment forms; three approaches = unitary, pluralist, Marxist; Dunlop's systems theory = actors (workers, management, government) + contexts (technology, market, power) → web of rules.

    Takeaway: Remember Dunlop's formula: three actors plus three contexts produce the web of rules that governs the workplace.

  • Q2. Describe the salient features of (a) Wage Code, 2019, (b) Industrial Relations Code, 2020.

    Answer: The Wage Code 2019 unifies four wage laws with one national definition of wages, a floor wage and equal pay for equal work, while the IR Code 2020 consolidates union, dispute and standing-order laws with a single negotiating union at 51% membership.

    The four new labour codes simplify 29 old labour laws; the Wage Code and the IR Code are two of them. (a) The Code on Wages, 2019 consolidates the Payment of Wages Act, Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Bonus Act, and Equal Remuneration Act. Its salient features: a single national definition of "wages" (basic pay plus allowances, excluding bonus and overtime, with a 50% ceiling rule for what counts as wage); universal coverage of all employments and all workers, including those in unorganised sectors; a floor wage fixed by the central government that states must not go below; timely payment of wages on or before the 7th of the next month; and no gender discrimination - equal wages for equal work for men and women. (b) The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 consolidates the Trade Unions Act, Industrial Disputes Act, and Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act. Its salient features: a single negotiating union is recognised if it has 51% membership, reducing union multiplicity; the threshold for requiring government permission for retrenchment rises from 100 to 300 workers - the code allows companies with up to 300 workers to retrench without prior government approval; standing orders (service rules) are required for establishments with 300+ workers; fixed-term employment is legally recognised; and a re-skilling fund is created - a retrenching company must pay 15 days' wages to the fund per worker. Example: an auto parts unit with 250 workers can now downsize without government permission, but must contribute to the re-skilling fund, which trains retrenched workers for new jobs. These codes aim at ease of doing business while protecting core worker rights.

    Key points to remember: Wage Code = one definition of wages (50% rule), universal coverage, floor wage, equal pay for equal work; IR Code = single negotiating union at 51%, retrenchment permission threshold raised to 300 workers, fixed-term employment, re-skilling fund.

    Takeaway: The two codes simplify 29 old laws - remember the 50% wage-ceiling rule and the retrenchment threshold raised to 300 workers.

  • Q3. What are the functions of labour administration machineries? Explain functioning and roles of the labour administrative machineries in states.

    Answer: Labour administration machinery implements labour policy through enforcement, registration, conciliation, statistics, welfare and policy advice, with the state Labour Commissioner heading the frontline machinery of inspectors, Labour Officers, courts and tribunals.

    Labour administration means the machinery through which the government implements labour policy and law. Its main functions are: enforcement of labour laws through inspections (factories, wages, safety, hours); registration and licensing of establishments and contractors; conciliation of industrial disputes before they escalate; collection of labour statistics for planning; implementation of welfare and social security schemes (ESI, PF); and advising the government on labour policy. At the centre, the Ministry of Labour and Employment sets policy, while the Chief Labour Commissioner (CLC) coordinates enforcement and conciliation for central laws. At the state level, the machinery works directly with workplaces. The state Labour Commissioner is the head of the state machinery and runs: factory inspection services, which check safety and working conditions under the Factories Act; the offices of Assistant Labour Commissioners and Labour Officers, who register establishments, inspect wages and records, and act as conciliation officers who mediate between management and unions in disputes; Labour Courts and Industrial Tribunals, which adjudicate disputes that conciliation cannot settle; welfare boards, which run provident fund, gratuity, and other schemes; and the Labour Department's statistical wings. Example: in a dispute at a textile mill, the state Labour Officer first inspects the complaint, the Assistant Labour Commissioner tries conciliation, and only if talks fail does the matter go to the Labour Court. The state machinery is therefore the frontline - most disputes are detected, inspected, and settled at the state level long before they reach the centre or the courts.

    Key points to remember: Functions = enforcement, registration, conciliation, statistics, welfare, policy advice; centre = Ministry and CLC; states = Labour Commissioner, inspectors, Labour Officers (conciliation), Labour Courts and Tribunals; states are the frontline of dispute handling.

    Takeaway: States are the frontline of dispute handling: inspectors detect, Labour Officers conciliate, and Labour Courts and Tribunals adjudicate what talks cannot settle.

  • Q4. Write a comprehension on the evolution of managerial unions in India. Describe the boundaries of managerial associations.

    Answer: Managerial unions in India grew from bank officers' associations (1950s-60s) through PSU executive associations and all-India federations, while staying confined to service conditions such as pay, promotion and transfers.

    Managerial unions are associations of officers, executives, and supervisors - white-collar staff above the worker level. Their evolution in India follows the growth of the organised sector. After independence, the nationalised banks were the first big employers of managerial staff, and officers' associations emerged in the 1950s-1960s - the All India Bank Officers' Confederation (AIBOC) and the All India Bank Officers' Association (AIBOA) are among the oldest. In the 1970s-1980s, as PSUs like BHEL, NTPC, and the LIC grew, executives' associations spread to every public-sector organisation, often with national federations. From the 1990s, liberalisation changed the scene: private-sector managers also formed associations (for example, officers' associations in new private banks), while multinational companies usually resisted recognition. Managerial associations were influenced by the Justice Punekar and later committee recommendations, and by 1988-89 an all-India federation of managerial staff was formed with about 100,000 members. The boundaries of managerial associations: membership is restricted to executives and supervisors, excluding workers; they generally cannot claim to represent workers in collective bargaining; their primary goals are service conditions - pay, promotion, transfers, and fairness of appraisal - rather than wage bargaining; and they are sometimes denied trade-union status or the right to strike, since managers are seen as part of management; recognition by the employer is voluntary in the private sector. Example: a bank officer association negotiates pay scales and transfer policy with the bank's board, but does not join worker unions in strikes. In short, managerial unions seek a voice for officers while staying within the boundaries of managerial identity.

    Key points to remember: Evolution = bank officers' associations (1950s-60s) → PSU executive associations (70s-80s) → private sector and federations (90s onward); boundaries = managers only, no workers, service conditions focus, limited bargaining rights, recognition voluntary in private sector.

    Takeaway: Managerial associations cover executives only, avoid worker wage-bargaining and strikes, and enjoy only voluntary recognition in the private sector.

  • Q5. Enlist the elements of collective bargaining. Give a brief account of various theories on collective bargaining.

    Answer: Collective bargaining is a voluntary give-and-take between union and management over wages, hours and conditions, explained by the theories of Webb, Dunlop, Chamberlain, and Walton and McKersie.

    Collective bargaining is the process in which representatives of workers (unions) and management negotiate the terms and conditions of employment, ending in a collective agreement. Its elements are: two organised parties - the union representing workers and management; a voluntary, give-and-take process of negotiation; the subjects of bargaining - wages, hours, working conditions, bonuses, and discipline; the collective agreement itself, which is binding for a fixed period (usually 1-3 years); the principle of good faith on both sides; and the requirement of industrial peace during the currency of the agreement. Theories of collective bargaining: Sidney and Beatrice Webb's economic theory (1897) saw bargaining as a power contest over the wage bargain, where unions raise workers' strength through collective withholding of labour. Dunlop's systems theory treats bargaining as rule-making inside the industrial relations system, producing a web of rules. Chamberlain's theory sees bargaining power as the ability to impose costs on the other party and to secure agreement; he distinguished economic power (cost of disagreement) and political power. Walton and McKersie's behavioural theory (1965) identified four sub-processes: distributive bargaining (dividing a fixed pie - wages), integrative bargaining (enlarging the pie through joint problem-solving), attitudinal structuring (shaping trust and attitudes between parties), and intra-organisational bargaining (aligning each side's own constituents). Example: an annual wage negotiation in a factory shows all four - the union demands 15% (distributive), both sides explore productivity-linked pay (integrative), trust is built through joint committees (attitudinal), and each side convinces its own members (intra-organisational).

    Key points to remember: Elements = two parties, voluntary negotiation, subjects, agreement, good faith, industrial peace; theories = Webb (power contest), Dunlop (rule-making), Chamberlain (bargaining power), Walton & McKersie (distributive, integrative, attitudinal structuring, intra-organisational).

    Takeaway: Remember Walton-McKersie's four processes: distributive, integrative, attitudinal structuring and intra-organisational bargaining.

  • Q6. What are principled negotiations? Discuss various tactics and strategies in negotiation, citing suitable examples.

    Answer: Principled negotiation (Fisher and Ury) negotiates on merits - separate people from the problem, focus on interests not positions, generate options, and insist on objective criteria.

    Principled negotiation, developed by Roger Fisher and William Ury at Harvard (Getting to Yes, 1981), is a method of negotiating on merits instead of positions. Its four principles: separate the people from the problem - attack the issue, not the person, and manage emotions; focus on interests, not positions - ask why each side wants what it wants (positions are what they say, interests are what they really need); generate a variety of options before deciding - invent win-win possibilities; and insist on objective criteria - fairness standards like market rates, law, or expert opinion. For example, instead of fighting over a 10% versus 5% wage increase (positions), the parties discover the real interests - the union needs cost-of-living protection, management needs a stable wage bill - and agree on a productivity-linked pay scheme. Tactics in negotiation: anchoring (opening with an extreme but defensible demand, for example a seller quotes a high price first); BATNA (knowing your best alternative to a negotiated agreement - the party with a better BATNA has more power); concessions sequenced slowly; deadlines to create urgency; good cop/bad cop (one tough, one friendly negotiator); and information tactics like silence or controlled disclosure. Strategies are the overall plans: distributive or win-lose (claiming value), integrative or win-win (creating value), and principled (merits-based). Example: in a supplier contract, the buyer anchors low, the supplier reveals its cost structure, both use market rates as objective criteria, and the principled outcome splits the difference on quality-linked terms. Remember: tactics win the deal, but strategy and relationships win the long-term partnership.

    Key points to remember: Principled negotiation (Fisher-Ury) = separate people from problem, focus on interests not positions, generate options, use objective criteria; tactics = anchoring, BATNA, deadlines, good cop/bad cop; strategies = distributive (win-lose), integrative (win-win), principled (merits).

    Takeaway: Positions are what sides say; interests are what they really need - and the party with the better BATNA holds the power.

  • Q7. Define grievance. Explain various approaches to grievance resolution with the help of relevant examples.

    Answer: A grievance is any perceived dissatisfaction with job, conditions or treatment; the step-ladder procedure escalates it from the supervisor through HR and the grievance committee to binding arbitration.

    A grievance is any dissatisfaction or complaint that an employee feels about their job, working conditions, or treatment - whether justified or not. It is an employee's perception of injustice, such as unfair promotion, wrong pay calculation, or biased supervision. Because an unattended grievance grows into low morale, absenteeism, or even disputes, organisations build resolution machinery. The approaches to grievance resolution: the open-door policy - any employee can walk straight to top management with a complaint; it is simple but works only in small organisations and may bypass line managers. The step-ladder procedure - the grievance moves up levels: immediate supervisor, department head, HR, grievance committee, and finally arbitration; this is the most common formal approach, as in large factories. The grievance committee - a joint management-worker committee that hears grievances at unit level, recommended by the Indian Labour Conference. Counselling and informal handling - the supervisor or HR counsellor resolves the issue before it becomes formal. Arbitration - if internal steps fail, an agreed neutral arbitrator decides, and the decision is binding. Example: in a manufacturing plant, a worker aggrieved about incentive payment first speaks to the supervisor, then the plant HR, then the joint grievance committee; if unresolved, an arbitrator settles the matter - the classic step-ladder. The code of discipline (1958) in India also requires every industry to set up grievance procedures, and the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act mandates grievance officers for large establishments. The principle: settle grievances at the lowest possible level, fast and fairly.

    Key points to remember: Grievance = perceived dissatisfaction, justified or not; approaches = open-door, step-ladder procedure, joint grievance committee, counselling, arbitration; settle at the lowest level, quickly; Code of Discipline 1958 and standing orders mandate procedures.

    Takeaway: Settle grievances at the lowest possible level, fast and fairly - the Code of Discipline 1958 requires every industry to have a procedure.

  • Q8. What are the forms of industrial actions? Explain various types of settlement procedures of industrial disputes in India.

    Answer: Workers use strikes, tools-down, go-slow, work-to-rule, sit-ins and gherao while management answers with lockouts; the ID Act 1947 routes disputes through negotiation, conciliation, arbitration and adjudication.

    Industrial actions are the weapons workers and management use when a dispute breaks out. Workers' actions include: strike - collective stoppage of work; tools-down - workers report but stop production; go-slow - work is deliberately slowed; work-to-rule - workers follow rules so literally that output falls; sit-in or stay-in strike - workers occupy the workplace without working; and gherao - management is surrounded and confined until demands are met. Management's action is the lockout - closing the establishment to pressurise workers, and closure or lay-off in extreme cases. Since strikes and lockouts destroy output, India's Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 provides settlement procedures: collective bargaining first - the parties negotiate directly (bipartite); conciliation - a conciliation officer (government) or a board of conciliation mediates and tries to bring a settlement, and a conciliation settlement is binding if signed by both parties; arbitration - the parties voluntarily refer the dispute to an arbitrator whose award is binding; adjudication - if conciliation fails, the government refers the dispute to a Labour Court (for rights disputes like dismissal), an Industrial Tribunal (for wages and conditions), or a National Tribunal (for disputes affecting more than one state); and works committees - joint bodies that prevent disputes at the shop-floor stage. Example: in a textile mill dispute over bonus, the union and management first negotiate, the Labour Commissioner conciliates, and failing that the dispute goes to the Industrial Tribunal whose award decides the bonus. The system's aim is to replace direct confrontation with orderly, rule-based settlement.

    Key points to remember: Workers' actions = strike, tools-down, go-slow, work-to-rule, sit-in, gherao; management = lockout; settlement = negotiation, conciliation, arbitration, adjudication (Labour Court, Industrial Tribunal, National Tribunal), works committees; ID Act 1947 governs the machinery.

    Takeaway: Settlement ladder: bargaining, conciliation, then arbitration or adjudication by Labour Courts, Industrial Tribunals and National Tribunals.
