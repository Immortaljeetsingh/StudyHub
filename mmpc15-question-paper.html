Solved Answers - MMPC-15 Research Methodology

Q1. (a) Research, methods vs methodology and types of research  |  (b) Features of good research

Answer: Research is a purposeful, systematic investigation using scientific methods to find answers to questions. Research methodology is the overall systematic approach or philosophy of conducting research — the "how" — while research methods are the specific techniques and tools used for data collection and analysis. Research is not a "fishing expedition"; it must be focused and purposeful. Research can be classified in several ways. By application: pure (theoretical) research, which adds to knowledge, and applied research, which solves practical problems — for example, a company researching consumer preferences before a product launch. By objective: descriptive (describing what exists), correlational (finding relationships) and explanatory (explaining causes). By enquiry: qualitative (words and meanings) versus quantitative (numbers and statistics). By data source: primary (fresh collection) versus secondary (existing data). Business research reduces uncertainty in decision-making, identifies threats and opportunities, forecasts demand and provides competitive advantage. Good research is Systematic, Objective, Reproducible, Controlled and Relevant (the S-O-R-C memory aid) — it follows a structured plan, is unbiased, can be replicated by others, manages variables and addresses a meaningful problem.

Key points:

  • Methodology = the overall approach ("the how"); methods = the specific tools and techniques
  • By application: pure (adds knowledge) vs applied (solves practical problems)
  • By objective: descriptive, correlational, explanatory; by enquiry: qualitative vs quantitative
  • By data source: primary (fresh) vs secondary (existing)
  • Good research: Systematic, Objective, Reproducible, Controlled, Relevant (S-O-R-C)

Takeaway: Research is focused, systematic inquiry — knowing the type of research tells you how the study must be designed.

Q2. (a) Steps of the research process  |  (b) Hypothesis and errors

Answer: The research process follows a systematic sequence of seven steps: (1) Define the research problem — identify a clear, specific and researchable question; (2) Literature review — survey existing published work to find gaps, avoid duplication and build a foundation; (3) Develop a working hypothesis — formulate a testable, falsifiable statement; (4) Prepare the research design — the blueprint specifying methods and procedures; (5) Collect data — using appropriate primary or secondary techniques; (6) Analyse data — process, tabulate and apply statistical tools; and (7) Prepare the report — document findings in the academic format. A hypothesis is a testable statement about the relationship between variables. The null hypothesis (H0) states that there is no effect or no difference, while the alternative hypothesis (H1) states that an effect exists. Testing can lead to two mistakes: a Type I error rejects H0 when it is actually true (a false positive), and a Type II error fails to reject H0 when H1 is true (a false negative). Researchers control these errors by choosing appropriate significance levels and sample sizes — for example, a pharma firm testing a new drug uses a 5% significance level, accepting only a 5% chance of a Type I error.

Key points:

  • Seven steps: define problem → literature review → hypothesis → research design → collect data → analyse data → write report
  • H0 = no effect / no difference; H1 = an effect or difference exists
  • Type I error: rejecting a true H0 (false positive); controlled by the significance level α
  • Type II error: failing to reject a false H0 (false negative); controlled by sample size
  • Example: pharma trial at α = 0.05 accepts a 5% chance of a false "drug works" conclusion

Takeaway: The seven steps are a disciplined sequence — skip one and the whole study leaks.

Q3. (a) Research designs and validity  |  (b) Threats to internal validity

Answer: Research designs are master plans for conducting a study. Exploratory research is used when the problem is vague or unclear; it generates ideas and hypotheses through literature searches, expert surveys and focus groups. Descriptive research describes the characteristics of a population or phenomenon — for example, "60 per cent of customers prefer online shopping" — using surveys and case studies. Experimental research establishes cause and effect by manipulating an independent variable and measuring its effect on the dependent variable, for example testing whether a training programme improves sales, with a treatment group and a control group. Experiments can be laboratory (artificial, high internal validity) or field (natural, high external validity). Internal validity is the degree of confidence that the independent variable caused the observed change in the dependent variable; external validity is the degree to which results can be generalised to other settings and populations; construct validity asks whether we are measuring what we think we are measuring. The major threats to internal validity are: history (external events during the study), maturation (natural changes in subjects over time), testing (being tested affects performance), selection bias (non-random assignment of groups) and mortality (subjects dropping out of the study).

Key points:

  • Exploratory: vague problem → generate hypotheses (focus groups, literature, experts)
  • Descriptive: describes what exists (surveys, case studies); Experimental: proves cause-effect by manipulating the IV
  • Internal validity = the IV truly caused the change; external validity = results generalise
  • True experiments use R + control + X; quasi-experiments lack random assignment
  • Threats to internal validity: history, maturation, testing, selection, mortality

Takeaway: Exploratory → Descriptive → Experimental is a ladder of rigour — climb as high as the problem demands.

Q4. (a) Primary vs secondary data and collection methods  |  (b) Questionnaire designing

Answer: Primary data is collected fresh for the current study and is perfectly suited to the research objectives, but is costlier and more time-consuming — for example, surveys, interviews and experiments. Secondary data is already collected by someone else for a different purpose, such as census data, industry reports and company records; it is cheap and quick but may not fit the research question. The four main methods of primary data collection are: personal interview (highest flexibility, highest cost, highest response rate but slow); mailed questionnaire (low cost, lowest response rate, slowest); telephone interview (medium flexibility, fast); and observation (systematic watching and recording of behaviour without direct questioning). Focus groups — moderated discussions with 6-10 participants — explore attitudes in depth. A good questionnaire is designed around three aspects: general form (layout and length), question sequence (start with easy, non-threatening questions, then core questions, then sensitive questions, and demographics last) and question wording. Wording rules include: use simple, clear language; avoid leading questions ("Don't you think..."); avoid double-barrelled questions; avoid ambiguous terms; and make response options mutually exclusive and exhaustive. The questionnaire must be pre-tested on 10-20 people before full deployment — for example, a hospital tests its patient satisfaction survey before a full rollout.

Key points:

  • Primary: fresh data (personal interview, mailed questionnaire, telephone, observation); secondary: existing data (census, reports, records)
  • Interview = flexible but costly; mailed = cheap but low response; telephone = fast; observation = objective
  • Questionnaire design rests on three pillars: general form, question sequence, question wording
  • Sequence: easy → core → sensitive → demographics
  • Avoid leading, double-barrelled and ambiguous items; pre-test on 10-20 people before rollout

Takeaway: Match the collection method to your budget and depth needs — and never deploy an un-pre-tested questionnaire.

Q5. (a) Four levels of measurement scales  |  (b) Likert scale, reliability vs validity

Answer: Measurement scales are classified into four levels (N-O-I-R), from least to most information. The nominal scale uses categories with no order — gender, religion, city — analysed with mode and frequencies. The ordinal scale ranks items but with unequal intervals — rankings, satisfaction levels — analysed with median and percentiles. The interval scale has equal intervals but no true zero — temperature, Likert scores — permitting mean, standard deviation and correlation. The ratio scale has a true zero and equal intervals — income, weight, age — permitting all statistical operations. The Likert scale presents agree-disagree statements rated on 5 or 7 points (strongly agree to strongly disagree); it is easy to use and versatile, though it can suffer from central tendency bias, where respondents choose the middle option. Other attitude scales include the semantic differential (bipolar adjectives such as good-bad), Thurstone (equal-appearing intervals, rigorous but time-consuming) and Guttman (cumulative items). Reliability is consistency — whether the scale gives the same result repeatedly (test-retest, split-half, Cronbach's alpha); validity is accuracy — whether the scale measures what it claims to measure. A scale can be reliable but not valid — consistent but wrong — but it cannot be valid without being reliable.

Key points:

  • N-O-I-R: nominal (categories, mode) → ordinal (ranks, median) → interval (equal units, no true zero) → ratio (true zero, all statistics)
  • Likert: 5/7-point agree-disagree statements; risk of central tendency bias
  • Other attitude scales: semantic differential (bipolar adjectives), Thurstone (equal-appearing intervals), Guttman (cumulative)
  • Reliability = consistency (test-retest, split-half, Cronbach's alpha)
  • Validity = accuracy (measures what it claims); reliable ≠ valid, but valid requires reliable

Takeaway: Scale level decides the statistics you may run — N-O-I-R is the ladder of measurement power.

Q6. (a) Probability vs non-probability sampling  |  (b) Essentials and parts of a research report

Answer: Sampling selects a subset of the population to study instead of conducting a census of every element. Probability sampling gives every unit of the population a known, non-zero chance of selection. It includes simple random sampling (every unit has an equal chance, like a lottery), stratified sampling (population divided into homogeneous strata, with random samples from each — useful for a company surveying employees by department), cluster sampling (randomly selecting groups and studying all units within them, suited to geographically dispersed populations) and systematic sampling (every nth unit from a list). Probability designs allow estimation of sampling error, the difference between the sample statistic and the population parameter. Non-probability sampling does not give all units a known chance: convenience sampling takes whoever is available (fast and cheap, useful for pilot studies), judgment sampling relies on the researcher's expertise, quota sampling fixes the number of respondents from each category and is common in market research, and snowball sampling uses referrals to reach rare or hidden populations. A good research report is clear, complete, accurate and honest, written for the reader with a logical flow. Its main parts are: the preliminary pages (title, abstract, contents), the body (introduction, literature review, methodology, findings, analysis and interpretation), the reference section (bibliography, appendices) and a summary or conclusion with recommendations, followed by proper citation of all sources to avoid plagiarism.

Key points:

  • Probability (S-S-C-S): simple random, stratified, cluster, systematic — known chance, sampling error computable
  • Non-probability (C-J-Q-S): convenience, judgment, quota, snowball — fast and cheap but selection-biased
  • Sampling error = difference between sample statistic and population parameter (probability designs only)
  • Report parts: preliminary pages → body (intro, review, methodology, findings) → conclusions → references/appendices
  • Essentials of a good report: clear, complete, accurate, honest, reader-oriented, properly cited

Takeaway: Choose the sampling design first — it decides how far your findings can be generalised.

Q1. 20

"What do you mean by the term 'research'? What are the different types of research? Discuss the role of research in day-to-day business activities with suitable examples."

Answer: Research is a systematic, purposeful investigation using scientific methods to discover facts and solve problems; it spans many types and supports daily business decisions from pricing to product launches.

Key points:

  • Research = systematic, objective, controlled, reproducible inquiry — not a "fishing expedition"
  • Types by application: pure/theoretical (adds knowledge) vs applied (solves practical problems)
  • Types by objective: descriptive, correlational, explanatory (causal)
  • Types by enquiry: qualitative vs quantitative; by data source: primary vs secondary
  • Business role: demand forecasting, competitor analysis, consumer preference studies — e.g., an FMCG firm pre-tests a new soap variant before national launch

Takeaway: Research is evidence, not guesswork — every business decision from pricing to positioning can be research-driven.

Q2. 20

"What is research design? What are the different types of research designs? Discuss."

Answer: Research design is the master plan of a study — what, where, when, how much and how — and the three main families are exploratory, descriptive and experimental designs.

Key points:

  • Design answers: what is the study about, where, when, how much data, how will it be collected & analysed
  • Exploratory: vague problem → hypotheses (literature search, expert interviews, focus groups)
  • Descriptive: describes characteristics — surveys, case studies
  • Experimental: manipulates the independent variable, controls extraneous variables, measures effect on the dependent variable
  • Experimental families: pre-experimental, true experimental (randomisation + control group), quasi-experimental (no randomisation)
  • Choice depends on the research objective, accuracy needed, cost and time

Takeaway: Design follows purpose — explore when vague, describe when known, experiment to prove cause and effect.

Q3. 20

"What are the different types of attitude measurement scales? Compare and contrast the various attitude measurement techniques."

Answer: Attitude scales measure the direction and intensity of feelings through rating scales (Likert, semantic differential), ranking techniques (paired comparison, rank order) and cumulative scales (Thurstone, Guttman), each with different costs and precision.

Key points:

  • Likert: agree-disagree statements on 5/7 points — easy and popular, but central tendency bias
  • Semantic differential: bipolar adjective pairs (good-bad, modern-outdated) — measures connotative meaning
  • Thurstone equal-appearing interval: judges sort statements into 11 equal-interval piles — interval-level, rigorous but laborious
  • Guttman cumulative: agreement implies agreement with all lower items — one-dimensional
  • Ranking techniques: paired comparison, rank order, constant sum — force choices but give no intensity
  • Compare: Likert wins on practicality, Thurstone on precision; choice depends on time, respondents and analysis level

Takeaway: Choose the scale by the measurement level your analysis needs — ranking data cannot run means.

Q4. 20

"What is data processing in research? Describe various types of data classification."

Answer: Data processing converts raw responses into a clean, organised form for analysis through editing, coding, classification and tabulation, and classification groups data into homogeneous classes.

Key points:

  • Processing sequence: editing → coding → classification → tabulation → presentation
  • Classification = arranging data into groups sharing common characteristics
  • Types: chronological (time), geographical (place), qualitative/attribute, quantitative/magnitude
  • Frequency distributions: discrete (individual values) and continuous (class intervals)
  • Principles: exhaustiveness, mutual exclusivity, homogeneity, stability, clarity
  • Good classification compresses data without losing information

Takeaway: Classification is the bridge from raw responses to meaningful statistics — every item must fit exactly one clear class.

Q5. 20

"What kind of questions will arise while reviewing the draft report? Discuss."

Answer: Reviewing a draft report raises questions about purpose, reader, structure, factual accuracy, analysis and presentation — the report must say the right thing to the right person in the right way.

Key points:

  • Purpose: does it serve the research objectives? Is the focus clear and non-repetitive?
  • Reader: who reads it — does language, length and depth match them?
  • Structure: logical sequence — problem → methodology → findings → conclusions → recommendations
  • Facts: are data accurate, complete, correctly tabulated; tables/charts labelled and consistent?
  • Analysis: do conclusions follow from evidence? Are limitations acknowledged?
  • Clarity & ethics: simple unambiguous language, correct citations, no plagiarism

Takeaway: Review the draft from the reader's chair — accuracy, structure and clarity decide whether the report convinces.

Q6. Write short notes on any three of the following: 20

"(a) Quasi and True Experimental Designs · (b) The Q-Sort Technique · (c) Editing of Data · (d) The Mann-Whitney U-test · (e) Judgment Sampling" (all five solved — pick any three)

Answer: True experiments randomly assign subjects (R) to treatment/control groups and manipulate the treatment (X), while quasi-experiments lack random assignment; Q-sort forces respondents to sort statements into a fixed distribution; editing scrubs raw data; the Mann-Whitney U-test compares two independent groups on ranks; judgment sampling selects elements an expert considers representative.

Key points:

  • (a) True: R + X + control → high internal validity; Quasi: intact groups (before-after, time series) → selection/history threats — used where randomisation is impractical or unethical
  • (b) Q-sort (Stephenson): respondents sort statements into a forced distribution (e.g., 9 piles), then responses are correlated across persons — deep but time-consuming, small samples only
  • (c) Editing: field editing at collection point + central editing at the office; fix errors by recontact, discard unusable items, guard against disguised or partial answers
  • (d) Mann-Whitney U: rank all observations together, sum ranks per group, compute U — the rank-based alternative to the t-test for two independent samples
  • (e) Judgment (purposive) sampling: expert-selected elements — cheap and fast, good for pilots and expert panels, but no sampling error and prone to researcher bias

Takeaway: These five notes cover the exam's favourite short-note bank — learn the one-line definition of each plus its one strength and one weakness.

Q7. (Section B — compulsory) 20

"Following are the scores obtained by two groups of students in a competitive examination. Test whether the two groups belong to the same population:
Scores — Group A: 51, 68, 90, 81, 30, 46, 99, 98, 11, 06, 19, 43  |  Group B: 95, 82, 65, 85, 65, 81, 50, 60, 15, 05, 35, 32"

Answer: Using the Mann-Whitney U test, U = 70.5 with |z| = 0.09 < 1.96, so H0 is accepted — the two groups belong to the same population.

Key points:

Worked solution (Mann-Whitney U test):

1. Hypotheses: H0: the two groups come from the same population; H1: they come from different populations. α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

2. Data: n1 = n2 = 12 (as above).

3. Ranks: rank all 24 scores together (ties get the average rank). Sum of ranks: Group A: R1 = 148.5; Group B: R2 = 151.5. (Check: 148.5 + 151.5 = 300 = 24 × 25 / 2 √)

4. U statistics: U1 = n1n2 + n1(n1+1)/2 − R1 = 144 + 78 − 148.5 = 73.5; U2 = n1n2 + n2(n2+1)/2 − R2 = 144 + 78 − 151.5 = 70.5. Test statistic U = min(73.5, 70.5) = 70.5.

5. Normal approximation: μU = n1n2/2 = 72; σU = √(n1n2(n1+n2+1)/12) = √(12×12×25/12) = √300 = 17.32. z = (70.5 − 72)/17.32 = −0.09.

6. Decision: |z| = 0.09 < 1.96 → accept H0. (Cross-check: U = 70.5 > tabulated U 37 at 5% → same conclusion.)

Takeaway: Nearly equal rank sums mean nearly identical score patterns — the two groups behave like one population.

Q1. 20

"What do you mean by research process? Explain the steps in formulating a research problem."

Answer: The research process is the step-by-step sequence from defining a problem to writing the report, and formulating the problem is its first and most decisive stage.

Key points:

  • Research process: define problem → review literature → formulate hypothesis → research design → collect data → analyse → report
  • Formulating the problem: identify broad area → review literature to narrow it → consult experts → restate precisely as a question → define concepts → check feasibility (data, time, cost, ethics)
  • A good problem statement is specific, researchable, relevant and not already answered
  • Common errors: too broad a topic, ambiguity of terms, no clear objective

Takeaway: A well-stated problem is half the research done — the way you frame it decides the whole study.

Q2. 20

"What do you understand by the terms 'attitude' and 'attitude measurement'? Discuss."

Answer: Attitude is a learned predisposition to respond consistently favourably or unfavourably to an object, person or idea, and attitude measurement assigns numbers to that predisposition through rating, ranking and scaling techniques.

Key points:

  • Components: cognitive (beliefs), affective (feelings), conative (behavioural tendency)
  • Attitudes are latent — measured indirectly through responses to statements
  • Tools: Likert, semantic differential, Thurstone, Guttman, Q-sort, paired comparison
  • Issues: social desirability bias, central tendency, response sets, faking

Takeaway: Attitudes can't be seen, only inferred — every scale is a mirror carefully built to catch the respondent's mind.

Q3. 20

"What do you mean by sampling design? What points should be taken into consideration while developing the sampling design for studying the problems of domestic airline passengers? Discuss."

Answer: Sampling design is the plan for selecting a representative subset of the population; for studying airline passenger problems it must balance representativeness, budget and the passenger profile.

Key points:

  • Sampling design covers: population definition, sampling frame, sampling unit, selection method, sample size, budget
  • For airline passengers: define population (all domestic flyers), frame (booking/passenger lists), method — stratified by class and route, cluster by flight
  • Cover all flight classes (economy/business), routes and seasons (peak vs off-peak travel)
  • Fix sample size for the required precision; keep data collection at accessible points (terminals, web check-in)
  • Probability designs permit sampling error estimation; non-probability designs are faster probes

Takeaway: A good airline survey samples every class, route and season proportionately — not one terminal on one day.

Q4. 20

"Describe, in brief, the importance of editing, coding, classification, tabulation and presentation of data in the context of the research study."

Answer: These five operations convert raw responses into clean, analysable data — each step guards data quality and makes the study's evidence trustworthy.

Key points:

  • Editing: detects and corrects errors/omissions at the point of collection
  • Coding: assigns symbols/numbers to answers → data becomes machine-processable
  • Classification: groups similar items into homogeneous classes (time, place, attribute, magnitude)
  • Tabulation: arranges classified data into rows and columns → comparison at a glance
  • Presentation: tables, charts and figures communicate findings to the reader
  • A weak link anywhere (e.g., skipped editing) distorts the final conclusions

Takeaway: Editing → Coding → Classification → Tabulation → Presentation is an assembly line — a slip at the start cannot be repaired at the end.

Q5. 20

"What are the merits and demerits of different methods of collecting primary data? Explain."

Answer: Personal interview, mailed questionnaire, telephone and observation each trade off cost, quality, coverage and bias — the choice depends on the study's budget, respondents and depth needs.

Key points:

  • Personal interview: deep, flexible, high response — but costly, slow, interviewer bias
  • Mailed questionnaire: wide coverage, low cost, no interviewer bias — but low response, delay, no clarification
  • Telephone: fast, moderate cost — but limited to phone users, short interviews
  • Observation: objective, records actual behaviour — but only overt behaviour, costly, needs interpretation
  • Online surveys: cheap and quick — but coverage bias and low control

Takeaway: No method is best — match the method to budget, respondents and the depth of data required.

Q6. Write short notes on any three of the following: 20

"(a) Different types of scaling techniques in research · (b) Quota Sampling · (c) Coding of data · (d) Factor analysis · (e) Parts of a report" (all five solved — pick any three)

Answer: Scaling techniques assign positions to attitudes via rating, ranking and cumulative methods; quota sampling fixes respondent counts per category; coding turns answers into codes; factor analysis condenses many variables into few factors; a report has preliminary pages, a body and end matter.

Key points:

  • (a) Rating: Likert, semantic differential, graphic; Ranking: paired comparison, rank order, constant sum; Cumulative: Thurstone equal-appearing interval, Guttman

  • (e) Report parts: preliminary (title, abstract, contents) → body (introduction, review, methodology, findings, conclusions) → end matter (bibliography, appendices)

Takeaway: One definition + one merit + one demerit per note is the winning short-note formula.

Q7. (Section B — compulsory) 20

"The data given below is on a large industrial plant's daily emission of sulphur oxide (in tons):
17, 24, 19, 19, 15, 20, 23, 10, 20, 17, 28, 23, 29, 6, 19, 18, 19, 24, 16, 31, 18, 14, 22, 13, 22, 15, 24, 20, 25, 23, 17, 17, 27, 24, 20, 24, 9, 26, 13, 14
Use the one-sample sign test to test the null hypothesis that the plant's true average daily emission of sulphur oxide is μ = 23.5 tons against the alternative hypothesis μ < 23.5 tons at the 0.05 level of significance."

Answer: With 11 values above 23.5 and 29 below (n = 40), z = −2.69; since |z| = 2.69 > 1.645, H0 is rejected — the true average daily emission is significantly below 23.5 tons.

Key points:

Worked solution (one-sample sign test):

1. Hypotheses: H0: μ = 23.5 tons; H1: μ < 23.5 tons (one-tailed, α = 0.05).

2. Signs: count values above 23.5 (+) and below 23.5 (−): pluses = 11, minuses = 29, ties = 0. n = 40.

3. Expected: under H0, pluses ~ Binomial(40, 0.5) → expected pluses = 40 × 0.5 = 20.

4. Test statistic (with continuity correction): z = (11 + 0.5 − 20) / √(40 × 0.5 × 0.5) = −8.5 / 3.16 = −2.69. (Uncorrected: (11 − 20)/3.16 = −2.85 — same decision.)

5. Critical value: at 5% one-tailed, tabulated z = 1.645.

6. Decision: |z| = 2.69 > 1.645 → reject H0. Conclusion: the plant's true average daily emission of sulphur oxide is significantly less than 23.5 tons.

Takeaway: 29 of 40 days emit below 23.5 tons — far too many for chance — the plant is running cleaner than 23.5 tons on average.

Q1. 20

"What are the sources of a research topic? What do you consider in selecting a research problem? Discuss the steps in formulating a research problem."

Answer: Research topics come from theory, observation, literature, practical problems and expert suggestion; selection needs interest, feasibility and significance, and formulation follows a defined sequence ending in one sharp question.

Key points:

  • Sources: unresolved theoretical questions, gaps in previous research, day-to-day practical problems, expert discussions, case records, assigned topics
  • Selection criteria: interest, competence, relevance, data availability, feasibility (time, cost), ethics, significance
  • Formulation steps: broad area → literature review → narrow down → precise question → define concepts → check testability and feasibility
  • A fuzzy problem yields fuzzy research — clarity at this stage saves everything later

Takeaway: Choose a topic you can actually study (data + time + ethics), then shrink it to one sharp question.

Q2. 20

"What is secondary data? State their main sources and point out the dangers involved in their use and the precautions necessary to use them. Illustrate with examples."

Answer: Secondary data is data already collected by someone else for another purpose; it is cheap and fast but must be checked for relevance, accuracy and timeliness before use.

Key points:

  • Sources: government publications (census, RBI, CSO), journals, company records, annual reports, internet databases
  • Dangers: outdated data, different units or definitions, incomplete coverage, biased original collection, mismatch with current objectives
  • Precautions: check the purpose and method of original collection, verify units and time period, cross-check two sources, document the source
  • Example: using the 2011 census for today's city demographics misleads a store-location decision

Takeaway: Secondary data is borrowed evidence — inspect its provenance before you trust it.

Q3. 20

"What are the various non-probability sampling methods? Discuss their use in business and government."

Answer: Non-probability sampling selects elements by convenience, judgment, quota or referral without giving every unit a known chance — used widely in business and government for speed and economy.

Key points:

  • Convenience: mall intercepts, available respondents — cheapest, weakest
  • Judgment: expert-selected representative elements — good for panels and pilots
  • Quota: fixed counts per category (age, gender, region) — common in market research and polls
  • Snowball: referrals chain — for hidden populations (drug users, professionals)
  • Business use: concept tests, ad recall, store-exit satisfaction; Government use: quick administrative probes, citizens' feedback before full-scale surveys
  • Merits: cheap, fast, no sampling frame needed; Demerits: selection bias, unknown sampling error, weak generalisation

Takeaway: Non-probability sampling trades rigour for speed — right for pilots and probes, wrong for census-scale inference.

Q4. 20

"What is data processing in the research? What are the various types of data classification in the research? Explain the significance of data presentation in the research."

Answer: Data processing (editing → coding → classification → tabulation) prepares raw data for analysis, and presentation in tables and charts makes the processed data communicate its findings.

Answer: The questionnaire standardises data collection at low cost; field experiments run designs in natural settings; the semantic differential measures attitudes on bipolar adjective pairs; discriminant analysis classifies objects into known groups; a line/arithmetic chart plots trends on equal-interval axes.

Q7. (Section B — compulsory) 20

"A large industrial plant's daily emission of sulphur oxides (in tons) is given in the following table:
17, 15, 20, 29, 19, 18, 22, 25, 27, 9, 24, 20, 17, 6, 24, 14, 15, 23, 24, 26, 19, 23, 28, 19, 16, 22, 24, 17, 20, 13, 19, 10, 23, 18, 31, 13, 20, 17, 24, 14
Use the one-sample sign test to test the null hypothesis that the plant's true average daily emission of sulphur oxide is μ = 23.5 tons against the alternative hypothesis μ < 23.5 tons at the 0.05 level of significance. Tabulated value at 5% level of significance is 1.645."

Answer: With 11 values above 23.5 and 29 below (n = 40), z = −2.69; since |z| = 2.69 > 1.645, H0 is rejected — the true average daily emission is significantly below 23.5 tons.

Key points:

Worked solution (one-sample sign test):

1. Hypotheses: H0: μ = 23.5 tons; H1: μ < 23.5 tons (one-tailed, α = 0.05).

2. Signs: values above 23.5 (+): 11; below 23.5 (−): 29; ties: 0. n = 40.

3. Expected: under H0, pluses ~ Binomial(40, 0.5) → expected = 20.

4. Test statistic: z = (11 + 0.5 − 20) / √(40 × 0.5 × 0.5) = −8.5 / 3.16 = −2.69 (continuity-corrected; uncorrected −2.85 — same decision).

5. Decision: |z| = 2.69 > tabulated 1.645 → reject H0. Conclusion: true average daily emission is significantly less than 23.5 tons.

Takeaway: Same sign test structure as Dec 2023 — count signs, compute z with the continuity correction, compare with the printed tabulated value.

Q1. 20

"What do you mean by review of literature? What are the various types of literature reviews carried out by the researchers? Discuss the role of literature review in the research."

Answer: Literature review is a systematic critical survey of published and unpublished work on the topic that maps what is known, where the gaps are and how to build on them.

Key points:

  • Types: context/historical review, theoretical review, empirical review, methodological review, integrative review
  • Role: avoids duplicating work, sharpens the problem and hypotheses, provides the theoretical foundation, reveals methods and instruments, identifies research gaps
  • Good review is critical and thematic, not a list — current, comprehensive, properly cited
  • Prevents plagiarism by positioning the study inside the existing body of knowledge

Takeaway: Literature review is reconnaissance — you don't march into a field someone has already mapped.

Q2. 20

"What is a questionnaire? Why is a questionnaire used? What are the three main guiding considerations in the construction of the questionnaire? Explain with suitable examples."

Answer: A questionnaire is a formalised schedule of questions used to gather primary data; its construction rests on three guiding considerations — general form, question sequence and question wording.

Key points:

  • Why used: standardisation, cheap wide coverage, respondent convenience, statistical comparability
  • General form: structured vs unstructured; layout, length, clear instructions
  • Question sequence: easy, non-threatening first → core questions → sensitive questions → demographics last
  • Question wording: simple language, short items, no leading or double-barrelled or ambiguous questions; mutually exclusive and exhaustive options
  • Pre-test on 10-20 respondents before full use

Takeaway: Three knobs — form, sequence, wording — decide whether your questionnaire is a clean instrument or a muddle.

Q3. 20

"What are the various types of scales and scaling techniques? Discuss the Thurstone's equal-appearing interval scale."

Answer: Scaling techniques include rating, ranking and cumulative methods; Thurstone's scale builds an interval measure by having judges place statements in 11 equally spaced piles.

Key points:

  • Rating: Likert, semantic differential, graphic rating; Ranking: paired comparison, rank order, constant sum
  • Thurstone procedure: collect 100+ statements → 10-20 judges sort into 11 piles (least to most favourable) → scale value of each statement = median pile position
  • Select 20-25 statements spaced evenly across the continuum; respondents tick agree/disagree; score = mean scale value of agreed statements
  • Merits: interval-level measurement, balanced coverage; Demerits: judge-dependent, laborious, time-consuming
  • Guttman cumulative scale: items of increasing intensity; agreement implies agreement with all easier items

Takeaway: Thurstone turns attitudes into interval numbers — elegant, but you pay for it in judge time.

Q4. 20

"Distinguish between parametric and non-parametric tests. What is the importance of non-parametric tests? Discuss."

Answer: Parametric tests assume known distributions (mainly normal) and need interval/ratio data, while non-parametric tests are distribution-free and work on ranks, signs and categories.

Key points:

  • Parametric: t-test, ANOVA, Pearson r, z-test — powerful when assumptions hold
  • Non-parametric: chi-square, Mann-Whitney U, sign test, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, runs test
  • Importance of non-parametric: no normality assumption, usable with ordinal and nominal data, robust to outliers, works on small and skewed samples
  • Cost: less powerful — ranks discard information; conclusions are cruder
  • Rule of thumb: unknown distribution + small n + ordinal data → non-parametric

Takeaway: Non-parametric tests are the safe fallback when data can't meet normality — you lose power, not validity.

Q5. 20

"What do you mean by ethics in research? Discuss the principles of research ethics."

Answer: Research ethics are the moral principles governing the conduct of research — honesty, informed consent, confidentiality, no harm and objectivity from problem choice to publication.

Key points:

  • Informed consent: respondents volunteer with full knowledge of the study's purpose and use
  • Confidentiality and anonymity: protect identity and data
  • No harm: physical, psychological and social safety of participants
  • Honesty and objectivity: no fabrication, no manipulation of results — researcher as judge, not pleader
  • Proper citation, no plagiarism, disclosure of funding/conflicts, ethical review by boards

Takeaway: Ethics is not paperwork — one fabricated dataset can betray every respondent and end a career.

Q6. Write short notes on any three of the following: 20

"(a) Multidimensional Scaling · (b) Internal validity and external validity · (c) Observation method · (d) Quota Sampling · (e) Coding of data" (all five solved — pick any three)

Answer: MDS maps perceived similarities into a spatial perceptual map; internal validity is causal confidence while external validity is generalisability; observation records behaviour directly; quota sampling fixes respondent counts per category; coding converts answers into analysable codes.

Key points:

  • (a) MDS: similarity ratings among all brand pairs → perceptual map in 2-3 dimensions; uses: positioning, competitor analysis, new-product gaps; dimension interpretation is subjective
  • (b) Internal: the IV truly caused the change (threats: history, maturation, testing, selection); External: results generalise to other people/settings/times; strict control raises internal but lowers external
  • (c) Observation: participant/non-participant, structured/unstructured, direct/indirect; objective and catches real behaviour — but only overt acts, observer bias, cannot see motives; retail traffic counts, safety studies
  • (d) Quota: fixed counts per category, convenient selection within — fast, controls mix, interviewer bias, no sampling error
  • (e) Coding: pre-coding (closed questions) or post-coding (open-ended); codebook = analysis dictionary; exhaustive, mutually exclusive codes

Takeaway: Two of these five (quota, coding) repeat from Dec 2023 — the short-note pool is small; master it once.

Q7. (Section B — compulsory) 20

"The arithmetic addition scores for 27 individuals are given in the following table belonging to two groups. Use Chi-square test to test the hypothesis of no difference between these two sets of scores at 5 per cent level of significance:
Group-I: 12, 16, 18, 7, 6, 4, 11, 12, 8, 20, 18, 16, 10, 4  |  Group-II: 7, 12, 14, 18, 5, 16, 9, 10, 14, 3, 18, 9, 7
Given χ²-tabulated value at 1 degree of freedom at 5 per cent is 6.63."

Answer: Using the median test, χ² = 0.04; since 0.04 < 3.84 (and even < the paper's printed 6.63), H0 is accepted — there is no significant difference between the two sets of scores.

Key points:

Worked solution (chi-square median test):

1. Hypotheses: H0: no difference between the two sets of scores; H1: there is a difference. α = 0.05.

2. Combined median: sort all 27 values together; the 14th value (middle of 27) = 11. Median = 11.

3. Contingency table (counts above median / at or below median): Group I: 7 / 7 (n = 14); Group II: 6 / 7 (n = 13). Column totals: 13 / 14; grand total 27.

4. Expected frequencies = row total × column total ÷ 27: 14×13/27 = 6.74; 14×14/27 = 7.26; 13×13/27 = 6.26; 13×14/27 = 6.74.

5. Test statistic: χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E = (7−6.74)²/6.74 + (7−7.26)²/7.26 + (6−6.26)²/6.26 + (7−6.74)²/6.74 = 0.010 + 0.009 + 0.011 + 0.010 = 0.04.

6. Decision: df = (2−1)(2−1) = 1. Tabulated χ² at 1 df, 5% = 3.84 (the paper prints 6.63, which is actually the 1% value — either way the decision is unchanged). 0.04 < 3.84 → accept H0: no significant difference between the two groups' arithmetic addition scores.

Takeaway: The median split makes the two groups look alike (7/7 vs 6/7) — a tiny chi-square means no real difference.

Q1. 20

"What is research methodology? Give a brief account of mathematical tools for the analysis in research methodology."

Answer: Research methodology is the systematic logic and philosophy of research from problem to conclusion, and mathematical tools — descriptive statistics, probability, tests of significance, correlation and regression — power its analysis stage.

Key points:

  • Methodology = the overall approach; methods = the specific techniques used within it
  • Descriptive tools: mean, median, mode, standard deviation, variance, skewness, kurtosis
  • Inferential tools: normal distribution, z, t, F, chi-square; correlation (Pearson/Spearman) and regression
  • Multivariate tools: factor analysis, discriminant analysis, cluster analysis, MDS
  • Match tool to data level: nominal → chi-square; ordinal → non-parametric; interval/ratio → parametric

Takeaway: Methodology sets the logic; mathematics does the measuring — always match the tool to the data level.

Q2. 20

"'A research scholar has to work as a judge and derive the truth and not as a pleader who is only eager to prove his case in favour of his plaintiff.' Discuss the statement, pointing out the objectives of the research."

Answer: The statement means the researcher must derive truth from evidence impartially, not argue a predetermined case — and the objectives of research (description, explanation, prediction, control, theory building) are served only by that neutrality.

Key points:

  • Researcher as judge: weigh all evidence and report what the data show, even against expectations
  • Researcher as pleader: confirmation bias — selecting data that fit, ignoring the rest (the danger)
  • Objectives of research: gain familiarity (exploratory), describe accurately, determine frequencies, test hypotheses, explain causes, predict and control outcomes
  • Safeguards: honest reporting, no cherry-picking, negative results disclosed, objectivity throughout

Takeaway: In research you decide with evidence and argue with data — advocacy is for courtrooms, not conclusions.

Q3. 20

"Explain random sampling and outline the different methods of random sampling."

Answer: Random (probability) sampling gives every element a known non-zero chance of selection, eliminating selection bias and permitting sampling error estimation; its methods are simple random, stratified, systematic and cluster sampling.

Key points:

  • Simple random: lottery or random number table — equal chance for every element; needs a complete list
  • Systematic: every kth element (k = N/n) from a random start
  • Stratified: homogeneous strata first, random sample within each (by income, region, class)
  • Cluster: natural groups selected at random; all or a sample of members studied
  • Merits: unbiased estimates, computable error, generalisable; Demerits: frames needed, costly, time-consuming

Takeaway: Random sampling is the gold standard because bias is excluded by design — and error becomes measurable.

Q4. 20

"'A questionnaire is the most popular method of data collection.' Do you agree? Justify your answer and explain the guidelines for using this method."

Answer: Yes — the questionnaire is the most popular method because it standardises questions, covers wide samples cheaply and suits literate respondents; its success depends on following guidelines of form, sequence and wording.

Key points:

  • Popularity: uniform stimulus, low cost, large coverage, anonymity, easy tabulation
  • Limitations: low response rate, no probing, superficial answers, misreading
  • Guidelines: clear objectives first; simple wording; no leading, double-barrelled or ambiguous items; logical sequence (easy → core → sensitive → demographics); exhaustive and exclusive options
  • Pre-test on 10-20 people; cover letter and anonymity assurance raise response
  • Use mixed question types — closed questions with open-ended follow-ups

Takeaway: The questionnaire is the most popular — and the most abused; guidelines are what make it scientific.

Q6. Write shot notes on any three of the following: 20

"(a) Pilot study · (b) Non-random sampling · (c) Questionnaire method · (d) Secondary data · (e) Research design" (all five solved — pick any three)

Answer: A pilot study is a small trial run of the instrument; non-random sampling selects without known chances; the questionnaire method standardises data collection; secondary data is pre-existing data; research design is the study's master plan.
