Q1. Explain the accounting concepts and conventions followed in the preparation of financial statements.

Answer: Accounting concepts are the fundamental assumptions on which financial statements are prepared. The core concepts include the Business Entity concept, which treats the business as separate from its owners; Going Concern, which assumes the business will continue indefinitely; Money Measurement, which records only monetary transactions; Periodicity, which divides the life of the business into accounting periods; and Accrual, under which revenue earned and expenses incurred are recorded rather than cash received or paid. Conventions are the practices that guide the application of these concepts. Full Disclosure requires that all material information be disclosed in the financial statements. Consistency requires the same accounting methods to be followed across periods so that results are comparable. Prudence, also called Conservatism, requires that no profits be anticipated but all likely losses be provided for. Materiality requires that only significant items be separately disclosed. Together, concepts and conventions ensure that financial statements are reliable, comparable, and understandable. For example, Tata Motors follows Ind AS while preparing its consolidated financial statements, and the business entity concept requires treating the parent and its subsidiary Jaguar Land Rover as separate entities that are later consolidated for group-level reporting.

Q2. What is a Cash Flow Statement? Distinguish between operating, investing, and financing activities.

Answer: The Cash Flow Statement shows the actual inflows and outflows of cash during a period, classified into three activities. Operating activities relate to the core business, such as receipts from customers and payments to suppliers and staff, and also include taxes and interest paid. Investing activities involve the purchase or sale of fixed assets, loans given to others, investment in subsidiaries, and interest or dividends received. Financing activities cover the issue of shares and debentures, repayment of borrowings, dividends paid, and buy-back of shares. The distinction matters because a company can be profitable yet cash-poor: profit is an accrual-based figure while the cash flow statement reveals real cash generation. Under the indirect method, preparation starts with net profit and then adjusts for non-cash items such as depreciation and provisions, and for changes in working capital. For instance, depreciation is added back since it is a non-cash expense, an increase in debtors is deducted because cash has not yet been received, and an increase in creditors is added because cash has not yet been paid. Operating cash flow is regarded as the true indicator of business health; ICICI Bank's large operating cash flow in FY2024 reflects its healthy core banking operations.

Key points:

  • The Cash Flow Statement shows the actual inflows and outflows of cash during a period, classified into three activities.
  • Operating activities relate to the core business, such as receipts from customers and payments to suppliers and staff, and also include taxes and interest paid.
  • Investing activities involve the purchase or sale of fixed assets, loans given to others, investment in subsidiaries, and interest or dividends received.

Q3. Explain break-even analysis. Define contribution and margin of safety with their significance.

Answer: Break-even analysis is a cost-volume-profit technique that determines the level of sales at which total revenue equals total cost and profit is zero. The break-even point is calculated as fixed costs divided by contribution per unit. Contribution is the difference between sales and variable costs; it represents the amount that each unit of sale contributes towards recovering fixed costs and then generating profit. Margin of safety is the excess of actual or budgeted sales over break-even sales, and it indicates the cushion available before the firm starts incurring losses. A high margin of safety means the firm can absorb a fall in sales without making losses. These concepts guide several managerial decisions. Pricing decisions rely on contribution: a product is worth selling as long as its price covers variable costs and contributes to fixed costs. Capacity and production decisions use the break-even point to assess the viability of expansion, and the assumptions of the analysis must be noted, namely linear cost behaviour, constant selling price, and production equal to sales. CVP analysis thus acts as a manager's compass for pricing, capacity, and profitability decisions, and a manager should know the break-even point before launching any product.

Key points:

  • Break-even analysis is a cost-volume-profit technique that determines the level of sales at which total revenue equals total cost and profit is zero.
  • The break-even point is calculated as fixed costs divided by contribution per unit.
  • CVP analysis thus acts as a manager's compass for pricing, capacity, and profitability decisions, and a manager should know the break-even point before launching any product.

Q4. What is ratio analysis? Explain the four categories of ratios and the DuPont analysis of Return on Equity.

Answer: Ratio analysis is the systematic use of ratios to interpret the relationship between items in financial statements, helping analysts evaluate a company's performance and financial position. Ratios are classified into four categories. Liquidity ratios, such as the current ratio and quick ratio, measure the ability to meet short-term obligations. Profitability ratios, such as net profit margin and return on capital employed, measure earning efficiency. Solvency ratios, such as debt-equity ratio and interest coverage, measure the ability to meet long-term obligations. Efficiency or turnover ratios, such as inventory turnover and asset turnover, measure how effectively assets are used. The DuPont analysis decomposes Return on Equity into three components: net profit margin multiplied by asset turnover multiplied by the equity multiplier. This shows whether ROE is driven by profitability, by efficient use of assets, or by financial leverage. Ratios must be interpreted with industry context because there are no universal good values. Their limitations include being historical, being affected by window dressing, and being distorted by inflation. Ratio analysis is therefore diagnostic rather than prescriptive: it tells the analyst where to investigate deeper rather than what to conclude.

Key points:

  • Ratio analysis is the systematic use of ratios to interpret the relationship between items in financial statements, helping analysts evaluate a company's performance and financial position.
  • Liquidity ratios, such as the current ratio and quick ratio, measure the ability to meet short-term obligations.
  • Ratios are classified into four categories.
