Q1. Discuss the five functions of management. Why is management called a continuous process?

Answer: Management performs five core functions: Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing, and Controlling (P-O-S-D-C). Planning is the first and most fundamental function; it bridges the gap between where an organization is and where it wants to be through objectives, premises, and action plans. Organizing creates the structure of authority and responsibility, groups activities, and allocates resources. Staffing ensures the right people occupy the right roles through job analysis, recruitment, selection, training, and appraisal. Directing guides people through leadership, communication, motivation, and supervision. Controlling sets standards, measures actual performance, compares it with standards, and takes corrective action. The process is continuous because controlling feeds feedback into planning, so the cycle restarts with better information. Management is never a one-time activity; it is dynamic, universal, and integrated, harmonizing human, material, financial, and informational resources. Gulick and Urwick extended the five functions into POSDCORB by adding coordinating, reporting, and budgeting. An effective manager needs both efficiency (doing things right) and effectiveness (doing the right things). The Tata Group illustrates all five functions, from strategic planning to the Tata Business Excellence Model for controlling.

Key points:

  • Management performs five core functions: Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing, and Controlling (P-O-S-D-C).
  • The Tata Group illustrates all five functions, from strategic planning to the Tata Business Excellence Model for controlling.
  • Planning is the first and most fundamental function; it bridges the gap between where an organization is and where it wants to be through objectives, premises, and action plans.

Q2. Explain the contributions of F.W. Taylor and Henri Fayol to management thought. How do they differ?

Answer: Taylor, the Father of Scientific Management (1911), focused on shop-floor efficiency. He used time and motion studies to find the "one best way" of doing each job and laid down four principles: develop a science for each element of work, scientifically select and train workers, cooperate with workers, and divide work equally between management and workers. He addressed the "soldiering" problem, in which workers deliberately worked below capacity. Fayol, a French mining executive (1916), developed the Administrative Theory, defining management as planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, and controlling, and proposed 14 universal principles including division of work, unity of command, scalar chain, and esprit de corps. The two differ in perspective: Taylor worked bottom-up from the shop floor, while Fayol worked top-down from the whole organization. Taylor emphasized worker-level productivity, while Fayol focused on the management process itself. Taylor's approach was criticized for treating workers as "economic men", while Fayol's principles were seen as rigid and ignoring the environment. In India, Maruti Suzuki's assembly lines apply Taylor's scientific methods, while corporate hierarchies such as L&T and Infosys follow Fayol's principles.

Key points:

  • Taylor, the Father of Scientific Management (1911), focused on shop-floor efficiency.
  • In India, Maruti Suzuki's assembly lines apply Taylor's scientific methods, while corporate hierarchies such as L&T and Infosys follow Fayol's principles.
  • He used time and motion studies to find the "one best way" of doing each job and laid down four principles: develop a science for each element of work, scientifically select and train workers, cooperate with workers, and divide work equally between management and workers.

Takeaway: In India, Maruti Suzuki's assembly lines apply Taylor's scientific methods, while corporate hierarchies such as L&T and Infosys follow Fayol's principles.

Q3. Explain Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs. How can a manager apply it to motivate employees?

Answer: Maslow proposed five levels of needs arranged in a hierarchy, satisfied from bottom to top: physiological needs (salary, food, shelter), safety needs (job security, health insurance, provident fund), social needs (belonging, team culture, mentorship), esteem needs (recognition, promotions, awards), and self-actualization (creative projects, leadership roles). A need stops motivating once satisfied, and lower needs dominate until they are met. Managers should identify the need level each employee is at and design motivators accordingly; you cannot motivate someone with recognition (esteem) if they are worried about job security (safety). Infosys applies the full hierarchy with competitive salaries, job security, team outings, promotions, and innovation labs for self-actualization. Marico combines Maslow with McClelland's needs: employees take initiative (achievement), receive stock options (affiliation), and celebrate wins publicly (esteem). Herzberg's two-factor theory complements this, telling managers to fix hygiene factors such as pay and conditions first, then add motivators like recognition. The memory aid is "Please Stop — Let's Eat Samosas" (Physiological, Safety, Love, Esteem, Self-actualization).

Key points:

  • Maslow proposed five levels of needs arranged in a hierarchy, satisfied from bottom to top: physiological needs (salary, food, shelter), safety needs (job security, health insurance, provident fund), social needs (belonging, team culture, mentorship), esteem needs (recognition, promotions, awards), and self-actualization (creative projects, leadership roles).
  • Infosys applies the full hierarchy with competitive salaries, job security, team outings, promotions, and innovation labs for self-actualization.

Q4. Differentiate between delegation and decentralization with suitable examples.

Answer: Delegation is the process by which a manager assigns authority and responsibility to a subordinate for specific tasks while retaining accountability, which can never be delegated. Its three elements are authority, responsibility, and accountability, and its scope is limited to particular tasks. Decentralization is the systematic dispersal of decision-making authority to lower levels throughout the organization; it is a philosophy or structural choice that results from extensive delegation. Delegation is an individual, task-level technique, whereas decentralization is an organization-wide arrangement. Decentralization leads to faster decisions, better local responsiveness, and development of managerial talent at lower levels, but may create inconsistency. Zomato illustrates delegation: founder Deepinder Goyal hands authority and KPIs to vertical heads (delivery, dining, Hyperpure, Blinkit), who remain accountable for their units. Godrej Group exemplifies decentralization, with each business unit operating semi-autonomously. At the other extreme, Indian Railways is highly centralized, with major decisions flowing from Rail Bhavan, and early Reliance concentrated authority in one person. The balance chosen depends on the company's size, industry, and leadership style.

  • Delegation is the process by which a manager assigns authority and responsibility to a subordinate for specific tasks while retaining accountability, which can never be delegated.
  • At the other extreme, Indian Railways is highly centralized, with major decisions flowing from Rail Bhavan, and early Reliance concentrated authority in one person.
