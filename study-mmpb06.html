Block-01: Ethics & Governance Foundations

Unit 1: Business Ethics

📖 Definition

Business Ethics = moral principles guiding business behavior. Three ethical theories: Utilitarianism (greatest good), Deontology (duty-based), Virtue Ethics (character-based).

Code of Ethics = formal document outlining ethical standards. Whistleblowing = reporting unethical/illegal activities. Corporate Governance = system of rules by which companies are directed and controlled.

Ethical Theories Explained

TheoryProponentCore PrincipleBusiness Application
UtilitarianismJeremy Bentham, John Stuart MillGreatest good for greatest numberCost-benefit analysis, stakeholder impact assessment
DeontologyImmanuel KantDuty-based, rules must be followed regardless of outcomeHonest advertising, fair treatment, contract compliance
Virtue EthicsAristotleCharacter-based, what would a virtuous person do?Building ethical culture, leadership by example

Ethical Decision-Making Process

  1. Identify the ethical issue
  2. Gather all relevant facts
  3. Evaluate alternatives using ethical theories
  4. Choose the best course of action
  5. Implement and review the decision

Stakeholder Analysis

Every business decision affects multiple stakeholders. Ethical managers consider the impact on all parties:

  • Shareholders — expect returns on investment
  • Employees — expect fair wages, safe working conditions
  • Customers — expect quality products, fair pricing
  • Suppliers — expect timely payments, fair terms
  • Community — expects environmental responsibility, job creation
  • Government — expects tax compliance, legal adherence

Unit 2: Corporate Governance — Concepts and Theories

📖 Definition

Agency Theory = separation of ownership (shareholders) and control (management). Agency Costs = Monitoring + Bonding + Residual Loss. Stakeholder Theory = consider ALL stakeholders, not just shareholders.

Agency Problem = managers may act in self-interest rather than shareholders' interest. Stewardship Theory = managers act as responsible stewards of the organization. Board of Directors = provides oversight, strategy direction, and accountability.

Agency Costs Explained

  • Monitoring Costs: Expenses shareholders incur to supervise management behavior (audit fees, board meetings, reporting requirements)
  • Bonding Costs: Expenses management incurs to assure shareholders they're acting in their interest (voluntary disclosures, governance codes)
  • Residual Loss: Despite monitoring and bonding, some inefficiency remains due to information asymmetry

Unit 3: Institutional Mechanism

📖 Definition

Institutional Investors = mutual funds, pension funds, insurance companies. Proxy Voting = shareholders vote without attending meetings. Executive Compensation = salary, bonuses, stock options.

Shareholder Activism = shareholders use their equity stake to influence corporate governance and decision-making. ESOP = Employee Stock Ownership Plan - aligns employee and shareholder interests.

Types of Institutional Investors

TypeCharacteristicsRole in Governance
Mutual FundsPool money from retail investorsVote on resolutions, engage with management
Pension FundsLong-term investors, stable capitalFocus on long-term value, ESG integration
Insurance CompaniesLarge asset base, conservativeRisk oversight, governance standards
Sovereign Wealth FundsGovernment-owned, very largeStrategic investments, national interest

Proxy Voting Mechanism

  • Process: Shareholders receive notice of AGM/EGM, vote on resolutions
  • Methods: Postal ballot, e-voting, voting at meeting
  • Key Resolutions: Board appointments, executive compensation, mergers, related party transactions
  • Institutional Role: Large investors exercise significant voting power

Executive Compensation Best Practices

  • Link pay to performance (both short-term and long-term)
  • Clawback provisions for restatements
  • Say-on-pay votes for shareholders
  • Disclosure of pay ratios (CEO vs median employee)
  • Independent compensation committee

Governance Codes

  • SOX Act (2002): US — response to Enron/WorldCom scandals. Key provisions: CEO/CFO certification of financial statements, auditor independence, internal controls assessment, whistleblower protection
  • Companies Act 2013: India — enhanced governance requirements. Key provisions: Independent directors, audit committee, CSR mandate (Section 135), women director requirement
  • SEBI Regulations: India — listed company governance. Key provisions: Corporate governance code for listed companies, related party transactions, disclosure requirements

Unit 4: Code of Conduct

📖 Definition

Code of Conduct = rules guiding employee behavior. Components: Integrity, Confidentiality, Conflict of Interest, Compliance. CSR = business responsibility to society.

Disclosure and Transparency = timely, accurate information to stakeholders about company performance, risks, and governance practices.

Code of Conduct Components

  1. Integrity and Honesty: Acting ethically in all business dealings
  2. Confidentiality: Protecting sensitive company and customer information
  3. Conflict of Interest: Avoiding situations where personal interests conflict with company interests
  4. Compliance: Adhering to all applicable laws, regulations, and company policies
  5. Reporting Mechanisms: Channels for reporting violations (hotlines, whistleblower policies)

Compliance Mechanisms

  • Regular training programs on ethics and compliance
  • Anonymous reporting channels (ethics hotlines)
  • Monitoring and auditing of compliance
  • Enforcement through disciplinary actions
  • Periodic review and update of the code

Block-02: Basel Framework

Unit 5: Basel Framework

📖 Definition

Basel Committee (BCBS) = global banking regulator. Basel I (1988) = 8% CAR. Basel II (2004) = three pillars. Basel III (2010) = strengthened post-2008 crisis.

Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) = ratio of bank's capital to its risk-weighted assets, ensuring it can absorb losses. Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA) = bank assets weighted according to credit risk.

Basel II Three Pillars Explained

PillarFocusKey Requirements
Pillar 1: Minimum CapitalCredit, Market, Operational RiskCAR = (Tier 1 + Tier 2) / RWA >= 8%
Pillar 2: Supervisory ReviewInternal capital assessmentBanks assess own capital needs, regulatory review
Pillar 3: Market DisciplineDisclosure requirementsPublic disclosure of risk profile, capital adequacy

Basel III Enhancements

CAR Formula with Example

CAR = (Tier 1 Capital + Tier 2 Capital) / Risk-Weighted Assets >= 8% Example: Bank has Tier 1 = Rs 80,000 crore, Tier 2 = Rs 20,000 crore, RWA = Rs 10,00,000 crore CAR = (80,000 + 20,000) / 10,00,000 = 10% Bank meets Basel III minimum (8% + 2.5% buffer = 10.5%) if it has additional buffer

Capital Tiers

  • Tier 1 (Core Capital): Equity capital, retained earnings (highest quality, absorbs losses immediately)
  • Tier 2 (Supplementary Capital): Revaluation reserves, subordinated debt, hybrid instruments

Unit 7: Disclosure and Transparency in Banking

📖 Definition

NPA (Non-Performing Asset) = loan overdue 90+ days. CRILC = RBI's database for borrowers with exposure >= Rs 5 crore. Asset Classification: Standard, Sub-standard, Doubtful, Loss.

Pillar 3 Disclosure = banks must publicly disclose risk profile, capital adequacy, risk management practices. NPA Recognition = based on 90-day overdue norm (used by RBI, while international norm is 90 days).

Unit 8: Global Perspective on Banking Governance

📖 Definition

2008 Financial Crisis exposed governance failures. G-SIBs = Globally Systemically Important Banks. Ring-fencing = separating retail from investment banking.

Too Big to Fail (TBTF) = concept that certain large, interconnected financial institutions cannot be allowed to fail due to systemic risk. Moral Hazard = banks take excessive risks knowing they'll be bailed out.

▶
Corporate Governance

Causes of 2008 Financial Crisis

  • Subprime mortgages: Loans to borrowers with poor credit history
  • Securitization: Packaging risky loans into complex derivatives (CDOs, MBS)
  • Rating agency failures: AAA ratings given to risky securities
  • Leverage: Banks holding too little capital relative to assets
  • Governance failures: Boards not understanding risks, excessive executive compensation

Post-Crisis Reforms

ReformPurposeImplementation
Higher CapitalMore loss-absorbing capacityBasel III: CET1 4.5%, Tier 1 6%
Stress TestingTest resilience under adverse scenariosCCAR (US), EBA (Europe)
Living WillsPlans for orderly failureRecovery and Resolution Plans
Ring-fencingSeparate retail from investment bankingUK Vickers Commission
CompensationLimit excessive risk-takingBonus caps, clawback provisions
Resolution FrameworksOrderly wind-down of failing banksFDIC (US), SRB (EU)

G-SIBs (Globally Systemically Important Banks)

  • Additional capital buffer: 1-3.5% of RWA based on systemic importance
  • Higher loss-absorbing capacity requirements
  • Enhanced supervision and disclosure
  • Examples: JP Morgan, HSBC, Deutsche Bank, ICBC
💡 Real-World Example

After 2008, Lehman Brothers collapsed (no ring-fencing), while JP Morgan survived but required $25B government bailout (too big to fail).

🧠 Memory Aid

"S-L-R-S" for post-crisis reforms: Stress testing, Living wills, Ring-fencing, Strict capital

✅ Unit Summary

  • 2008 crisis exposed governance failures globally
  • G-SIBs: additional capital buffer 1-3.5% of RWA
  • "Too big to fail" = systemic risk from large interconnected banks

Unit 10: Corporate Governance in Mutual Funds

📖 Definition

Mutual Fund = pool of money managed by AMC. Structure: Sponsor > Trust > AMC > Fund. NAV = (Total Assets - Liabilities) / Units. SEBI (Mutual Fund) Regulations, 1996.

Sponsor = entity that establishes the mutual fund. Trustee = holds assets for benefit of unit holders. AMC = Asset Management Company that manages investments.

▶
Corporate Governance

TrustHolds assetsLegal ownership of fund assets
TrusteeOversightEnsure AMC acts in investor interest
AMCManages investmentsInvestment decisions, operations

MF Categories (SEBI Classification)

  • Equity Funds: Invest 65%+ in stocks (higher risk, higher return)
  • Debt Funds: Invest in bonds, government securities (lower risk)
  • Hybrid Funds: Mix of equity and debt (balanced risk)
  • Index Funds: Track a market index (Nifty, Sensex)
  • ELSS: Equity Linked Savings Scheme (tax saving under 80C)

NAV Formula

NAV = (Total Assets - Total Liabilities) / Total Outstanding Units Example: Fund has Rs 1000 crore assets, Rs 50 crore liabilities, 50 crore units NAV = (1000 - 50) / 50 = Rs 19 per unit
💡 Real-World Example

Model Answer Structure: Basel Framework (10 marks)

  1. Introduction (2 marks): Define Basel Committee (BCBS), purpose (global banking stability), evolution (I, II, III)
  2. Basel II Three Pillars (3 marks): Pillar 1 (minimum capital - CAR formula), Pillar 2 (supervisory review), Pillar 3 (market discipline)
  3. Basel III Enhancements (3 marks): Higher capital (CET1 4.5%, Tier 1 6%), buffers (conservation 2.5%, countercyclical 0-2.5%), leverage ratio (3%), liquidity (LCR, NSFR)
  4. Indian Context (1 mark): RBI implementation, Indian banks' CAR (13-14%), impact on lending
  5. Conclusion (1 mark): Basel framework ensures banking stability, India compliant

Model Answer Structure: Agency Theory (10 marks)

  1. Introduction (2 marks): Define agency theory (Jensen & Meckling 1976), principal-agent separation
  2. Agency Problem (2 marks): Information asymmetry, moral hazard, adverse selection
  3. Agency Costs (2 marks): Monitoring costs, bonding costs, residual loss
  4. Solutions (2 marks): Board oversight, executive compensation alignment, governance codes
  5. Indian Examples (1 mark): Satyam scam, Infosys governance, HDFC Bank
  6. Conclusion (1 mark): Good governance reduces agency costs, increases firm value

📋 Key Formulas & Numbers

  • Basel III: CET1 = 4.5%, Tier 1 = 6%, Total = 8% + 2.5% buffer
  • NPA: overdue 90+ days
  • CSR: 2% of average net profit (3 years)

Quick Revision: One-Liner Concepts

  • Agency Theory: Ownership vs control separation creates agency costs
  • Stakeholder Theory: Balance all stakeholders, not just shareholders (Freeman)
  • Basel I: 8% CAR, credit risk only
  • Basel II: Three pillars - minimum capital, supervisory review, market discipline
  • Basel III: Higher capital, liquidity requirements, leverage ratio
  • CAR: Capital Adequacy Ratio = (Tier 1 + Tier 2) / RWA
  • Tier 1: Core capital - equity, retained earnings
  • Tier 2: Supplementary - revaluation reserves, subordinated debt
  • NPA: Non-Performing Asset = loan overdue 90+ days
  • Standard Asset: Regular payments, 0-10% provisioning
  • Sub-standard: 90 days - 12 months overdue, 15-25% provisioning
  • Doubtful: Sub-standard for 12+ months, 25-100% provisioning
  • Loss Asset: Identified as uncollectible, 100% provisioning
  • CRILC: RBI database for borrowers with exposure >= Rs 5 crore
  • SARFAESI: Banks seize collateral without court intervention
  • IBC: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code - 180+90 days resolution
  • G-SIBs: Globally Systemically Important Banks - additional 1-3.5% buffer
  • Ring-fencing: Separating retail from investment banking
  • Living Wills: Plans for orderly failure of banks
  • NBFC: Cannot accept demand deposits, no DICGC coverage
  • NAV: (Assets - Liabilities) / Units (Mutual Funds)
  • Solvency Margin: 150% minimum (Insurance)
  • Financial Inclusion: Jan Dhan (50+ crore accounts), UPI (10B+ transactions/month)
  • CSR: Section 135 - 2% of average net profit, threshold 500/1000/5 Cr
  • ESG: Environmental, Social, Governance factors
  • AML: KYC, CTR (> Rs 10 lakh), STR
  • SOX Act: US response to Enron/WorldCom (2002)
  • Companies Act 2013: India - independent directors, audit committee, CSR mandate
