Q1. (a) Selling vs Marketing  (b) Elements of the marketing mix

Answer: selling pushes products after production; marketing satisfies customer needs before, during and after production — and the 4Ps (Product, Price, Place, Promotion) are its toolbox.

Key points:

(a) Selling vs Marketing: selling is a narrow, transaction-focused activity; marketing is a broad, customer-focused philosophy.

  • Orientation: selling is inward-looking (“we must sell what we make”); marketing is outward-looking (“we make what customers need”).
  • Focus: selling focuses on the product and pushing it; marketing focuses on customer needs and wants.
  • Means: selling uses aggressive promotion and persuasion (like insurance agents, LIC); marketing uses integrated decisions on product, price, place and promotion.
  • End: selling aims at immediate profit through sales volume; marketing aims at long-term customer satisfaction and loyalty (Flipkart’s customer-first approach).
  • Scope: selling begins after production; marketing begins before production, with market research, and continues after the sale with after-sales service.

(b) Marketing mix (4Ps): the marketing mix is the set of controllable tools the firm blends to satisfy its target market.

  • Product: features, quality, branding, packaging — e.g., Amul’s butter, cheese and ice cream range.
  • Price: pricing strategy, discounts, credit terms — e.g., Amul keeps butter at ₹50/100g for affordability.
  • Place: distribution channels, coverage, logistics — e.g., Coca-Cola is available in 25 lakh+ outlets across India.
  • Promotion: advertising, PR, personal selling, sales promotion — e.g., Surf Excel’s “Daag Acche Hain” campaign. For emerging markets, Jagdish Sheth’s 4As (Attraction, Affordability, Accessibility, Awareness) make the mix more relevant.
Marketing Mix — The 4Ps of Product, Price, Place, Promotion

Takeaway: selling pushes products, marketing satisfies customers; marketing starts before production; 4Ps = Product, Price, Place, Promotion; 4As for India; use one Indian example per P.

Q2. (a) Segmentation bases for E-vehicles and Smartphones  (b) Organisational buying process

Answer: segmentation splits the market into groups sharing characteristics (geographic, demographic, psychographic, behavioural); organisational buying is a formal, multi-stakeholder 7-step process from need recognition to performance review.

Key points:

(a) Segmentation bases: segmentation divides the market into distinct groups with shared characteristics, using geographic, demographic, psychographic and behavioural bases.

  • (i) E-vehicle market: geographic (cities with charging infrastructure, like Delhi, Bengaluru, Mumbai); demographic (income ₹10 lakh+, age 25-50, two-car households); psychographic (eco-conscious, tech-early adopters, status seekers); behavioural (high daily commuters who value low running cost). Example: Tata Motors positions Nexon EV for urban commuters and MG Comet for young city couples.
  • (ii) Smartphone market: demographic (income, age, student vs professional); psychographic (gaming enthusiasts, camera lovers, status-conscious); behavioural (heavy app users, brand loyalists, upgrade cycle); geographic (urban vs rural — rural users want long battery life). Example: Samsung’s S-series targets premium users while Galaxy M-series targets budget-conscious online buyers.

(b) Organisational buying process: business buying is rational and involves multiple stakeholders. The process has seven stages: (1) problem/need recognition; (2) general need description (specifications); (3) product specification; (4) supplier search; (5) proposal solicitation (quotations); (6) supplier selection and order; (7) performance review. Buying situations are straight rebuy, modified rebuy and new task. Example: an auto manufacturer like Tata Motors sourcing steel follows this full process through its buying centre.

Takeaway: four bases — geographic, demographic, psychographic, behavioural (MASA criteria for effective segments); E-vehicle → charging infrastructure + income; smartphone → income + usage; organisational buying = 7 stages ending with performance review.

Q3. (a) Brand strategies with advantages and disadvantages  (b) Product life cycle and pricing

Answer: a firm chooses among branded house, house of brands, endorsed and freestanding strategies — each trading brand-equity leverage against risk and cost; pricing must follow the PLC stage (skim or penetrate → compete → defend → harvest).

Key points:

(a) Brand strategies available to a firm:

  • Branded house (one master brand): e.g., Tata for Tata Motors, Tata Steel, Tata Tea. Advantage: strong brand equity transfers to every product. Disadvantage: one failure can damage the whole family.
  • House of brands (separate brands per product): e.g., HUL’s Surf, Dove, Lux, Pepsodent. Advantage: risk isolation, each brand targets its own segment. Disadvantage: expensive — every new brand needs separate promotion investment.
  • Endorsed brands (sub-brand with parent’s backing): e.g., Aashirvaad by ITC, Bingo by ITC. Advantage: parent credibility + brand independence. Disadvantage: too much endorsement can confuse the sub-brand’s identity.
  • Freestanding brands (no parent link): e.g., P&G’s Whisper. Advantage: complete freedom in positioning. Disadvantage: no equity transfer, built from zero.

(b) How product life cycle stages affect pricing: Introduction — choose skimming (high price, recover R&D — Samsung S-series at ₹70,000+) or penetration (low price for quick share — Jio’s free data); Growth — competition arrives, prices hold or ease slightly while share is maximised; Maturity — intense competition, price cuts, discounts and promotional pricing to defend share (smartphone market); Decline — harvest pricing, cut price to clear stock or divest (feature phones).

Product Life Cycle (PLC) — Sales and Profit curves
BCG Matrix — portfolio position guides brand investment

Takeaway: 4 strategies — branded house, house of brands, endorsed, freestanding; price follows the PLC — skim or penetrate at introduction, compete at growth, defend at maturity, harvest at decline; Jio = penetration, Samsung = skimming.

Q4. (a) Retailing in the digital age  (b) Product modification for rural markets

Answer: digital retail has made shopping boundaryless — omnichannel, quick commerce and social commerce now coexist with the kirana; rural markets demand product modification for affordability (sachets), durability (sturdy packs) and local relevance (vernacular labels).

Key points:

(a) The retailing landscape has changed drastically with the digital age. E-commerce grew from nothing to a ₹5+ lakh crore market; customers now shop on apps, compare prices instantly, and expect home delivery. Key changes: omnichannel retail (Lenskart combines online try-on with 2,000+ physical stores); quick commerce (Zepto delivers groceries in 10 minutes from dark stores); social commerce (Meesho turns housewives into resellers on WhatsApp); click & collect (JioMart + Reliance Retail); and webrooming — research online, buy in store (78% of Indian consumers). Digital payment (UPI), reviews on Zomato and Google, and targeted ads (CRED’s viral digital campaigns) have made the store “boundaryless”. Traditional kiranas survive by joining digital platforms, proving the landscape is digital-first, not digital-only.

(b) Product modification strategies for rural markets: rural buyers have lower incomes, erratic power, poor roads and high price sensitivity, so companies modify products for affordability and durability: smaller pack sizes (shampoo sachets at ₹1-2, Parle-G at ₹5); lower price points and value pricing (Amul’s affordable butter); sturdier packaging and longer shelf life (heat-resistant, moisture-proof packs); simplified features (basic mobiles with long battery life); local language labelling and regional flavours; and smaller, repairable units. HUL’s Project Shakti and Coca-Cola’s cold-chain-ready packs bring these modifications to 6 lakh+ villages. The 4As framework — Attraction, Affordability, Accessibility, Awareness — guides all rural product decisions.

Takeaway: digital retail = omnichannel + quick commerce + social commerce; rural modification = sachets, lower prices, durable packaging, local language; use 4As for rural markets; examples: sachet shampoo, Parle-G, Lenskart, Meesho, Zepto.

Q5. Physical evidence, service personnel and customer influence in a Bank and a Restaurant (Compulsory)

Answer: services are intangible, so tangible cues (physical evidence), the people delivering the service, and the visible influence of one customer on another determine perceived quality — in both a bank and a restaurant.

Key points:

Physical evidence is the tangible part of a service that customers can see, touch and judge before and during the service. It is crucial because services are intangible.

  • Bank: branch layout and interiors, ATM machines, cheque books, passbooks, signage, the mobile app interface, uniforms of staff, and the overall cleanliness. A neat, secure branch signals trust — a shabby one signals risk.
  • Restaurant: ambience, decor, lighting, furniture, table setting, menu design, plating of food, uniforms, music and hygiene of the kitchen area (visible kitchens). Zomato-style photos of dishes are also physical evidence that drives ordering.

Role of service personnel: because services are produced and consumed at the same time (inseparability), the employee is the service. Bank tellers and relationship managers must be courteous, knowledgeable and quick; restaurant waiters must be attentive, accurate and friendly. Poor behaviour by one cashier or waiter can destroy the customer’s whole experience, so training and empowerment of frontline staff are essential (RATER: reliability, assurance, tangibles, empathy, responsiveness).

How customers influence other customers: customers are visible to each other in service settings — a long queue signals popularity (social proof) but slow service; a busy restaurant attracts more customers; satisfied customers create word-of-mouth and post reviews on Google and Zomato, which drive new customers; dissatisfied customers write negative reviews that spread fast. Banks and restaurants must therefore manage the queue, encourage reviews and handle complaints quickly, because every customer is both a user and a marketer.

Takeaway: physical evidence = tangible cues (bank: branch, app, passbook; restaurant: ambience, plating, menu); service personnel = the service itself; customers influence customers through queues, word-of-mouth and online reviews; IIVP + RATER frame the answer.

Q1. (a) “Marketing involves much more than selling and advertising.” Discuss. How would you apply your knowledge of marketing concept to promote (i) Safe Driving Habits (ii) Save Water?  (b) Discuss the main objectives of sales promotion. Explain some of the sales promotion directed at consumers, which can be used by a soap manufacturer.

Answer: marketing is a customer-first value process, while selling and advertising are only two promotion tools — the same marketing concept can also be applied to change social behaviour (safe driving, saving water) and to plan sales promotions.

Key points:

  • Selling & advertising are subsets: they persuade after production; marketing starts with research on needs before production and continues after the sale with service and feedback.
  • Marketing concept: satisfy customer needs profitably through the 4Ps — product, price, place, promotion — coordinated, not just promoted.
  • (i) Safe driving habits: product = safer cars and roads; price = lower insurance premiums for accident-free years; place = campaigns in schools, RTOs, offices; promotion = fear-and-emotion ads (“Buckle Up”, Ministry of Road Transport films, celebrity drivers).
  • (ii) Save water: sell the “benefit” of water security; price = conservation-linked water tariffs; place = village-level jal committees and schools; promotion = “Catch the Rain”-style campaigns, social media challenges, vernacular radio.
  • Social marketing: the product here is a behaviour change — same concept, different deliverable.
  • Sales promotion objectives: induce trial, attract brand switchers, reward loyal buyers, clear stocks, push off-season and festival sales, support dealers and amplify advertising.
  • Consumer promotions for a soap manufacturer: free samples (Cinthol-style), price-off packs, buy-one-get-one, extra-quantity packs (“25% extra”), gift-with-pack (soap + towel), scratch cards and lucky draws, redemption coupons, sachets for trial.

Takeaway: marketing ≠ selling — it is needs → value → satisfaction; the same 4P thinking sells goods, habits and causes; sales promotion = short-term tools (samples, price-offs, BOGO, draws) used sparingly.

Q2. (a) What are the steps in the consumer decision-making process? Do all consumers decisions involve these steps? Discuss with an example.  (b) What is the concept of holistic marketing? Explain each of the components and their importance in the exchange process.

Answer: the consumer decision process has five steps — need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase, post-purchase behaviour — but low-involvement and routine buys skip the middle steps; holistic marketing (Kotler) aligns relationship, integrated, internal and socially responsible marketing into one system.

Key points:

  • Need recognition: trigger — hunger, an ad, a friend’s phone.
  • Information search: internal (memory) and external (friends, internet, reviews, showroom visits).
  • Evaluation of alternatives: compare price, features, brand, after-sales (compensatory vs non-compensatory rules).
  • Purchase decision: brand + place + payment choice; can be overridden by price or social influence.
  • Post-purchase behaviour: satisfaction builds loyalty; cognitive dissonance (doubt) follows high-value purchases.
  • Do all decisions follow? No — routine (re-buying the same soap) and impulse (chocolate at the counter) skip search and evaluation; buying a car runs all five steps fully.
  • Holistic marketing components: relationship marketing (loyalty & lifetime value — Flipkart Plus), integrated marketing (one consistent voice across all 4Ps — Amul), internal marketing (every employee a marketer — Taj Hotels), socially responsible marketing (ethics and society — Tata CSR) — each component deepens trust and reduces the cost of every exchange.

Takeaway: five steps is the full model, involvement decides how many are used; holistic marketing = relationships + integration + internal alignment + social responsibility as one engine of exchange.

Q3. (a) What is Product Positioning? Discuss the need and its importance. Explain the tasks that marketers should consider for successful positioning.  (b) Explain briefly the stages through which you would test ideas coming up for new products until the final stage of launching the new product.

Answer: positioning is designing the offer and its image to occupy a distinctive place in the customer’s mind; new products pass through eight gates from idea generation to commercial launch.

Key points:

  • Positioning defined: Kotler — “the act of designing the company’s offering and image to occupy a distinctive place in the target market’s mind”; it answers “why should I buy you?”.
  • Need & importance: buyers judge on perception, not features; a clear position differentiates from rivals (Nexon EV = safe urban EV, Thar = adventure SUV, WagonR = tall space + value).
  • Tasks for success: define the target segment; map competitors on a perceptual map; choose the value proposition (points of parity and difference); write a positioning statement; deliver it through every P; monitor and re-position when the market shifts.
  • New product stages: idea generation → screening → concept development & testing → business analysis → product development & prototype → test marketing → commercialisation.
  • Gate logic: each stage filters risk — weak ideas die at screening, unprofitable concepts at business analysis, weak reactions at test marketing, before full launch money is spent.

Takeaway: position is perception — find the gap, claim it credibly, reinforce through the 4Ps; new products move from idea to launch through gates that kill bad bets early.

Q4. Write short notes on any three of the following: (a) Industrial goods classification (b) Cost-based pricing (c) Measuring advertising effectiveness (d) Internet vs. Digital marketing (e) Challenges in rural marketing.

Answer: industrial goods fall into three buckets — materials & parts, capital items, supplies & services; cost-based pricing adds a margin to cost; advertising effectiveness is measured on communication effect and sales effect; internet marketing is a subset of digital marketing; rural markets strain every element of the mix.

Key points:

  • Industrial goods classification: materials & parts (raw materials like cotton and steel, manufactured materials, component parts like tyres) go into the product; capital items (plants, machinery, equipment) are long-life investments; supplies and business services (lubricants, stationery, couriers, consultancy) support operations — each needs a different selling process.
  • Cost-based pricing: cost-plus (total cost + fixed %), mark-up (retailer adds standard %, kirana ~10-15%), target-return (price set for a desired ROI); advantages — simple and defensible; disadvantage — ignores demand, competition and perceived value.
  • Measuring advertising effectiveness: pre-testing (concept, copy, portfolio tests); post-testing of communication (unaided/aided recall, recognition like Starch, attitude shifts, enquiry tests); post-testing of sales (matched-market experiments, share analysis, spend-vs-sales); digital metrics (impressions, CTR, conversion, ROAS).
  • Internet vs digital marketing: digital marketing is the umbrella (internet + mobile apps, SMS, smart TV, digital outdoor); internet marketing is only online channels (website, search, email, e-commerce); plan digital, execute internet + non-internet digital together.
  • Challenges in rural marketing: scattered low-income buyers, poor roads and erratic power, low literacy (visual messages needed), 6 lakh+ villages with tiny shops, weak media reach — met with hub models (HUL Project Shakti, ITC e-Choupal), sachet pricing and haat/mela touchpoints.

Takeaway: classify industrial goods by use; cost-plus is simple but blind; measure ads before and after (“did they remember it, did it sell”); internet ⊂ digital; rural success = 4As engineered, not assumed.

The company has added a new item in its product category and wishes to brand the new item. (a) Suggest a suitable brand name and propose promotion mix for the brand which is targetted for rural markets. Key points:

  • Brand name criteria: short, pronounceable, meaningful in local languages, taste-suggestive, no negative meaning in any regional tongue, memorable — “Mithaas” (sweetness) tested with rural consumers.
  • Promotion - awareness: regional TV (DD regional channels) and radio with local-celebrity, visual-led ads for low-literacy audiences.
  • Promotion - trial: free tasting and sampling at haats and melas, ₹1-2 trial packs, lucky-draw on pack.

Marketing mix cycle — brand and pack decisions must serve rural customer needs

December 2022 - Term-End Examination (MMPC-006)

Q1. (a) Define the following terms with an example: (i) Market (ii) Marketing (iii) Transactional Marketing (iv) Relationship Marketing.  (b) What is organizational buying behavior? Discuss the steps involved in organizational buying process.

Answer: a market is the set of actual and potential buyers; marketing is managing profitable exchange by creating, communicating and delivering value; transactional marketing focuses on a single exchange while relationship marketing builds long-term bonds; organisational buying is a formal multi-stakeholder process in seven steps.

Key points:

  • Market: Kotler — “all the actual and potential buyers of a product or service” (e.g., the Indian smartphone market).
  • Marketing: AMA — creating, communicating and delivering value (Amul’s full product-to-promotion engine).
  • Transactional marketing: one-time exchange, product-centred, little post-sale contact (a single saree purchase at a local shop).
  • Relationship marketing: customer-centred, loyalty and repeat business (Jio plan renewals, Amazon Prime).
  • Organisational buying behavior: the process firms use to identify, evaluate and choose suppliers — rational, high-value, handled by a buying centre (users, influencers, buyers, deciders, gatekeepers).
  • Seven steps: need recognition → general need description → product specification → supplier search → proposal solicitation → supplier selection and order → performance review; situations: straight rebuy, modified rebuy, new task.

Takeaway: market = buyers; marketing = value process; transaction = one deal, relationship = lifetime value; org buying = 7-step formal process run by a buying centre.

Q2. (a) Why is an understanding of product levels important? With suitable examples, discuss the five product levels that you are familiar with.  (b) What is a product life cycle? Discuss the four stages of product life cycle with reference to any motorcycle brand of your choice.

Answer: a product is a bundle of benefits built on five levels — core, generic, expected, augmented, potential; the PLC tracks sales and profit through introduction, growth, maturity and decline, each needing a different strategy (e.g., Hero Splendor).

Key points:

  • Core benefit: the real need satisfied (a hotel stay = rest and sleep).
  • Generic product: the basic version (a room with a bed).
  • Expected product: attributes buyers normally expect (clean room, AC, WiFi).
  • Augmented product: beyond expectations (free breakfast, spa, loyalty points) — the level where brands compete.
  • Potential product: future transformation (AI-personalised smart rooms).
  • PLC stages with a motorcycle brand: Introduction (awareness, losses — early electric two-wheelers); Growth (rapid sales, rising profits, new rivals); Maturity (peak sales, fierce competition — Hero Splendor defends with mileage, variants and service network); Decline (falling sales — original Bajaj Chetak was retired, then relaunched as electric).
Product Life Cycle (PLC) — sales and profit over four stages

Takeaway: compete at the augmented level; read the PLC stage and change strategy — Splendor defends maturity, Chetak shows decline can become relaunch.

Q3. (a) What constitute distribution in marketing? How does distribution add value to the marketing effort? Explain with an example.  (b) What is Digital Marketing? Discuss any four types of digital marketing that you are familiar with.

Answer: distribution is the channels, logistics, storage and delivery that make products available — it adds time, place and possession utility; digital marketing promotes through electronic media in four main forms — social media, search, content and email/mobile.

Key points:

  • What distribution includes: channel structure (producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer), transportation, warehousing, inventory, credit, and market information flows.
  • Value added: time utility (available when wanted), place utility (where wanted), possession utility (ownership transferred easily) — Coca-Cola’s 25 lakh+ outlets make a cold drink available at a village shop in summer heat; Amul’s cold chain delivers fresh milk daily.
  • Digital marketing: promotion through internet and electronic devices — measurable and targeted.
  • Social media marketing: Facebook/Instagram/YouTube engagement and ads (Zomato’s witty Twitter).
  • Search engine marketing: SEO for organic ranking + SEM/Google Ads for paid visibility (Flipkart bidding on “buy phone online”).
  • Content marketing: blogs, videos, webinars that attract customers (boAt’s influencer content, CRED’s web series).
  • Email & mobile marketing: newsletters, SMS, WhatsApp campaigns, push notifications.

Takeaway: distribution creates the three utilities of time, place and possession; digital marketing = social + search + content + email/mobile, all measurable.

Q4. Write short notes on any three of the following: (a) Segmentation in Industrial Markets (b) Functions of Packaging (c) Steps in Integrated Marketing Communication Process (d) Types of direct Channels of Distribution (e) Pull marketing approach of Digital Marketing.

Answer: industrial markets segment on macro bases (industry, size, location) and micro bases (buying behaviour, criteria); packaging protects, contains, informs and sells; the IMC process runs from audience to measurement; direct channels bypass intermediaries; digital pull marketing earns attention instead of buying it.

Key points:

  • Segmentation in industrial markets: macro — industry (steel, pharma), company size, region, end-use; micro — purchasing structure, buying criteria (price vs quality vs JIT), buying situation, buyer traits; example: a bearings maker targets Pune-Chennai auto OEMs, then JIT-focused buyers among them.
  • Functions of packaging: containment and protection (milk pouches), identification and information (FSSAI labels, MRP), promotion and differentiation (“silent salesman” — Dairy Milk purple), convenience (resealable, portion packs), logistics economy (light stackable PET over glass).
  • Steps in IMC process: identify target audience → set communication objectives (awareness → conviction → action) → design the message → select media → fix budget and tool blend → implement and measure.
  • Types of direct channels: level-0 (no intermediaries): company-owned stores (Bata, Apple), direct selling and party plans (Amway, Tupperware), D2C e-commerce (boAt, Mamaearth), telemarketing and mail order.
  • Pull marketing approach of digital marketing: SEO, content, YouTube reviews and social content make customers come and search for the brand; advantages — cheaper, qualified, trust-building, compounding; limitation — slow build-up and skill-dependent.

Takeaway: industrial segmentation = who the firm is + how it buys; packaging is the silent salesman; IMC = audience → message → media → measure; pull beats push on cost and quality of leads.

Q5 (Section B - Compulsory). Case: India’s famed and ace investor has announced his plans to enter the aviation sector with his low-cost airline tentatively named as Vikasa. The promoter plans flights across India with an “endeavor to be the nation’s most dependable, affordable and greenest airline”, operating ~70 planes in four years. The ultra-low-cost carrier (ULCC) model aligns with the government’s UDAN scheme (‘Ude Desh ka Aam Naagrik’). Assume you have been retained as a consultant. Questions: (a) Suggest the bases for segmentation, its evaluation and selection of market segment in line with Governments ambitious UDAN scheme. (b) For a new venture of this kind, what combination of Integrated Marketing Communications tools would you recommend and why?

Answer: segment flyers by geography (UDAN tier-2/3 routes), purpose (business, leisure, VFR), price sensitivity and behaviour; evaluate with MASA and select the underserved mass segment — first-time flyers; promote with advertising + PR + digital + sales promotion + direct marketing under one “affordable flying” message.

Key points:

  • Geographic: metros for trunk routes, tier-2/3 UDAN-connected towns for spokes.
  • Demographic: income ₹3-15 lakh, first-time flyers who currently travel by train/bus.
  • Psychographic & behavioural: value seekers, price-sensitive bookers, occasional vs frequent flyers, booking lead time.
  • Evaluation (MASA): measurable (route and traffic data), accessible (booking and distribution reachable), substantial (route density), actionable (fleet and ULCC cost structure); check profitability and fit with UDAN viability-gap funding.
  • Selection: ultra-low-cost mass market on UDAN routes — no-frills service, secondary airports, high frequency.
  • IMC tools - awareness: launch TV + print + airport outdoor advertising (IndiGo’s “ab har aam aadmi udega” precedent).
  • IMC tools - credibility, trial, loyalty: PR and launch-event coverage tied to the UDAN story; digital search and social campaigns for bookings; introductory fare sales and card cashbacks for trial; app pushes, email and a loyalty programme for repeat flying — all carrying one consistent “dependable, affordable, green” message.
Segmentation matrix - bases for dividing any market (airline: routes, purpose, price sensitivity)

Takeaway: airline STP = UDAN geography + purpose + price sensitivity, evaluated on MASA; launch mix = advertising + PR + digital + fares + loyalty under one message.

June 2023 - Term-End Examination (MMPC-006)

Q1. (a) Explain the following terms with an example: (i) Needs (ii) Concept of Segment (iii) Integrated Marketing (iv) Performance Marketing.  (b) Why is it necessary for a firm to monitor the micro and macro environmental factors to keep pace with changing times? Substantiate.

Answer: needs are basic deprivations (hunger), wants are culture-shaped, and segments are groups with shared needs; integrated marketing aligns every P and tool into one voice, performance marketing pays only for measurable outcomes — and firms must scan micro and macro environments to catch opportunities and threats early.

Key points:

  • Needs: basic human requirements (hunger → food); wants are needs shaped by culture (pizza vs dal-roti); demands are wants plus purchasing power.
  • Concept of segment: dividing a market into homogeneous groups — the same bike brand sells a premium segment and a commuter segment.
  • Integrated marketing: all 4Ps and all communication tools work in sync (Amul’s single “Taste of India” message across every medium).
  • Performance marketing: pay-for-outcome models — CPC/CPA ads, affiliate links, growth-hacking campaigns with measurable ROI (CRED’s viral digital campaigns).
  • Micro environment: customers’ shifting tastes, competitor actions, supplier costs and channel behaviour hit the firm daily.
  • Macro environment (PESTLE): GST policy, inflation, health-consciousness, UPI/5G technology, sustainability norms — a firm that scans early moves first (Jio sensed the data boom; Kodak missed digital).
  • Substantiation: COVID forced restaurants to delivery-only; firms already scanning (Swiggy, Zomato) pivoted in weeks — use SWOT and PESTLE continuously, not once.

Takeaway: needs are universal, segments are targetable groups; integration unifies the mix, performance marketing pays for results; scan micro daily and macro strategically.

Q2. (a) What constitutes a consumer product? Explain the bases of classifying consumer products with suitable examples.  (b) Explain the concept of PLC.   • Product differentiation: features (iPhone camera), quality and durability (Titan), service (Amazon Prime), people (Taj), image (Royal Enfield) — the basis of positioning and premium pricing.
  • Measuring communication effectiveness: pre-tests (concept, copy) and post-tests — recall and recognition (Starch), attitude shifts, enquiry tests, matched-market sales experiments, and digital metrics (impressions, CTR, conversion, ROAS).
  • Handling objections in selling: listen and acknowledge, answer with evidence, “feel-felt-found”, boomerang (turn the objection into a reason), compensation (admit the weakness, offset with strengths), then confirm and close.
  • 4Ps of green marketing: green product (eco materials, Tata Nexon EV), green price (premium justified, FAME subsidies), green place (carbon-efficient logistics, reverse logistics), green promotion (truthful eco-claims, certifications — no greenwashing).

Takeaway: 4As = the buyer’s 4Ps; differentiate on what customers value; measure both memory and sales; treat objections as buying signals; green the whole mix honestly.

Q5 (Section B - Compulsory). Case: Lifeline is a fast growing kitchen appliances premium brand offering a wide range of appliances, now poised to offer its products as corporate gifting for employees especially during the festive season, with the baseline “Smart Gifts for Smart Kitchens”, wishing to consolidate the market as a long-term strategy. Questions: (a) Suggest marketing mix elements emphasizing on pricing, promotion and distribution aspects. (b) The packaging decisions that you would consider and why?

Answer: position Lifeline as the premium corporate-gifting choice — premium pricing with bulk-order logic, B2B promotion built on personal selling and festive campaigns, and direct + portal distribution with gift service; packaging must be premium, protective, personalised and ready-to-gift.

  • Packaging - protection: sturdy cushioned boxes for appliances in transit.
  • Packaging - premium and personalised: branded boxes with metallic finish and premium unboxing (the box is the first impression); sleeve/insert with the gifting company’s logo and festive message; ready-to-gift with carry handles and gift wrap; recyclable materials to reinforce the premium modern brand story.

June 2025 - Term-End Examination (MMPC-006)

Q1. (a) Distinguish between selling and marketing in the present context.  (b) Elaborate the detailed elements of marketing mix with suitable examples.

Answer: selling pushes the product after production; marketing satisfies customer needs before, during and after production through the 4Ps — Product, Price, Place and Promotion.

Key points:

  • Orientation: selling is inward (“sell what we make”); marketing is outward (“make what customers need”).
  • Means and end: selling uses persuasion for immediate volume (LIC agents); marketing uses integrated decisions for long-term satisfaction and loyalty (Flipkart’s customer-first approach).
  • Scope: selling starts after production; marketing starts before it, with research, and continues after the sale with service.
  • Product: features, quality, branding, packaging — Amul’s butter-cheese-ice cream range.
  • Price: strategy, discounts, credit — Amul’s affordable ₹50/100g butter.
  • Place: channels, coverage, logistics — Coca-Cola in 25 lakh+ outlets.
  • Promotion: advertising, PR, selling, sales promotion — Surf Excel’s “Daag Acche Hain”; for emerging markets, Sheth’s 4As (Attraction, Affordability, Accessibility, Awareness).
Marketing Mix - the 4Ps of Product, Price, Place, Promotion

Takeaway: selling pushes, marketing satisfies; the 4Ps are the toolbox, the 4As the Indian lens — one example per P wins marks.

Q2. (a) Discuss the basis of segmenting the market for (i) E-vehicle and (ii) Smart phone.  (b) Discuss the process of organisational buying.

Answer: both markets segment on geographic, demographic, psychographic and behavioural bases, with different weights per product; organisational buying is a formal seven-step process driven by a buying centre.

Key points:

  • (i) E-vehicle bases: geographic (cities with charging infrastructure — Delhi, Bengaluru, Mumbai); demographic (income ₹10 lakh+, age 25-50, two-car households); psychographic (eco-conscious, early adopters, status seekers); behavioural (daily commuters valuing low running cost) — Tata Motors: Nexon EV for urban commuters, MG Comet for young city couples.
  • (ii) Smartphone bases: demographic (income, age, student vs professional); psychographic (gamers, camera lovers, status-conscious); behavioural (heavy app users, brand loyalists, upgrade cycles); geographic (urban vs rural — rural wants long battery life) — Samsung: S-series premium, M-series budget online.
  • Organisational buying: rational, multi-stakeholder, handled by a buying centre (users, influencers, buyers, deciders, gatekeepers).
  • Seven steps: need recognition → general need description → product specification → supplier search → proposal solicitation (quotations) → supplier selection and order → performance review; situations: straight rebuy, modified rebuy, new task (Tata Motors sourcing steel runs the full process).
Segmentation matrix - bases for E-vehicles and smartphones

Takeaway: four bases, product-specific weights — EV leans on charging geography and income, phones on income and usage; org buying ends with performance review.

Q3. (a) What are the brands strategy available to a firm? Examine their specific advantages and disadvantages.  (b) How do the stages of product life cycle affect the pricing of a product? Explain.

Answer: a firm chooses among branded house, house of brands, endorsed and freestanding strategies — each trades equity leverage against risk and cost; pricing must follow the PLC: skim or penetrate at introduction, compete at growth, defend at maturity, harvest at decline.

Key points:

  • Branded house: one master brand (Tata for cars, steel, tea) — advantage: equity transfers to every product; disadvantage: one failure damages the whole family.
  • House of brands: separate brands (HUL: Surf, Dove, Lux) — advantage: risk isolation, segment-specific brands; disadvantage: expensive, each brand needs its own promotion investment.
  • Endorsed brands: sub-brands with parent backing (Aashirvaad by ITC) — advantage: parent credibility + independence; disadvantage: over-endorsement confuses identity.
  • Freestanding brands: no parent link (P&G’s Whisper) — advantage: complete positioning freedom; disadvantage: built from zero, no equity transfer.
  • Introduction pricing: skimming (high price, recover R&D — Samsung S-series) or penetration (low price for quick share — Jio’s free-data launch).
  • Growth and maturity pricing: hold or ease prices while maximising share as competition arrives; at maturity, price cuts, discounts and promotional pricing defend share (smartphones).
  • Decline pricing: harvest — cut price to clear stock or divest (feature phones).
PLC and pricing - stage decides the pricing strategy

Takeaway: four brand strategies trade equity for risk; price follows the PLC — Jio = penetration, Samsung = skimming.

Q4. (a) “The retailing landscape has changed drastically with the advent of digital age.” Examine the statement with examples.  (b) Examine strategies adopted for the product modification in rural markets with suitable examples.

Answer: digital retail has made shopping boundaryless — omnichannel, quick commerce and social commerce now work alongside the kirana; rural markets reward products modified for affordability, durability and local relevance.

Key points:

  • Omnichannel: Lenskart combines online try-on with 2,000+ physical stores.
  • Quick commerce: Zepto delivers groceries in ~10 minutes from dark stores.
  • Social commerce: Meesho turns housewives into resellers on WhatsApp.
  • Click & collect and payments: JioMart + Reliance Retail; UPI and Zomato/Google reviews make the store “boundaryless” — even kiranas survive by joining digital platforms (digital-first, not digital-only).
  • Smaller packs: shampoo sachets at ₹1-2, Parle-G at ₹5 — price points fit daily incomes.
  • Sturdier and simpler products: heat-resistant, moisture-proof packaging; basic mobiles with long battery life; local-language labels and regional flavours (HUL Project Shakti, Coca-Cola’s cold-chain-ready packs in 6 lakh+ villages).
  • 4As guide: Attraction, Affordability, Accessibility, Awareness — the rural product decision framework.

Takeaway: retail is omnichannel now — apps, quick commerce and kirana coexist; rural modification = sachets, durability, local language, guided by the 4As.

Q5 (Section B - Compulsory). Describe the attribute of physical evidence in case of a Bank and a Restaurant. Examine the role of their service personnel. How can customers influence other customers in these service organisations?

Answer: services are intangible, so physical evidence (tangible cues), service personnel (who are the service itself) and customer-to-customer influence (queues, word of mouth, reviews) jointly decide perceived quality in a bank and a restaurant.

Key points:

  • Bank - physical evidence: branch layout, ATM machines, passbooks, signage, the mobile app interface, staff uniforms, cleanliness — a neat secure branch signals trust, a shabby one signals risk.
  • Restaurant - physical evidence: ambience, decor, lighting, furniture, table settings, menu design, plating, music, visible kitchen hygiene — Zomato-style dish photos also act as evidence that drives ordering.
  • Role of service personnel: services are produced and consumed simultaneously (inseparability) — the employee IS the service; bank tellers and relationship managers must be courteous, knowledgeable and quick; waiters attentive, accurate and friendly; training and empowerment matter (RATER: reliability, assurance, tangibles, empathy, responsiveness).
  • Customers influencing customers: a long queue signals popularity but slow service; a busy restaurant attracts more diners; satisfied customers spread word of mouth and post Google/Zomato reviews that win new customers; negative reviews spread fast — manage queues, encourage reviews, fix complaints quickly.

Takeaway: evidence proves quality, personnel deliver it, and every customer is both a user and a marketer — IIVP + RATER frame this answer.
