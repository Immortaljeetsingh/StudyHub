Q1. What are the main reasons that motivate firms to go international? Explain with Indian examples.

Answer: Firms go international for four broad motives. Market seeking means entering foreign markets to access new customers, follow clients abroad, or saturate the domestic market; Dr Reddy’s entered the US and European generic drug markets where margins are higher. Resource seeking means accessing raw materials, labour, or natural resources; ONGC Videsh invested in oil fields in Russia, Vietnam, and Mozambique. Efficiency seeking reduces costs through economies of scale and factor-cost arbitrage; Infosys and TCS set up global delivery centres to serve clients worldwide at lower cost. Strategic asset seeking acquires brands, technology, patents, or distribution networks; the Tata Group acquired Corus, Jaguar Land Rover, and Tetley, while Bharat Forge acquired Carl Dan Peddinghaus in Germany. Going international also exposes firms to barriers such as tariff and non-tariff barriers, cultural differences, bureaucratic delays, currency risk from exchange-rate fluctuations, and political risk from government instability, policy changes, or expropriation. Firms therefore need to match their motive with the right entry strategy and carefully assess the risks of each foreign market before committing resources.

Key points:

  • Firms go international for four broad motives.
  • Efficiency seeking reduces costs through economies of scale and factor-cost arbitrage; Infosys and TCS set up global delivery centres to serve clients worldwide at lower cost.
  • Market seeking means entering foreign markets to access new customers, follow clients abroad, or saturate the domestic market; Dr Reddy’s entered the US and European generic drug markets where margins are higher.

Takeaway: Firms therefore need to match their motive with the right entry strategy and carefully assess the risks of each foreign market before committing resources.

Q2. What is globalization? Discuss the factors that have driven globalization.

Answer: Globalization refers to the growing interdependence of national economies through cross-border flows of trade, investment, technology, and people. Its main drivers are technology, which has made communication, containerised shipping, and air travel cheaper and faster; trade liberalisation, led by the WTO and free-trade agreements, which reduced average tariffs from about 40 per cent in 1947 to around 4 per cent by 2020; regional integration, through blocs such as the EU, ASEAN, and USMCA that create larger integrated markets; MNC activities, since intra-firm trade accounts for about 33 per cent of world trade; financial liberalisation, which allows cross-border investment through capital-account convertibility; and political changes such as the end of the Cold War, China’s opening in 1978, and India’s LPG reforms of 1991. India’s journey illustrates the evolving paradigm: before 1991 the economy was inward-looking under the License Raj and import substitution, but after liberalisation FDI rose from about 100 million dollars in 1991 to 84 billion dollars by 2022, IT exports grew to 194 billion dollars, and sectors such as IT services and pharmaceuticals globalised rapidly, though challenges of trade deficit and protectionist pressures remain.

Q3. Explain the role of WTO agreements and the dispute settlement mechanism in international trade.

Answer: The WTO provides the legal and institutional framework for global trade. Its key agreements include GATT for trade in goods, GATS for services, and TRIPS for intellectual property rights. Under TRIPS, patents enjoy minimum 20-year protection, copyright 50-year protection, and trademarks and trade secrets are also safeguarded; developing countries retain flexibilities such as compulsory licensing, which India used in 2012 for the cancer drug Nexavar. The Dispute Settlement Body (DSB) is the WTO’s most distinctive mechanism: it resolves trade disputes between member countries through a binding process of consultation, panel examination, appellate body review, and implementation, and non-compliance can lead to authorised retaliation in the form of tariffs. India has been both complainant and respondent, winning its case against the US on steel tariffs in 2014 and losing its case on domestic solar panel requirements. The binding nature of rulings protects smaller economies, since every member has an equal right to seek redress. However, the Appellate Body has been non-functional since 2019 because of the US blocking appointments, weakening the dispute system, and the WTO continues to negotiate on agriculture, services, and new issues such as e-commerce.

Key points:

  • The WTO provides the legal and institutional framework for global trade.
  • Under TRIPS, patents enjoy minimum 20-year protection, copyright 50-year protection, and trademarks and trade secrets are also safeguarded; developing countries retain flexibilities such as compulsory licensing, which India used in 2012 for the cancer drug Nexavar.
  • Its key agreements include GATT for trade in goods, GATS for services, and TRIPS for intellectual property rights.

Q4. Compare the ethnocentric, polycentric, regiocentric, and geocentric orientations in international marketing.

Answer: These four orientations, known as the EPRG framework, describe the philosophy a company adopts towards foreign markets. Ethnocentric orientation applies the home-country approach everywhere, treating foreign markets as extensions of the domestic market; Patanjali uses this approach by marketing the same products globally. Polycentric orientation treats each country separately, adapting products and marketing to local conditions; Apollo Hospitals adapts to Middle East markets, and ITC Hotels adapts food menus to local tastes such as halal offerings in the Middle East. Regiocentric orientation develops a regional approach for similar markets; Unilever in South Asia balances standardisation within the region while adapting across regions. Geocentric orientation adopts a globally integrated approach, treating the world as a single market, as Apple does with its global product strategy. Companies typically evolve from ethnocentric to polycentric and towards geocentric as they gain international experience. The choice of orientation determines the degree of standardisation versus adaptation in the marketing mix, including product, price, promotion, and distribution decisions, and firms must balance the cost savings of standardisation against the local responsiveness needed for customer acceptance in each market.

  • These four orientations, known as the EPRG framework, describe the philosophy a company adopts towards foreign markets.
