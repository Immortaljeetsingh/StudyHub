Solved Answers - MMPC-14 Financial Management

Q1. (a) Three key financial decisions and wealth maximization  |  (b) Agency problem

Financial management is the planning, organizing, directing and controlling of financial activities. Firm value depends on three key decisions: V = f (I, F, D). The investment decision decides where to deploy funds — which projects, assets and working capital mix to hold. The financing decision decides how to raise funds — the mix of debt and equity (capital structure) that minimises the cost of capital. The dividend decision decides how much profit to distribute as dividends and how much to retain for reinvestment. Profit maximization is flawed because profit is ambiguous, it ignores the time value of money and risk. Wealth maximization — maximising the market value of shares — considers both time value and risk, and is therefore the superior objective. Tata Group, for example, invests in R&D and sustainability for long-term wealth creation rather than short-term profit. The agency problem arises because owners (principals) hire managers (agents) who may pursue their own interests. Agency costs include monitoring costs, bonding costs and residual loss. They are reduced through performance-linked pay, stock options (ESOPs — used by Infosys to align managers with shareholders) and good corporate governance.

Answer: Firm value is set by three decisions — investment, financing and dividend (V = f (I, F, D)) — and wealth maximisation (maximising the market value of equity) beats profit maximisation because it prices in time value and risk, while the agency problem (managers vs owners) is contained through incentive alignment and governance.

  • Three decisions: Investment (what to buy), Financing (how to pay), Dividend (how much to return).
  • Wealth maximisation = maximising market value of shares; considers TVM and risk.
  • Profit maximisation is ambiguous, ignores risk and the time value of money.
  • Agency problem: managers (agents) pursue self-interest against owners (principals).
  • Agency costs: monitoring, bonding and residual loss.
  • Reduction: ESOPs, performance-linked pay, board oversight and good governance (Infosys, Tata).

Takeaway: Every FM decision is a lever on one goal — shareholder wealth — with agency costs as the leak to plug.

Q2. (a) Time value of money with worked FV and PV  |  (b) Present value of an annuity

A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow because of earning power, inflation and uncertainty. Compounding finds the future value of a present sum, and discounting finds the present value of a future sum. TVM is the foundation of every financial decision, from capital budgeting to retirement planning. An annuity is a series of equal cash flows at regular intervals.

Future value (compounding): FV = PV × (1 + r)n
FV = 10,000 × (1.12)3 = 10,000 × 1.4049 = Rs. 14,049

Present value (discounting): PV = FV / (1 + r)n
PV = 13,000 / (1.10)3 = 13,000 / 1.331 = Rs. 9,767

(b) PV of an annuity: PVA = A × [1 - (1+r)-n] / r
PVA = 5,000 × [1 - (1.10)-4] / 0.10
(1.10)-4 = 0.6830; PVA = 5,000 × 3.1699 = Rs. 15,849

Answer: A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow because of earning power, inflation and uncertainty; compounding (FV = PV × (1+r)n) moves money forward and discounting (PV = FV/(1+r)n) brings it back, with annuities valued by their series formula.

  • FV = PV × (1+r)n; PV = FV / (1+r)n.
  • PVA = A × [1-(1+r)-n] / r (annuity received at year-end).
  • Rule of 72: doubling years ≈ 72 / rate.
  • More frequent compounding (quarterly, monthly) raises FV.
  • FV drives saving goals; PV drives valuation and pricing.

Takeaway: Same equation, two directions — compound forward to build wealth, discount back to price anything.

Q3. (a) Cost of capital and WACC numerical  |  (b) CAPM cost of equity

The cost of capital is the minimum rate of return required to keep the value of the firm intact — it is the opportunity cost of funds and serves as the discount rate in capital budgeting. The cost of debt is the after-tax interest rate because interest is tax-deductible: Kd = I(1-T)/Np. The cost of preference shares is the dividend divided by the issue price: Kp = Dp/Pp. The cost of equity can be found by the dividend growth model or CAPM: Ke = Rf + β(Rm - Rf). WACC combines all sources using market value weights.

(a) WACC: WACC = Σ (wi × ki)

Step 1: Weights. Total capital = 60,00,000 + 40,00,000 = 1,00,00,000.
we = 60,00,000/1,00,00,000 = 0.60;   wd = 40,00,000/1,00,00,000 = 0.40

Step 2: Cost of debt after tax: Kd = 12% × (1 - 0.30) = 8.4%
Cost of equity: Ke = 14% (given)

Step 3: WACC = (0.60 × 14%) + (0.40 × 8.4%) = 8.4% + 3.36% = 11.76%

(b) CAPM: Ke = Rf + β(Rm - Rf) = 7% + 1.2(12% - 7%) = 7% + 6% = 13%

Answer: The cost of capital is the minimum return a firm must earn to keep its value intact; here the after-tax debt cost (12% × 0.70 = 8.4%) and the equity cost (14%) are blended with market-value weights of 60:40 to give WACC = 11.76%, and CAPM sets Ke = 13%.

  • Kd (after tax) = Interest rate × (1 − T) — interest is tax-deductible.
  • Ke by CAPM = Rf + β(Rm − Rf); by dividend model = D1/P0 + g.
  • WACC = Σ (weight × cost) with market-value weights.
  • Lower WACC makes more projects NPV-positive.
  • WACC is the discount rate for project evaluation.

Takeaway: The debt tax-shield lowers WACC — but weights are market-value based, and WACC is the hurdle every project must clear.

Q4. (a) Investment appraisal methods with NPV numerical  |  (b) Payback period

Capital budgeting evaluates long-term investment decisions. The five methods are: payback period (time to recover the initial investment — simple, liquidity-focused, but ignores TVM and post-payback flows); accounting rate of return (average profit/average investment — uses accounting data, ignores TVM); NPV (sum of discounted cash flows minus investment; accept if NPV > 0); IRR (the discount rate at which NPV = 0; accept if IRR > WACC); and profitability index (PV of inflows/PV of outflows; accept if PI > 1, useful under capital rationing). NPV is the best method because it directly measures the increase in shareholder wealth, handles mutually exclusive projects correctly, and assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital, which is realistic.

(a) NPV: NPV = Σ CFt/(1+r)t - Initial investment

Step 1: Discount each inflow at 10%.
Year 1: 40,000/1.10 = 36,364
Year 2: 40,000/1.21 = 33,058
Year 3: 40,000/1.331 = 30,053

Step 2: Total PV of inflows = 36,364 + 33,058 + 30,053 = 99,475

Step 3: NPV = 99,475 - 1,00,000 = -Rs. 525
Since NPV is negative, the project should be rejected.

(b) Payback period: Payback = Year before full recovery + (Unrecovered cost / cash flow in recovery year)
Recovery: 40,000 + 40,000 = 80,000 by year 2; unrecovered = 20,000 in year 3.
Payback = 2 + (20,000/40,000) = 2 + 0.5 = 2.5 years

Answer: Capital budgeting judges long-term projects by payback, ARR, NPV, IRR and PI; this project (Rs. 1,00,000 outlay, Rs. 40,000 p.a. for 3 years at 10%) has NPV of −Rs. 525 and must be rejected, though its payback is a comfortable 2.5 years.

  • Accept if NPV > 0; IRR > WACC; PI > 1.
  • NPV = Σ CF/(1+r)t − initial outlay; directly measures shareholder wealth.
  • Payback = year before full recovery + unrecovered cost / next year's flow.
  • Here NPV = 99,475 − 1,00,000 = −525 → reject on NPV; payback = 2.5 years.
  • Break-even sales = fixed cost / contribution ratio.

Takeaway: NPV is the decision master; payback answers only "how fast?" and break-even only "how safe?" — never the other way round.

Q5. (a) Capital structure and EBIT-EPS analysis  |  (b) Leverage numericals

Capital structure is the mix of long-term debt and equity financing. The optimal capital structure minimises the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) and maximises firm value. Features of an appropriate capital structure are: profitability (minimises cost of capital), solvency (the firm can meet interest and principal obligations) and flexibility (the firm can raise additional funds when needed). Other determinants are leverage (debt boosts EPS when ROI exceeds the cost of debt — trading on equity), the tax advantage of debt, control (equity dilutes control, debt does not) and firm size. The EBIT-EPS analysis compares financing alternatives by computing EPS at different EBIT levels; the indifference point is the EBIT at which EPS is identical under two plans: (EBIT - I1)(1-T)/N1 = (EBIT - I2)(1-T)/N2. Infrastructure companies such as Adani use high leverage because their ROI exceeds the cost of debt, while IT companies such as TCS prefer equity for flexibility.

(b) Leverage: DOL = Contribution/EBIT; DFL = EBIT/(EBIT - Interest); DCL = DOL × DFL

Step 1: Contribution = Sales - Variable cost = 5,00,000 - (60% of 5,00,000) = 5,00,000 - 3,00,000 = 2,00,000

Step 2: EBIT = Contribution - Fixed cost = 2,00,000 - 80,000 = 1,20,000

Step 3: DOL = 2,00,000/1,20,000 = 1.67
DFL = 1,20,000/(1,20,000 - 20,000) = 1,20,000/1,00,000 = 1.20
DCL = 1.67 × 1.20 = 2.00

Answer: Capital structure is the long-term debt–equity mix; the optimal structure minimises WACC and maximises firm value by balancing the debt tax-shield against financial-distress cost, and the EBIT–EPS analysis locates the EBIT where two financing plans give equal EPS (here DOL 1.67, DFL 1.20, DCL 2.00).

  • Features of an appropriate structure: profitability, solvency, flexibility, conservatism, control.
  • Trade-off: debt tax-shield lowers WACC at first, financial-distress risk raises it later.
  • EBIT–EPS indifference: (EBIT − I1)(1−T)/N1 = (EBIT − I2)(1−T)/N2.
  • DOL = Contribution/EBIT; DFL = EBIT/(EBIT − Interest); DCL = DOL × DFL.
  • Here DOL = 2,00,000/1,20,000 = 1.67; DFL = 1.20; DCL = 2.00 → a 1% sales change swings EPS ≈ 2%.
  • Determinants: trading on equity, tax advantage, control, flexibility, asset structure.

Takeaway: Structure is a U-curve — cheap debt first, distress later; the optimum is minimum WACC, not maximum debt.

Q6. (a) Dividend theories  |  (b) Forms of dividend and dividend policies

Dividend theories debate whether the dividend decision affects firm value. The irrelevance school is led by Modigliani and Miller (MM): in perfect markets — no taxes, no transaction costs, rational investors — dividend policy does not affect firm value because investors can create home-made dividends by selling shares, and value depends on the earning power of assets, not the payout. The relevance school argues the opposite. Walter's model relates the return on investment (r) to the cost of capital (k): growth firms (r > k) should retain profits and pay low dividends; declining firms (r < k) should distribute more; only when r = k is value independent of payout. Gordon's bird-in-hand argument holds that investors value certain current dividends more than uncertain future capital gains, so a higher payout raises value. In practice, companies distribute dividends in different forms: cash dividend, bonus shares, stock splits and buybacks. The common policies are stable rupee dividend (a fixed amount per share, as followed by Infosys), stable payout ratio (fixed percentage of earnings), incremental dividend, and residual dividend (paying only from profits left after funding investment needs). TCS regularly does buybacks and bonus issues to return cash to shareholders.

Answer: Dividend theories debate whether payout changes firm value: Walter and Gordon (relevance) say it does — growth firms should retain while high-payout firms satisfy risk-averse investors — while MM (irrelevance) show in perfect markets that investors create home-made dividends, so only investment policy matters.

  • Walter: retain when r > k (growth firms); distribute when r < k; indifferent at r = k.
  • Gordon bird-in-hand: P0 = D1/(Ke − g); current dividend beats uncertain capital gains.
  • MM: perfect markets (no tax, no costs, rational investors) → dividend policy irrelevant.
  • Forms of dividend: cash, bonus shares, stock split, buyback.
  • Policies: stable rupee, stable payout ratio, incremental, residual.
  • Info content: dividend changes signal management's view of future earnings.

Takeaway: Relevance says payout signals value; MM says only investment policy creates it — both agree retention must fund positive-NPV projects.

June 2022 — Term-End Examination 8 Questions

Q1. Explain the nature of Finance Function and discuss the approaches of Financial Management.

Answer: The finance function procures and deploys funds, and its approach has evolved from the traditional view of episodic fund-raising to the modern managerial view where investment, financing and dividend decisions are taken continuously to maximise wealth.

  • Finance function covers estimation, procurement, allocation and control of funds.
  • Traditional approach: raising funds only at events (issue, merger, expansion); ignored internal allocation.
  • Modern/managerial approach: continuous, integrated, broad scope — decisions, not events.
  • Decision-making approach: liquidity, profitability and risk-return trade-off treated as one system.
  • Today the CFO is a strategic partner, not a treasury clerk.

Takeaway: FM grew from "raising money when needed" to "managing money every day" — decisions over events.

Q2. Explain the concept of 'Future Value' and describe how it is calculated. Bring out the difference between Future Value and Present Value.

Answer: Future value is what a present sum grows into at compound interest (FV = PV × (1+r)n), while present value is what a future sum is worth today (PV = FV/(1+r)n) — FV adds interest forward, PV removes it backward.

  • FV = PV × (1+r)n — compounding a single amount.
  • PV = FV / (1+r)n — discounting, the mirror image.
  • FV > PV whenever r > 0 and n > 0; the gap widens with n.
  • More frequent compounding: FV = PV × (1 + r/m)mn.
  • FV answers "what will my savings become?"; PV answers "what is a future receipt worth now?"
  • Example: Rs. 10,000 at 10% for 3 years → FV = Rs. 13,310; Rs. 13,310 after 3 years → PV = Rs. 10,000.

Takeaway: FV and PV are inverse readings of the same equation — know which direction your question faces.

Q3. What is the need for Valuation? What is the genesis of it? Explain the various ways in which the term value can be defined.

Answer: Valuation is needed to price securities, businesses and assets for transactions, investment and reporting; its genesis is that value equals expected future benefits discounted for time and risk, and "value" takes different meanings — book, market, intrinsic, going-concern, liquidation and replacement.

  • Needs: IPO and M&A pricing, investment appraisal, credit and tax decisions, statutory reporting.
  • Genesis: value springs from expected future cash flows and the risk of receiving them.
  • Book value: assets − liabilities as per the balance sheet.
  • Market value: the price the market is willing to pay.
  • Intrinsic/fundamental value: PV of expected future cash flows (true worth to an investor).
  • Going-concern value vs liquidation value; replacement value.

Takeaway: Always state which value you mean — book for accounting, intrinsic for investing, liquidation for winding up.

Q4. Why is Cost of Equity Capital difficult to measure? Discuss the different methods of computing Cost of Equity Capital.

Answer: Cost of equity is difficult because equity has no contractual fixed charge, dividends are discretionary and the return is a risky residual — so Ke is estimated, by dividend-price-plus-growth, CAPM, earnings yield, bond yield plus risk premium or realised yield.

  • No fixed contractual payment, unlike debt interest or preference dividend.
  • Dividends are not compulsory; earnings are residual and uncertain.
  • Risk-free rate, beta and market risk premium are all estimates.
  • Dividend growth model: Ke = D1/P0 + g.
  • CAPM: Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf).
  • Earnings yield: Ke = EPS/P0; also bond yield + risk premium and realised yield.
  • Practice: use two or more methods and take an average.

Takeaway: Ke is an estimate, not a price — the exam answer must name at least three methods.

Q5. Discuss Equity Capital and Debt Capital as sources of long-term capital bringing out the advantages and disadvantages of each source.

Answer: Equity is permanent ownership capital with a residual claim, no fixed charge but higher cost and dilution; debt is borrowed capital with contractual interest, a priority claim and a tax shield, but it creates a fixed financial obligation.

  • Equity: no maturity, no fixed charge, residual claim, voting rights, control dilution.
  • Debt: fixed maturity, contractual interest (tax-deductible), priority claim over equity.
  • Equity is the costliest source; debt is the cheapest because of the tax shield.
  • Debt raises EPS while ROI > interest rate (trading on equity) but adds financial risk.
  • Equity suits long-gestation, uncertain projects; debt suits stable cash-flow firms.
  • Neither 100% equity (too costly) nor 100% debt (too risky) is optimal.

Takeaway: Equity buys safety at a price; debt buys cheapness at a risk — the blend is the capital structure.

Q6. What is the meaning of 'Behavioural Finance'? Discuss any five biases that influence investment decisions of an investor.

Answer: Behavioural finance studies how psychology and cognitive biases make real investors deviate from rational models; five key biases are overconfidence, herding, loss aversion, anchoring and confirmation bias.

  • Overconfidence: overestimating skill → overtrading and under-diversification.
  • Herding: copying the crowd → bubbles and crashes.
  • Loss aversion: losses feel about twice as painful as equal gains → holding losers too long.
  • Anchoring: fixing on the purchase price or initial information.
  • Confirmation bias: seeking only information that supports an existing view.
  • Others: mental accounting, disposition effect, regret aversion.

Takeaway: Markets are not always rational — naming your own bias is the first line of defence.

Q7. Explain the concept of Capital Structure. Discuss the various determinants of a capital structure.

Answer: Capital structure is the permanent mix of debt, preference and equity; the right structure minimises WACC and maximises value subject to profitability, solvency, flexibility, control, cost, tax shield, asset structure and market conditions.

  • Meaning: the long-term financing mix of the firm.
  • Optimal structure: minimum WACC → maximum firm value.
  • Determinants: trading on equity, tax advantage of debt, control (debt avoids dilution), flexibility, solvency, profitability, firm size, asset structure, market conditions.
  • Stable EBIT and tangible fixed assets support higher leverage.
  • Structure changes with industry norms and the growth stage of the firm.

Takeaway: No universal structure — it is the balance of cost, risk, control and flexibility for each firm.

Q8. Aravind Motors Ltd. is considering two mutually exclusive projects. Both require an initial cash outlay of Rs. 10,000 each for machinery and have a life of 5 years. The company's required rate of return is 10% and it pays tax at 50%. The projects will be depreciated on a straight-line basis. Net cash flows before taxes — Project 1: 4,000 every year; Project 2: 6,000, 3,000, 2,000, 5,000, 5,000. PV factors at 10%: 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683, 0.621. Calculate and suggest which project is better under (i) Pay Back Period method (ii) NPV and Profitability Index methods.

Answer: After-tax flows are P1 = Rs. 3,000 p.a. and P2 = 4,000, 2,500, 2,000, 3,500, 3,500; payback picks Project 1 (3.33 vs 3.43 years), while NPV (Rs. 1,370 vs Rs. 1,767) and PI (1.137 vs 1.177) both pick Project 2 — accept Project 2 as the wealth-maximising choice.

Key points:

Given: Outlay = Rs. 10,000 each; life 5 yrs; required return 10%; tax 50%; SL depreciation = 10,000/5 = Rs. 2,000 p.a.
Formula: After-tax CF = (CFBT − Dep) × (1 − T) + Dep

Project 1 (CFBT 4,000 p.a.): (4,000 − 2,000)(0.50) + 2,000 = Rs. 3,000 p.a. × 5 years
Project 2: Y1: (6,000−2,000)(0.5)+2,000 = 4,000   Y2: (3,000−2,000)(0.5)+2,000 = 2,500   Y3: 2,000   Y4: 3,500   Y5: 3,500

(i) Payback = Investment / annual CF (annuity) or year + unrecovered/next CF:
P1: 10,000 / 3,000 = 3.33 years
P2: cumulative 4,000 + 2,500 + 2,000 = 8,500; remaining 1,500 in Y4 → 3 + 1,500/3,500 = 3.43 years

(ii) NPV = Σ CF × PV factor − Outlay:
P1: 3,000 × (0.909+0.826+0.751+0.683+0.621) = 3,000 × 3.790 = 11,370 − 10,000 = +Rs. 1,370
P2: (4,000×0.909) + (2,500×0.826) + (2,000×0.751) + (3,500×0.683) + (3,500×0.621)
= 3,636 + 2,065 + 1,502 + 2,391 + 2,174 = 11,767 − 10,000 = +Rs. 1,767

Profitability Index = PV of inflows / Outlay: P1 = 11,370/10,000 = 1.137; P2 = 11,767/10,000 = 1.177

Decision: Payback method → Project 1 (shorter). NPV and PI → Project 2 (higher wealth addition). Both are acceptable (NPV > 0, PI > 1); as mutually exclusive choices, Project 2 is better because it adds Rs. 1,767 vs Rs. 1,370.

Takeaway: When payback and NPV disagree, trust NPV — payback ignores the time value of the flows it counts.

Q1. What is 'Profit Maximisation'? What are its limitations? How is it different from Wealth Maximisation?

Answer: Profit maximisation targets the largest short-run accounting profit but is ambiguous, ignores time value and risk and breeds short-termism, so wealth maximisation — maximising the present value of future cash flows (market value of shares) — is the superior objective.

  • Profit is ambiguous: EBT, PAT, EPS or ROI — which one?
  • Ignores timing of returns (TVM) and risk (two projects, same profit, different risk).
  • Encourages short-termism: defer maintenance, cut R&D.
  • Wealth max: PV of expected cash flows at a risk-adjusted rate.
  • Wealth max handles profit, time and risk in one objective.

Takeaway: Profit is a means; value creation is the end.

Q2. What is Future Value? How is it calculated? Explain with a suitable example.

Answer: Future value is the value of a present sum at a future date after compounding — FV = PV × (1+r)n; e.g., Rs. 10,000 at 12% for 3 years = Rs. 10,000 × 1.4049 = Rs. 14,049.

  • FV = PV × (1 + r)n; interest earns interest.
  • Example: Rs. 10,000 at 12% p.a. for 3 years = Rs. 14,049.
  • Rule of 72: 72/12 = 6 years to double at 12%.
  • Frequency matters: FV = PV(1 + r/m)mn.
  • Inverse operation is discounting to PV.

Takeaway: Compounding is the engine of wealth — time is the exponent.

Q3. What is the significance of calculating Cost of Equity Capital? Discuss the different methods of computing it.

Answer: Cost of equity is the return equity investors require; it matters as the project hurdle rate, a WACC component and a valuation input, and is estimated by the dividend-price-plus-growth model, CAPM, earnings yield, bond yield plus risk premium and realised-yield methods.

  • Significance: discount rate for equity-financed projects, WACC input, security pricing.
  • Dividend growth: Ke = D1/P0 + g.
  • CAPM: Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf).
  • Earnings yield: Ke = EPS/P0; bond yield + risk premium; realised yield.
  • All are estimates — cross-check with two methods.

Takeaway: Ke is the price of the residual-risk claim — estimated, never quoted.

Q4. What do you mean by 'Working Capital'? Why is it important? Discuss the different components of working capital.

Answer: Working capital is the investment in current assets (net of current liabilities); it is important because it is the liquidity that keeps operations running — too little means insolvency, too much means idle funds — and its components (cash, inventory, receivables) must each be optimised.

  • Gross WC = total current assets; net WC = CA − CL.
  • Components: cash and bank, inventory (raw material, WIP, finished goods), receivables, marketable securities, prepaid expenses.
  • Operating cycle: cash → inventory → receivables → cash.
  • Trade-off: liquidity vs profitability.
  • Determinants: nature of business, seasonality, credit policy, growth.

Takeaway: Working capital is the firm's bloodstream — manage each component for the liquidity-profitability balance.

Q5. What is the 'Money Market'? What is its significance? Discuss the role played by different participants in this market.

Answer: The money market deals in short-term (up to one year) funds and instruments; its significance is liquidity management, monetary policy transmission and low-risk parking of surpluses, and its participants are RBI, banks, mutual funds and LIC, corporates, brokers and finance companies.

  • Instruments: T-bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit, call money, repos.
  • RBI: regulator and monetary-policy operator (open market operations).
  • Banks: borrow and lend call money; MFs and LIC: surplus investors.
  • Corporates: issue commercial paper for working capital.
  • No physical floor — a telecommunication-based market.

Takeaway: Money market = overnight-to-one-year money; know instrument-participant pairs.

Q6. What is the importance of Capital Structure Planning? Explain the different determinants of Capital Structure.

  • Example: cutting R&D lifts this year's profit but destroys long-term value.

Takeaway: Profit is a means, not the end — value creation is the end.

Q2. Explain the concept of Investment Risk. Discuss the different types of risk.

  • Risk = variability of return; uncertainty has no probability distribution.
  • Business risk: operating variability — demand, cost, competition.
  • Financial risk: added by fixed interest burden of debt.
  • Market risk (systematic): affects all firms — recession, policy.
  • Interest-rate, inflation (purchasing power), liquidity, default, forex risk.
  • Unsystematic risk is diversifiable; only systematic risk is priced.

Q4. What is the significance of Investment Decisions? Explain the payback method of investment appraisal with the help of a suitable example.

  • Payback = year before full recovery + unrecovered / next year's inflow.
  • Example: Rs. 1,00,000 outlay; Rs. 40,000 + 40,000 = 80,000 by year 2; 20,000/40,000 → 2.5 years.

Q5. What is the significance of managing cash in an organisation? Explain the different techniques of managing cash flows of an organisation.

Answer: Cash is the most liquid but least productive asset, so cash management minimises idle balances while avoiding shortages; techniques are cash budgeting, accelerating collections (lockbox, concentration banking), slowing disbursements and investing surpluses in marketable securities.

  • Significance: meet obligations (liquidity) and avoid idle funds (profitability).
  • Cash budget: forecast inflows and outflows to spot surplus or deficit.
  • Accelerate collections: lockbox, concentration banking, prompt billing.
  • Decelerate disbursements: pay on due date, use the float, centralised payables.
  • Surplus: park in near-cash marketable securities (T-bills, CP).
  • Baumol and Miller-Orr models fix the optimum cash balance.

Takeaway: Cash management = speed collections, slow payments, invest the idle — never let cash sleep.

Q6. Compare and contrast financing of a firm through Equity shares and Debentures and bring out their relative advantages and disadvantages.

Answer: Equity shares are ownership with residual profit sharing and voting rights but no fixed charge; debentures are borrowed funds with fixed tax-deductible interest and a priority claim — debentures are cheaper and preserve control, equity is permanent and safer.

  • Equity: ownership, residual claim, permanent, voting rights, no fixed charge.

Q7. Why is dividend decision important for a company? Briefly explain the Relevance Theories of Dividend.

Takeaway: Relevance theories: payout changes value; MM: only in imperfect markets — real markets lean relevance.

  • Three reasons: earning capacity, inflation, uncertainty.
  • Significance: capital budgeting, security valuation, loan EMIs, retirement planning.
  • Compounding: FV = PV × (1+r)n.
  • Discounting: PV = FV / (1+r)n.
  • Frequency: FV = PV × (1 + r/m)mn.

Takeaway: TVM is the grammar of all finance — master the two directions.

Q2. What is the need for valuation of Securities? Discuss the different Business Valuation Approaches.

Answer: Securities need valuation for purchase and sale decisions, portfolio management, IPO pricing and statutory valuation; the approaches are income (discount expected cash flows), market (comparable prices and multiples) and asset-based (NAV).

  • Need: investing, M&A, financial reporting, tax, capital raising.
  • Income approach: PV of dividends/cash flows (DDM, DCF).
  • Market approach: P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA of comparable securities.
  • Asset approach: NAV — assets minus liabilities.
  • Bond value = PV of coupons + redemption; equity value via DDM.

Takeaway: Bonds are priced by contract, equities by expectation — both are PV problems.

Q3. What is the significance of Working Capital? What are the determinants of Working Capital?

Answer: Working capital management keeps the firm liquid and profitable by optimising current assets; the need is determined by business nature, operating cycle, seasonality, credit policy, growth, inflation, profitability and access to short-term finance.

  • Net WC = CA − CL; gross WC = total current assets.
  • Significance: solvency, uninterrupted operations, goodwill, optimum returns.
  • Determinants: nature and scale of business; production cycle; seasonality; credit terms; growth; inflation; short-term credit availability.
  • Conservative vs aggressive policies trade liquidity against return.
  • Manage cash, inventory and receivables to their optima.

Takeaway: Working capital need = f(operating cycle, seasonality, credit policy) — match it, don't guess it.

Q4. What are 'Equity Shares' and 'Debentures'? Compare and contrast these two in relation to their advantages and disadvantages.

Answer: Equity shares are ownership capital with residual claims and voting rights; debentures are creditors' capital with fixed tax-deductible interest and a priority claim — equity is permanent and safe but costly, debentures are cheap and control-preserving but obligational.

  • Equity: residual, permanent, no fixed charge, voting, discretionary dividends.
  • Debentures: contractual interest, tax shield, priority, fixed maturity, no voting.
  • Cost: equity highest; debentures cheapest (shield).
  • Risk: debentures add financial risk (fixed burden).
  • Control: equity dilutes; debentures do not.
  • Choose the mix by cash-flow stability and growth needs.

Takeaway: Equity for permanence, debt for cheapness — the mix is the strategy.

Q5. Why is dividend decision important for a firm? Explain briefly, different relevance theories of dividend.

Answer: The dividend decision matters because it signals earnings quality and satisfies investor cash preference; the relevance theories — Walter (retention when r > k), Gordon (bird-in-hand: P0 = D1/(Ke − g)) and Lintner (payout smoothing) — hold that payout affects value, unlike MM's irrelevance in perfect markets.

  • Dividend decision: the distribution vs retention trade-off.
  • Walter: r > k → retain; r < k → distribute.
  • Gordon: investors value certain current dividends; higher payout → higher P0.
  • Lintner: gradual adjustment towards a target payout (signalling stability).
  • MM: perfect markets make dividend policy irrelevant — home-made dividends.
  • Empirically, payout changes carry information.

Takeaway: Exam favourite — Walter's r-vs-k rule, Gordon's bird-in-hand, MM's home-made dividends.

Q6. Explain the characteristics of a sound investment appraisal method. Discuss the different discounted cash flow methods of appraising investment proposals.

Answer: A sound method must use cash flows, the time value of money, the full project life and risk, and rank projects to maximise wealth; the DCF methods are NPV, IRR, profitability index and discounted payback.

  • Criteria: TVM-based; cash-flow based; whole-life; risk-adjusted; objective ranking; wealth maximising.
  • NPV = Σ CF/(1+r)t − outlay; accept NPV > 0.
  • IRR: the rate at which NPV = 0; accept IRR > cost of capital.
  • PI = PV of inflows / outlay; accept PI > 1; ranks under rationing.
  • Discounted payback: payback on discounted flows.
  • NPV is best; IRR can mis-rank mutually exclusive projects.

Takeaway: NPV decides, IRR reports the return rate, PI ranks under rationing — all discount the flows.

Q7. What is Behavioural Finance? Discuss its scope and characteristics. Differentiate between Behavioural Finance and Traditional Finance.

Answer: Behavioural finance studies how psychological biases and heuristics distort investor decisions and prices; its scope spans pricing anomalies, investor behaviour and market microstructure, and its characteristics — bounded rationality, sentiment, framing — contrast with traditional finance's rational-efficient-market assumption.

  • Scope: anomalies (momentum, value, post-earnings drift), biases, herding, noise trading, bubbles.
  • Characteristics: irrationality, heuristics, framing, loss aversion, limited arbitrage.
  • Traditional: rational agents, EMH, price = intrinsic value.
  • Behavioural: prices can diverge from value for long spells.
  • Application: better investing discipline, product design, policy.

Takeaway: Traditional finance says how prices should behave; behavioural says how they actually do.

Q8. A company needs Rs. 5,00,000 for acquiring a new plant. Three financing options: (i) 50,000 equity shares of Rs. 10 each; (ii) 25,000 equity shares of Rs. 10 each + 2,500 debentures of Rs. 100 each at 8% interest; (iii) 25,000 equity shares of Rs. 10 each + 2,500 preference shares of Rs. 100 each at 8% dividend. EBIT is Rs. 1,00,000 and tax rate 50%. Calculate EPS for each option and suggest which option is best.

Answer: EPS is Rs. 1.00 under all-equity, Rs. 1.60 with debt and Rs. 1.20 with preference shares — Option (ii), debt financing, gives the highest EPS because interest is tax-deductible while preference dividend is not.

Key points:

Given: Funds needed = Rs. 5,00,000; EBIT = Rs. 1,00,000; tax = 50%.
Option (ii) debt = 2,500 × 100 = Rs. 2,50,000 → Interest = 8% × 2,50,000 = Rs. 20,000
Option (iii) preference = 2,500 × 100 = Rs. 2,50,000 → Pref. dividend = 8% × 2,50,000 = Rs. 20,000

Formula: EPS = (EBIT − Interest)(1 − T) / Number of equity shares [preference dividend deducted after tax]

Option (i) — all equity: (1,00,000 × 0.50) / 50,000 = 50,000 / 50,000 = Rs. 1.00
Option (ii) — 25,000 equity + debt: (1,00,000 − 20,000) × 0.50 / 25,000 = 40,000 / 25,000 = Rs. 1.60
Option (iii) — 25,000 equity + preference: [(1,00,000 × 0.50) − 20,000] / 25,000 = 30,000 / 25,000 = Rs. 1.20

Decision: Option (ii) gives the highest EPS (Rs. 1.60) — the interest tax shield makes debt cheaper than preference shares — Option (ii) is best.

Takeaway: In EBIT-EPS, rank debt over preference shares — the tax shield is the difference (1.60 vs 1.20).
