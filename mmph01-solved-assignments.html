Q1. Define an organisation. Explain the features of an open system and why all real organisations are open systems.

Answer: An organisation is a social unit with specific purposes, derived from the Greek word "organon" meaning a tool or instrument. Chester I. Barnard defined it as "a system of consciously coordinated activities of two or more persons." Levitt (1965) identified four components of an organisation: task, people, technology, and structure. Organisations can be studied as open or closed systems. A closed system is self-contained and isolated from its environment; it exists only theoretically. An open system constantly interacts with its environment and has seven features: subsystems, synergy, an input-output model, goal-seeking, entropy, steady state, and feedback. All real organisations are open systems because they depend on their environment for inputs such as raw materials, people, and information, convert them through transformation processes, and return outputs like products and services. A manufacturing company illustrates this: it receives raw materials (inputs), transforms them (throughput), sells to customers (output), and uses customer feedback to improve (feedback loop). The McKinsey 7S model complements this view by balancing hard elements (strategy, structure, systems) with soft elements (style, staff, shared values, skills).

Key points:

  • An organisation is a social unit with specific purposes, derived from the Greek word "organon" meaning a tool or instrument.
  • All real organisations are open systems because they depend on their environment for inputs such as raw materials, people, and information, convert them through transformation processes, and return outputs like products and services.
  • Barnard defined it as "a system of consciously coordinated activities of two or more persons." Levitt (1965) identified four components of an organisation: task, people, technology, and structure.

Takeaway: The McKinsey 7S model complements this view by balancing hard elements (strategy, structure, systems) with soft elements (style, staff, shared values, skills).

Q2. Compare the classical, neo-classical, and contingency theories of organisation.

Answer: Classical theory focuses on efficiency, structure, and principles. It has three streams: Scientific Management (Taylor) with time-and-motion studies and standardisation, Administrative Theory (Fayol) with his 14 principles, and Bureaucracy (Weber) with hierarchy, rules, and impersonality. Its main limitation is that it ignores human factors. Neo-classical theory emerged from the Human Relations Movement; Elton Mayo's Hawthorne Experiments showed that social factors matter more than physical working conditions, and that productivity improves when workers know they are being observed (the Hawthorne Effect). Systems theory treats the organisation as an open system with inputs, transformation, and outputs; Katz and Kahn (1966) first applied open systems theory to organisations. Contingency theory holds that there is no one best way to organise; the design depends on the environment, technology, and size of the organisation. Burns and Stalker and Lawrence and Lorsch showed that structure must fit situational conditions. A standard exam answer compares these three perspectives in a table: classical emphasises efficiency but ignores humans, neo-classical overemphasises social factors, and contingency offers no universal principles but the best fit for real situations.

Key points:

  • Classical theory focuses on efficiency, structure, and principles.
  • It has three streams: Scientific Management (Taylor) with time-and-motion studies and standardisation, Administrative Theory (Fayol) with his 14 principles, and Bureaucracy (Weber) with hierarchy, rules, and impersonality.
  • A standard exam answer compares these three perspectives in a table: classical emphasises efficiency but ignores humans, neo-classical overemphasises social factors, and contingency offers no universal principles but the best fit for real situations.

Q3. Explain Mintzberg's five parts of an organisation and his five organisational forms.

Answer: Henry Mintzberg described every organisation as having five parts: the strategic apex (top management such as the CEO and board), the middle line (middle managers connecting top to bottom), the operating core (workers who do the basic work), the technostructure (analysts such as engineers, planners, and accountants), and the support staff (indirect services like maintenance, legal, and the cafeteria). Based on how these parts combine, Mintzberg proposed five organisational forms. The simple structure has low formalisation and high centralisation, best for small entrepreneurial firms. The machine bureaucracy has high formalisation and centralisation, suited to stable mass production. The professional bureaucracy has low-to-moderate formalisation and low centralisation, best for professional services. The divisionalised form is decentralised with low formalisation within divisions, suited to large diversified companies. The adhocracy is highly flexible and decentralised, designed for innovation and change. The memory aid is SMPDA: Simple, Machine, Professional, Divisionalised, Adhocracy. This framework connects directly to Burns and Stalker's mechanistic-organic continuum, where mechanistic structures suit stable environments and organic structures suit unstable ones.

Key points:

  • Henry Mintzberg described every organisation as having five parts: the strategic apex (top management such as the CEO and board), the middle line (middle managers connecting top to bottom), the operating core (workers who do the basic work), the technostructure (analysts such as engineers, planners, and accountants), and the support staff (indirect services like maintenance, legal, and the cafeteria).
  • Based on how these parts combine, Mintzberg proposed five organisational forms.
  • The memory aid is SMPDA: Simple, Machine, Professional, Divisionalised, Adhocracy.

Q4. Describe the Hackman-Oldham Job Characteristics Model and explain how a manager can apply it to enrich jobs.

Answer: Hackman and Oldham's Job Characteristics Model explains how job design influences motivation, satisfaction, and performance. It identifies five core job dimensions: skill variety (using different skills), task identity (completing a whole piece of work), task significance (impact on others' lives), autonomy (freedom in scheduling and procedures), and feedback (direct information about performance). These dimensions create three critical psychological states: experienced meaningfulness (from skill variety, task identity, and task significance), experienced responsibility (from autonomy), and knowledge of results (from feedback). The motivating potential score is calculated as MPS = (Skill Variety + Task Identity + Task Significance)/3 x Autonomy x Feedback. Managers can enrich jobs by combining tasks, giving employees responsibility for planning their own work, and providing direct feedback. This approach contrasts with job simplification (Taylor's fit-people-to-jobs approach), job rotation (lateral movement), and job enlargement (horizontal loading of more tasks). Job enrichment is vertical loading: adding responsibility, freedom, and growth opportunities, as Herzberg recommended. The memory aid is STTAF: Skill Variety, Task Identity, Task Significance, Autonomy, Feedback.

Key points:

  • Hackman and Oldham's Job Characteristics Model explains how job design influences motivation, satisfaction, and performance.
  • It identifies five core job dimensions: skill variety (using different skills), task identity (completing a whole piece of work), task significance (impact on others' lives), autonomy (freedom in scheduling and procedures), and feedback (direct information about performance).
  • The memory aid is STTAF: Skill Variety, Task Identity, Task Significance, Autonomy, Feedback.
