Q1. Define International HRM and explain the factors that make it more complex than domestic HRM. (10 marks)

Answer: International Human Resource Management (IHRM) is the management of human resources across national borders - the procurement, allocation, and utilization of people in multinational corporations. It covers the same functions as domestic HRM - recruitment, selection, training, compensation, and performance management - but adds the complexity of managing employees from multiple nationalities in multiple countries. Key terms include MNCs and TNCs, and the employee categories PCNs (parent-country nationals), HCNs (host-country nationals), and TCNs (third-country nationals). Six factors distinguish IHRM from domestic HRM: (1) a wider range of nationalities among employees; (2) broader and more diverse external relationships; (3) the political, legal, and economic complexity of the international environment; (4) the enduring complexity of coordinating across time zones, currencies, and legal systems; (5) additional functions such as expatriate taxation, visa management, and repatriation; and (6) different approaches, because practices that work in one culture may fail in another - for example, a performance appraisal system designed for one country may conflict with collectivist norms elsewhere. Effective IHRM aligns people practices with international business strategy while remaining sensitive to local contexts.

Key points:

  • International Human Resource Management (IHRM) is the management of human resources across national borders - the procurement, allocation, and utilization of people in multinational corporations.
  • Six factors distinguish IHRM from domestic HRM: (1) a wider range of nationalities among employees; (2) broader and more diverse external relationships; (3) the political, legal, and economic complexity of the international environment; (4) the enduring complexity of coordinating across time zones, currencies, and legal systems; (5) additional functions such as expatriate taxation, visa management, and repatriation; and (6) different approaches, because practices that work in one culture may fail in another - for example, a performance appraisal system designed for one country may conflict with collectivist norms elsewhere.
  • It covers the same functions as domestic HRM - recruitment, selection, training, compensation, and performance management - but adds the complexity of managing employees from multiple nationalities in multiple countries.

Takeaway: Effective IHRM aligns people practices with international business strategy while remaining sensitive to local contexts.

Q2. Explain Hofstede's cultural dimensions and their relevance to international HRM. (10 marks)

Answer: Hofstede defined culture as "the collective programming of the mind" that distinguishes one group from another, and his research identified six dimensions for comparing national cultures. (1) Power Distance (PDI) - the extent to which less powerful members accept unequal power distribution; India's high power distance means subordinates expect direction and hierarchy. (2) Individualism vs Collectivism (IDV) - whether identity is personal or group-based; Indian culture is collectivist, emphasizing family and community. (3) Masculinity vs Femininity (MAS) - assertiveness and achievement versus cooperation and care. (4) Uncertainty Avoidance (UAI) - tolerance for ambiguity; high-avoidance cultures prefer rules and job security. (5) Long-Term vs Short-Term Orientation (LTO) - thrift and perseverance versus tradition. (6) Indulgence vs Restraint (IVR) - freedom to enjoy life versus strict social norms. Relevance to IHRM: compensation and reward systems must match achievement norms; leadership styles must suit the power distance; recruitment, appraisal, and training must respect cultural values; and expatriates must understand these dimensions to adapt. Hofstede's framework, along with Trompenaars' seven dimensions, helps HR managers design globally consistent yet culturally sensitive policies and prepare employees for cross-cultural assignments.

Key points:

  • Hofstede defined culture as "the collective programming of the mind" that distinguishes one group from another, and his research identified six dimensions for comparing national cultures.
  • (1) Power Distance (PDI) - the extent to which less powerful members accept unequal power distribution; India's high power distance means subordinates expect direction and hierarchy.
  • (2) Individualism vs Collectivism (IDV) - whether identity is personal or group-based; Indian culture is collectivist, emphasizing family and community.

Takeaway: Hofstede's framework, along with Trompenaars' seven dimensions, helps HR managers design globally consistent yet culturally sensitive policies and prepare employees for cross-cultural assignments.

Q3. What is culture shock? Describe its stages and the selection criteria for international assignments. (10 marks)

Answer: Culture shock is the disorientation an employee experiences when living and working in an unfamiliar culture. It typically follows four stages: (1) Honeymoon - excitement about the new country, its food, and its people; (2) Crisis - frustration, homesickness, and difficulty adjusting as reality sets in; (3) Adjustment - gradual learning of local norms, language, and work practices; (4) Mastery - full competence and comfort in the host culture. Some employees never progress beyond the crisis stage, leading to premature return, which is costly: expatriate failure costs two to three times the annual salary. Selection criteria for international assignments therefore go beyond technical competence: cultural adaptability and openness, family situation and spouse support, language proficiency, motivation to take the assignment, emotional stability and resilience, and past international exposure. Organizations must also decide whether to select PCNs, HCNs, or TCNs based on their staffing philosophy - ethnocentric, polycentric, regiocentric, or geocentric. Because family adjustment is a leading cause of failure, companies like Tata and Infosys involve families in the selection process and provide pre-departure support to improve the chances of assignment success.

Key points:

  • Culture shock is the disorientation an employee experiences when living and working in an unfamiliar culture.
  • Because family adjustment is a leading cause of failure, companies like Tata and Infosys involve families in the selection process and provide pre-departure support to improve the chances of assignment success.

Q4. Explain the components of international compensation and the Balance Sheet approach. (10 marks)

Answer: International compensation must keep expatriates' standard of living at home, motivate them to accept assignments, and ensure fairness relative to host-country employees. The main components are: base salary; a foreign service premium (an inducement of 10-30 per cent of base salary); a cost-of-living allowance (COLA) to offset higher prices in the host country; a housing allowance; an education allowance for children; relocation and moving expenses; and tax equalization, since expatriates often face double taxation. The Balance Sheet approach aims to maintain the expatriate's home-country standard of living: the employer compares what the employee would spend at home with what they will spend in the host country and pays allowances for the difference, making the package "neutral" between home and host locations. Its advantages are equity and ease of home-country comparison, but it is complex, expensive, and administratively heavy. The alternative Going Rate approach pays host-country market rates, which is simpler and locally competitive but can create inequities between expatriates and between assignees in different countries. Additional challenges include currency fluctuations, pay equity with local staff, and dual-career couples, where the spouse's career must also be considered.

Key points:

  • International compensation must keep expatriates' standard of living at home, motivate them to accept assignments, and ensure fairness relative to host-country employees.

  • Additional challenges include currency fluctuations, pay equity with local staff, and dual-career couples, where the spouse's career must also be considered.
