Q1. What is strategy? Explain Mintzberg's 5 Ps of strategy.10 Marks

Answer:Strategy is the grand design through which an organisation achieves its long-term objectives using its resources in a dynamic environment. The word derives from the Greek strategos, the art of the general. Glueck defined strategy as a unified, comprehensive and integrated plan relating strategic advantage to environmental challenges; Chandler described it as the determination of long-term goals, courses of action and resource allocation; and Porter called it the creation of a unique and valued position involving a different set of activities. Mintzberg argued that strategy can be understood through five Ps. As a Plan, it is an intended, consciously formulated course of action for the future. As a Ploy, it is a manoeuvre to outwit competitors. As a Pattern, it is a consistent stream of actions visible in what the organisation actually does, whether intended or not. As a Position, it locates the organisation in its environment, such as a niche market placement. As a Perspective, it reflects the organisation's fundamental way of seeing the world. Apple's consistent premium differentiation and ecosystem strategy over decades is a classic pattern. In conclusion, the 5 Ps show that strategy is simultaneously intended and emergent, external and internal, and must be distinguished from tactics, which are short-term and specific.

Key points:

  • Answer: Strategy is the grand design through which an organisation achieves its long-term objectives using its resources in a dynamic environment.
  • As a Position, it locates the organisation in its environment, such as a niche market placement.
  • The word derives from the Greek strategos, the art of the general.

Takeaway: In conclusion, the 5 Ps show that strategy is simultaneously intended and emergent, external and internal, and must be distinguished from tactics, which are short-term and specific.

Q2. Explain the steps of the strategic management process.10 Marks

Answer:The strategic management process converts intent into results through five steps. Step one, Strategic Intent, establishes the vision (where we want to be), the mission (what business we are in), and long-term objectives and goals. Step two, Environmental Analysis, scans the external environment using PESTLE and the EFE matrix and the internal environment using SWOT and VRIN analysis, revealing opportunities, threats, strengths and weaknesses. Step three, Strategy Formulation, develops business-level strategies such as cost leadership, differentiation and focus, and corporate-level strategies such as growth, stability and retrenchment. Step four, Strategy Implementation, translates plans into action by allocating financial, human, physical and technological resources, designing the organisational structure and managing change. Step five, Evaluation and Control, monitors performance against standards using the Balanced Scorecard and KPIs, and takes corrective action. The process is continuous: environmental changes feed back into reformulated intent. Infosys's “Win in the flat world” intent guided its globalisation, and Amazon's customer-centric vision shapes every strategic decision. In conclusion, the process ensures that strategy is analysed, formulated, implemented and evaluated in a disciplined, repeating cycle rather than remaining an aspiration.

Key points:

  • Answer: The strategic management process converts intent into results through five steps.
  • Step three, Strategy Formulation, develops business-level strategies such as cost leadership, differentiation and focus, and corporate-level strategies such as growth, stability and retrenchment.
  • Step one, Strategic Intent, establishes the vision (where we want to be), the mission (what business we are in), and long-term objectives and goals.

Takeaway: In conclusion, the process ensures that strategy is analysed, formulated, implemented and evaluated in a disciplined, repeating cycle rather than remaining an aspiration.

Q3. Explain Porter's Five Forces model with the help of the Indian telecom industry.10 Marks

Answer:Porter's Five Forces model, developed in 1979, judges an industry's attractiveness and profit potential through five forces: threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitutes and competitive rivalry. The stronger the forces, the lower the profit potential. New entry depends on barriers such as economies of scale, brand loyalty, capital requirements, switching costs and regulation. Supplier power is high when few suppliers dominate or switching costs are high; buyer power is high when products are undifferentiated and buyers are price-sensitive; substitute threat depends on relative price and performance; and rivalry intensifies with many balanced competitors, slow growth and high exit barriers. Indian telecom illustrates all five forces changing at once: when Jio entered in 2016 with massive investment, rivalry became hyper-competitive and price wars left Vodafone Idea struggling; buyer power rose with cheap data plans; VoIP substitutes undercut traditional voice; tower sharing reduced supplier power; and capital barriers were breached by a deep-pocketed entrant. By contrast, Indian FMCG enjoys low new-entry threat because of strong brand loyalty and distribution barriers. In conclusion, the five forces framework helps firms assess industry attractiveness and position themselves before committing resources.

Key points:

  • Answer: Porter's Five Forces model, developed in 1979, judges an industry's attractiveness and profit potential through five forces: threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitutes and competitive rivalry.
  • New entry depends on barriers such as economies of scale, brand loyalty, capital requirements, switching costs and regulation.
  • The stronger the forces, the lower the profit potential.

Takeaway: In conclusion, the five forces framework helps firms assess industry attractiveness and position themselves before committing resources.

Q4. Explain the Resource-Based View (RBV) and the VRIN framework.10 Marks

Answer:The Resource-Based View is an inside-out perspective of strategy: it holds that a firm's internal resources and capabilities, not industry structure, are the primary source of competitive advantage. While the I/O model looks outside-in and says the external environment determines strategy, RBV says firms should analyse what they already possess and build advantage on core competencies. Resources are classified as tangible (property, equipment, cash), intangible (brand, patents, culture), capabilities (skills in using assets) and core competencies (unique strengths meeting the VRIN criteria). Barney's VRIN framework specifies that a resource yields sustainable advantage only if it is Valuable (exploits opportunities or neutralises threats), Rare (few competitors possess it), Inimitable (not easily copied) and Non-substitutable (no equivalent alternative exists). Tata Group's core competency of trust and brand reputation built over 150 years fits perfectly: valuable, rare across Indian industry, inimitable because history cannot be replicated, and non-substitutable. Supporting tools include Porter's value chain, which shows where value is created in primary and support activities, and the IFE matrix, which quantifies internal strength. In conclusion, RBV and VRIN direct managers to develop and protect resources that competitors cannot imitate.

  • Answer: The Resource-Based View is an inside-out perspective of strategy: it holds that a firm's internal resources and capabilities, not industry structure, are the primary source of competitive advantage.
