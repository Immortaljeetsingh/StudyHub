  • Q1(a) Solved - Project, Its Characteristics and Life Cycle

    Answer: A project is a temporary, unique, resource-consuming endeavour with defined objectives, and its life cycle runs through initiation, planning, execution and closure.

    A project is a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service or result. It has a defined beginning and end, a clear objective, and consumes resources such as money, people, materials and time. Examples include building a metro line, launching a new product or organizing a film shoot.

    Characteristics: (1) Uniqueness - every project is different; (2) temporary nature - a fixed start and finish; (3) defined objectives - specific deliverables and acceptance criteria; (4) interdependence - activities are linked in a logical sequence; (5) use of resources with a budget; (6) risk and uncertainty - outcomes are never fully certain; (7) a dedicated team and a project manager who coordinates effort. Because projects are unique, their execution always involves some uncertainty, unlike routine operations which repeat the same process daily.

    The project life cycle has four phases. In the initiation phase, the idea is evaluated, objectives are set and a project charter is prepared. In the planning phase, scope, schedule, budget, quality, resources and risks are planned in detail - this is where networks, work breakdown structures and budgets are made. In the execution phase, the plan is implemented, teams do the work and progress is monitored against the plan. In the closure phase, the deliverables are handed over, lessons learned are recorded and the project is formally closed and audited. Managing these phases well is the essence of project management.

    Key points to remember: project = temporary + unique + objective; life cycle = initiation, planning, execution, closure; each phase ends with reviewable deliverables.

    Takeaway: Lead with temporary + unique + objective, then walk the four phases with one deliverable per phase (charter, plan, deliverables, handover).

  • Q1(b) Solved - Functions of a Project Manager

    Answer: The project manager's key functions are planning, organizing, staffing, directing, controlling, coordinating and risk management, with communication running through all of them.

    A project manager is the single person accountable for the success of a project. His key functions are: planning - defining scope, objectives, work breakdown, schedule and budget; organizing - assembling and structuring the project team and allocating resources to activities; staffing - recruiting, training and motivating team members; directing and leading - guiding the team, communicating expectations and resolving conflicts; controlling - comparing actual progress with the plan, measuring variances and taking corrective action; coordinating - integrating the work of departments, contractors and vendors; and risk management - identifying threats, planning responses and monitoring them. He is also the communication hub - reporting status to the steering committee, sponsors and stakeholders.

    Beyond these, a project manager must manage scope changes formally, ensure quality standards are met, manage stakeholder expectations and document lessons learned at closure. Modern project managers also need soft skills such as negotiation, conflict resolution and leadership, because projects are delivered through people. In the Indian context, project managers in construction, IT and infrastructure combine technical knowledge with PERT/CPM tools, earned value analysis and risk registers to keep projects on schedule and within budget. The functions mirror general management, but with a sharper focus on time, cost and quality targets.

    Key points to remember: core functions - planning, organizing, staffing, directing, controlling, coordinating; add risk and communication management; the PM is accountable for time, cost and quality.

    Takeaway: Memorise the function chain (planning to risk management) and show each function tied to time, cost and quality - the PM's three accountability pillars.

  • Q2(a) Solved - Project Feasibility Analysis

    Answer: Feasibility analysis tests a proposed project on four dimensions - market, technical, financial and economic - and the project proceeds only when all four are positive.

    Feasibility analysis examines whether a proposed project is worth undertaking and can be implemented successfully. It is done before resources are committed and covers four dimensions.

    Market feasibility studies demand for the product or service: total market size, growth rate, customer segments, competition, pricing and sales projections. It answers: will there be enough customers at the planned price? Technical feasibility checks whether the project can be built and operated with available technology, skills, raw materials, land, water, power and infrastructure, and whether the technology is proven, maintainable and affordable. Financial feasibility determines whether the project is financially viable: it estimates total investment, cost of production, working capital, revenue and cash flows, and applies criteria such as Net Present Value, Internal Rate of Return, Payback Period and Break-even Point. Economic feasibility looks beyond the firm: it evaluates social cost and benefit - employment generation, foreign exchange earnings, regional development and effects on the environment - which matters for government and public-sector appraisals.

    A project passes feasibility only if all four dimensions are positive. For example, a solar power plant may have good technical feasibility but fail financial feasibility if tariffs are too low, or pass financially but be rejected on economic grounds if it displaces farmland. A feasibility report consolidates these studies into a go/no-go recommendation for the board.

    Key points to remember: four feasibilities - market, technical, financial, economic; use NPV, IRR, payback and BEP in financial appraisal; economic feasibility adds social cost-benefit.

    Takeaway: Present the four feasibilities as a go/no-go sequence - NPV, IRR and payback under financial, and social cost-benefit under economic.

  • Q2(b) Solved - Project Charter

    Answer: A project charter is the sponsor-issued authorisation document that carries the business case, scope, budget, milestones, PM authority and approval signatures.

    A project charter is a short formal document that authorizes the project to start and gives the project manager the authority to use organizational resources. It is issued by the project sponsor or top management after the feasibility study, and it formally links the project to the strategy of the organization. The charter answers: why this project, who is in charge, what will be delivered, and within what limits.

    Key elements of a charter: project name and business case (the reason and expected benefits); objectives and success criteria; scope - what is included and excluded; key stakeholders and their expectations; the project manager's name and authority level; the sponsor; budget estimate and schedule summary; major milestones; assumptions, constraints and initial risks; and approval signatures. The charter is not the detailed plan - it is the starting document that makes the project official. It is especially important because it prevents scope creep later: anything outside the charter needs a formal change request. In organizations like infrastructure companies and IT firms, the charter is the first gate in the project approval process, and no major spending is allowed before it is signed. It also helps the team stay aligned: every member knows the objective, the limits and who to report to.

    Key points to remember: charter = authorization document from the sponsor; contains business case, scope, PM authority, budget, milestones, assumptions and approvals; the first gate before spending.

    Takeaway: The charter is the first gate - it authorises spending, names the PM and prevents scope creep until a formal change request is raised.

  • Q3(a) Solved - Critical Path Method Numerical

    Answer: Forward and backward passes give a 15-day project with critical path A-C-F-H (1-2-4-6-7) and total floats of 2, 4, 2 and 2 days for B, D, E and G.

    Step 1 - Draw the network: activities and durations: A: 1-2 (4), B: 1-3 (2), C: 2-4 (5), D: 3-4 (3), E: 3-5 (6), F: 4-6 (4), G: 5-6 (3), H: 6-7 (2).

    Step 2 - Forward pass (earliest times): E1 = 0. E2 = 0+4 = 4. E3 = 0+2 = 2. E4 = max(4+5, 2+3) = max(9, 5) = 9. E5 = 2+6 = 8. E6 = max(9+4, 8+3) = max(13, 11) = 13. E7 = 13+2 = 15. Project duration = 15 days.

    Step 3 - Backward pass (latest times): L7 = 15. L6 = 13. L5 = 13-3 = 10. L4 = 13-4 = 9. L3 = min(9-3, 10-6) = min(6, 4) = 4. L2 = 9-5 = 4. L1 = 0.

    Step 4 - Floats (total float = LS - ES): A: 0-0 = 0; B: 2-0 = 2; C: 4-4 = 0; D: 6-2 = 4; E: 4-2 = 2; F: 9-9 = 0; G: 10-8 = 2; H: 13-13 = 0.

    Step 5 - Critical path: activities with zero float are A, C, F, H, so the critical path is 1-2-4-6-7 with duration 4 + 5 + 4 + 2 = 15 days. Activities B, D, E and G are non-critical and can be delayed without delaying the project: B by 2 days, D by 4 days, E by 2 days and G by 2 days. Their total floats are 2, 4, 2 and 2 days respectively.

    Answer: project duration = 15 days; critical path = A-C-F-H (1-2-4-6-7); floats: B = 2, D = 4, E = 2, G = 2 days.

    Key points to remember: forward pass takes maxima, backward pass takes minima; total float = LS - ES; zero float marks critical activities; project duration = earliest finish of the last event.

    Takeaway: Forward pass takes maxima, backward takes minima, float = LS - ES, and zero float on A, C, F, H is the critical-path signature.

  • Q3(b) Solved - Total Float vs Free Float

    Answer: Total float delays the project safely while free float delays only the successor's early start - total float is always ≥ free float and both are zero on the critical path.

    Total float (also called slack) is the amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the overall project completion date. It is computed as LS - ES or LF - EF. Free float is the amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the early start of any succeeding activity; it is computed as the earliest start of the immediate successor minus the earliest finish of the activity. The difference lies in whose schedule is protected: total float protects the project, free float protects the successor activities.

    For example, in the network above, activity D has total float 4 days and free float 4 days, because its successor F does not start until day 9, four days after D finishes at day 5. Activity E, by contrast, has total float 2 days but free float 0, because its successor G starts the moment E finishes (ES of G = EF of E = 8). Total float is always greater than or equal to free float, and both are zero for critical path activities. Managers use total float for resource levelling - delaying non-critical work to smooth resource use - and use free float when they want to avoid any impact on downstream activities. Interfering float is the difference between total and free float.

    Key points to remember: total float = LS - ES (protects project); free float = ES of successor - EF of activity (protects successor); critical activities have zero float.

    Takeaway: Compute TF = LS - ES and FF = ES of successor - EF of activity, and cite the D-versus-E example from the network above (D has free float, E has none).

  • Q4(a) Solved - Project Crashing Numerical

    Answer: Crashing A and H by one day each (Rs 1,000/day) shortens the project to 13 days at a minimum extra cost of Rs 2,000, for a total project cost of Rs 12,000.

    Step 1 - Crash cost per day of each critical activity: A: (3,000 - 2,000)/(4 - 3) = Rs 1,000/day; C: (5,500 - 4,000)/(5 - 4) = Rs 1,500/day; F: (4,200 - 3,000)/(4 - 3) = Rs 1,200/day; H: (2,000 - 1,000)/(2 - 1) = Rs 1,000/day.

    Step 2 - Normal project cost: critical activities' normal cost = 2,000 + 4,000 + 3,000 + 1,000 = Rs 10,000. Normal duration = 15 days (path A-C-F-H = 4+5+4+2).

    Step 3 - Crash day 1: crash the cheapest critical activity. A and H both cost Rs 1,000/day. Crash A by 1 day (4 → 3). New duration = 14 days. Critical path A-C-F-H is still 3+5+4+2 = 14 days, longer than the other path 1-3-5-6-7 = 2+6+3+2 = 13 days, so it remains critical.

    Step 4 - Crash day 2: A has reached its crash limit, so the next cheapest is H at Rs 1,000/day. Crash H by 1 day (2 → 1). New duration = 13 days. Check: A-C-F-H = 3+5+4+1 = 13 days and path B-E-G-H = 2+6+3+1 = 12 days, so no new critical path appears.

    Answer: crash A and H by one day each; minimum crash cost = 1,000 + 1,000 = Rs 2,000; new project duration = 13 days; total project cost = 10,000 + 2,000 = Rs 12,000. (Had we crashed C or F first, the cost would be higher, so A and H are optimal.)

    Key points to remember: crash cost/day = (crash cost - normal cost)/(normal time - crash time); always crash the cheapest critical activity; watch crash limits and recheck the critical path after every crash.

    Takeaway: Crash cost per day = (crash cost - normal cost)/(normal time - crash time); always take the cheapest critical activity and recheck the path after every day crashed.

  • Q4(b) Solved - Project Crashing: Meaning and Use

    Answer: Crashing shortens a project by adding resources to critical activities at extra cost, and it is used when early completion saves more than the crash cost - for deadlines, launches or penalty clauses.

    Project crashing is the technique of reducing the total duration of a project by expediting activities, usually by adding extra resources such as more workers, overtime, extra machinery or faster equipment, at additional cost. Each activity has a normal time with normal cost and a crash time (the minimum possible duration) with crash cost; crashing an activity shortens it only up to its crash limit, and the cost of saving one day is called the crash cost per day. The objective is to shorten the project at the lowest possible extra cost, so we crash the critical path activities with the lowest crash cost per day first, and we never crash a non-critical activity unless the critical path shifts.

    Crashing is used when the project must finish earlier than planned - for example, to meet a contractual deadline with penalty clauses, to launch a product before a competitor, to complete construction before the monsoon season, or to release funds early. It is justified only while the saving from early completion exceeds the crash cost; beyond that point the trade-off is not profitable. Direct costs rise with crashing while indirect costs such as supervision, interest and overheads fall with shorter duration, so the optimum duration is where total cost is minimum. Crashing is part of the time-cost trade-off analysis in Unit 7 of MMPO-02 and should always be done on critical activities with the cheapest marginal cost.

    Key points to remember: crashing = time-cost trade-off; crash cheapest critical activity first; respect crash limits; optimum where total (direct + indirect) cost is minimum.

    Takeaway: Crashing is a time-cost trade-off - crash the cheapest critical activity up to its limit while the saving from early completion still exceeds the crash cost.

  • Q5(a) Solved - Earned Value Analysis Numerical

    Answer: PV = Rs 2,00,000, EV = Rs 1,75,000 and AC = Rs 2,40,000 give CV = -Rs 65,000, SV = -Rs 25,000, CPI = 0.73, SPI = 0.875 and EAC ≈ Rs 6,85,000 - the project is over budget and behind schedule.

    Step 1 - Compute the three key values: Planned Value (PV) = 40% of BAC = 0.40 × 5,00,000 = Rs 2,00,000 (work that should have been done). Earned Value (EV) = 35% of BAC = 0.35 × 5,00,000 = Rs 1,75,000 (work actually done, in money terms). Actual Cost (AC) = Rs 2,40,000.

    Step 2 - Variances: Cost Variance CV = EV - AC = 1,75,000 - 2,40,000 = -Rs 65,000 (negative = cost overrun). Schedule Variance SV = EV - PV = 1,75,000 - 2,00,000 = -Rs 25,000 (negative = behind schedule).

    Step 3 - Performance indices: CPI = EV/AC = 1,75,000/2,40,000 = 0.73 (we get only 73 paise of value for every rupee spent). SPI = EV/PV = 1,75,000/2,00,000 = 0.875 (project is running at 87.5% of the planned speed).

    Step 4 - Estimate at Completion: EAC = BAC/CPI = 5,00,000/0.73 ≈ Rs 6,85,000. If the same efficiency continues, the project will cost about Rs 6.85 lakh against a budget of Rs 5 lakh.

    Comment: the project is in trouble on both fronts. CV and SV are both negative, and CPI and SPI are both below 1. The cost overrun of Rs 65,000 and schedule delay must be investigated - possible causes are poor estimation, material price rise, idle resources or rework. Management should either fast-track, crash critical activities, or re-baseline, because at the current CPI the project will exceed its budget by roughly Rs 1.85 lakh.

    Key points to remember: EV = % complete × BAC; CV = EV - AC, SV = EV - PV; CPI = EV/AC, SPI = EV/PV; CPI < 1 and SPI < 1 mean over budget and behind schedule; EAC = BAC/CPI.

    Takeaway: Quote EV = % complete × BAC, CV = EV - AC, SV = EV - PV, and both indices below 1 mean corrective action is needed now.

  • Q5(b) Solved - Project Management Information System (PMIS)

    Answer: A PMIS is the set of tools, procedures and data that collects, processes and communicates project information - schedules, costs, risks and progress - into reports for decisions.

    A Project Management Information System (PMIS) is the organized set of tools, procedures and data used by the project team to collect, store, process and communicate project information - schedules, costs, resources, progress, issues, risks and documents. In modern practice it is usually software such as Microsoft Project, Primavera P6 or cloud platforms, but it also includes the manual reports, registers and meetings of smaller projects. The PMIS takes inputs like activity status, actual costs and resource utilization, processes them, and produces outputs like updated schedules, earned value reports, cash-flow statements and exception reports that flag activities behind schedule or over budget.

    Benefits: single source of truth for the project - everyone works from the same updated plan; faster and more accurate reporting, so managers detect variances early and take corrective action; better tracking of cost and schedule performance through EVM calculations; transparent communication with stakeholders through dashboards and reports; systematic documentation of changes, issues and risks; and easier audit trails at project closure. A PMIS supports the monitoring and control function of the project manager: without timely, accurate information, decisions are based on guesswork. The effectiveness of a PMIS depends on discipline - data must be entered honestly and on time - otherwise even the best software produces misleading reports.

    Key points to remember: PMIS = people + procedures + software for project data; key outputs - updated schedules, EVM reports, exception reports; quality depends on timely, accurate data entry.

    Takeaway: A PMIS gives one source of truth and early variance detection, but its value depends entirely on honest, timely data entry.

  • Q6(a) Solved - Project Risk Management Process

    Answer: Risk management runs through four steps - identification, analysis (qualitative then quantitative), response planning (avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept) and monitoring - all recorded in the risk register.

    Risk management in projects is a continuous process with four main steps. Risk identification finds and documents potential risks that could affect the project - through brainstorming, checklists, expert interviews, SWOT analysis and past project records. Risks are classified as threats (negative) or opportunities (positive) and recorded in a risk register. Risk analysis assesses each risk on probability and impact. Qualitative analysis ranks risks using a probability-impact matrix (e.g., high, medium, low) to decide priorities; quantitative analysis goes further, using techniques such as sensitivity analysis, decision trees or Monte Carlo simulation to estimate the effect on the project schedule and budget in numbers.

    Risk response planning decides actions for the high-priority risks. For threats, the strategies are: avoid (remove the cause), mitigate (reduce probability or impact), transfer (shift the risk to a contractor or insurer) and accept (acknowledge and budget for it). For opportunities, the parallel strategies are exploit, enhance, share and accept. Each risk gets an owner and a budget. Risk monitoring and control runs through the project: risks are tracked, triggers are watched, responses are executed when risks materialize, new risks are identified, and the register is updated at every review meeting. The goal is not to eliminate all risk - that is impossible - but to ensure risks are known, prioritized and managed before they turn into problems, keeping surprises to a minimum.

    Key points to remember: four steps - identify, analyze (qualitative then quantitative), respond (avoid/mitigate/transfer/accept), monitor; the risk register is the living document of this process.

    Takeaway: Walk the four steps as a cycle and attach the four response strategies plus the risk register as the living record of every identified risk.

  • Q6(b) Solved - Risk vs Uncertainty and the Risk Register

    Answer: Risk is measurable uncertainty - outcomes with estimable probabilities - while uncertainty has unknown outcomes or probabilities, and the risk register documents every risk with probability, impact, response, owner and status.

    Risk exists when the possible outcomes of a future event are known and their probabilities can be estimated - for example, the chance of rain delaying a construction project can be estimated from weather history. Uncertainty exists when the outcomes themselves, or their probabilities, cannot be known - for example, how a new government regulation will affect a project, or how a customer will react to an untested feature. In short, risk is measurable uncertainty; uncertainty is risk without measurable probabilities. Project managers respond differently: risks can be quantified, insured or transferred, while uncertainty is handled through flexibility, buffers, contingency funds, monitoring and scenario planning. Decision-makers can use probability calculations for risk but must rely on judgment for uncertainty.

    A risk register is a document (or software table) that records every identified risk of the project. For each risk it lists: an ID and description; category; probability and impact ratings and overall score; the response strategy; the risk owner; trigger conditions; and current status. The register is created during identification, updated at every review, and becomes the main input for risk monitoring and control. It is also reviewed at project closure so lessons learned are available for future projects. A well-maintained risk register is the simplest and most effective risk-management tool available to a project manager.

    Key points to remember: risk = measurable probability; uncertainty = unknown outcomes or probabilities; risk register = ID, probability, impact, response, owner, status for every risk.

    Takeaway: One line separates them - probabilities known or not - and the register's columns (ID, probability, impact, response, owner, status) answer the second half of the question.
