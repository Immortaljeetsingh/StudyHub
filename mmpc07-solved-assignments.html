Q1. Explain the process of communication with the help of the Shannon-Weaver model.(10 Marks)

Answer: Communication is a systematic, cyclical sequence in which a sender encodes an idea into symbols, transmits it through a channel, the receiver decodes it, and feedback completes the loop. The Shannon-Weaver model (1948) is the classic linear representation of this process. Its elements are the information source (the sender's idea), the transmitter that encodes the idea, the channel or medium of transfer, the receiver that decodes the message, and the destination that finally understands it. The model introduced the concept of noise, any interference that distorts the message during transmission. Noise can be physical (construction sounds), psychological (prejudice), semantic (jargon), technical (poor internet), organizational (too many hierarchy levels), or cultural (different norms). The model stresses that communication is not complete until feedback is received; feedback transforms one-way transmission into two-way communication and lets the sender confirm understanding. In Indian organizations, an instruction that passes through five hierarchy levels, as at Indian Railways, is a classic example of organizational noise distorting meaning, which is why choosing the right channel and genuinely encouraging feedback are essential for effective communication.

Key points:

  • Communication is a systematic, cyclical sequence in which a sender encodes an idea into symbols, transmits it through a channel, the receiver decodes it, and feedback completes the loop.
  • In Indian organizations, an instruction that passes through five hierarchy levels, as at Indian Railways, is a classic example of organizational noise distorting meaning, which is why choosing the right channel and genuinely encouraging feedback are essential for effective communication.
  • The Shannon-Weaver model (1948) is the classic linear representation of this process.

Takeaway: In Indian organizations, an instruction that passes through five hierarchy levels, as at Indian Railways, is a classic example of organizational noise distorting meaning, which is why choosing the right channel and genuinely encouraging feedback are essential for effective communication.

Q2. Distinguish between verbal and non-verbal communication. Explain Mehrabian's 7-38-55 rule.(10 Marks)

Answer: Verbal communication uses spoken or written words; it is direct, explicit, and recordable, and includes speeches, emails, letters, reports, and phone calls. Non-verbal communication uses body language, facial expressions, gestures, tone, and even silence; it is subtle, often unconscious, and culture-specific. Examples include eye contact, posture, handshake, and dress. Mehrabian's rule states that when feelings and attitudes are communicated face to face, only 7% of the emotional impact comes from words, 38% from vocal tone, and 55% from body language, meaning 93% of emotional communication is non-verbal. The non-verbal dimensions are kinesics (gestures and movement), proxemics (personal space), haptics (touch), chronemics (use of time), and paralanguage (voice quality). Because non-verbal cues are culture-specific, the same gesture can carry different meanings: the Indian head nod meaning "I hear you" is often misread by Western managers as "I agree." Managers must therefore align their verbal message with their body language, because when the two conflict, listeners believe the non-verbal signal.

Key points:

  • Verbal communication uses spoken or written words; it is direct, explicit, and recordable, and includes speeches, emails, letters, reports, and phone calls.
  • Examples include eye contact, posture, handshake, and dress.
  • Non-verbal communication uses body language, facial expressions, gestures, tone, and even silence; it is subtle, often unconscious, and culture-specific.

Q3. What is listening? Discuss the types of listening and the barriers to effective listening.(10 Marks)

Answer: Listening is the active process of receiving, constructing meaning from, and responding to spoken messages. It is not the same as hearing; hearing is passive, while listening requires conscious effort. We spend about 45% of our communication time listening, yet immediately after hearing we retain only 50% of a message, and after 48 hours retention drops to 25%, half of which is wrong. The main types are active listening (full engagement with feedback), passive listening (minimal engagement), empathic listening (understanding feelings, as a counselor does), critical listening (evaluating content, as an investor does during an earnings call), and appreciative listening (listening for enjoyment). Barriers fall into two groups: external barriers such as noise, poor acoustics, and phone notifications, and internal barriers such as prejudice, emotional state, fatigue, and rehearsing one's response instead of listening. The SOLER model offers a practical remedy: sit squarely, keep an open posture, lean forward, maintain eye contact, and respond. Since most managers listen at only about 25% efficiency, practising these techniques substantially reduces miscommunication and builds trust in the workplace.

Key points:

  • Listening is the active process of receiving, constructing meaning from, and responding to spoken messages.
  • Barriers fall into two groups: external barriers such as noise, poor acoustics, and phone notifications, and internal barriers such as prejudice, emotional state, fatigue, and rehearsing one's response instead of listening.
  • It is not the same as hearing; hearing is passive, while listening requires conscious effort.

Q4. What do you understand by ethical communication? Explain its principles and common unethical practices.(10 Marks)

Answer: Ethical communication is the honest, responsible, fair, and respectful exchange of information, marked by transparency, accountability, and concern for the impact of messages on all stakeholders. The five principles are captured by the mnemonic TRRF-T: Truthfulness (sharing honest information), Respect (preserving the dignity of others), Responsibility (owning the consequences of one's message), Fairness (communicating without bias), and Transparency (being open and clear). Common unethical practices include lying, exaggeration or overstating facts without evidence, omission or withholding key information, manipulation through fake urgency, plagiarism, and spin, such as describing layoffs as "right-sizing" in a press release. Ethics also governs privacy: organizations must protect personal data under the IT Act 2000, safeguard trade secrets through NDAs, honour whistleblowing obligations under the Companies Act 2013, and obtain informed consent before sharing information. In India, the Companies Act 2013 requires qualifying companies to spend 2% of average net profit on CSR, and communication about such activities must be accurate and verifiable, since misleading claims invite SEBI penalties.

Key points:

  • Ethical communication is the honest, responsible, fair, and respectful exchange of information, marked by transparency, accountability, and concern for the impact of messages on all stakeholders.
  • Common unethical practices include lying, exaggeration or overstating facts without evidence, omission or withholding key information, manipulation through fake urgency, plagiarism, and spin, such as describing layoffs as "right-sizing" in a press release.
  • Ethics also governs privacy: organizations must protect personal data under the IT Act 2000, safeguard trade secrets through NDAs, honour whistleblowing obligations under the Companies Act 2013, and obtain informed consent before sharing information.
