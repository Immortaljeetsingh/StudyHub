Solved Assignment Questions & Answers

Q1. Define industrial relations. Explain the four approaches to IR with Indian examples.

Answer: Industrial relations refers to the relationship between employers and employees (and their organisations) and the government, arising out of employment. The ILO defines IR as covering the relationship between the state on the one hand and the employers' and workers' organisations on the other, and the relationship between the occupational organisations themselves. The four approaches to IR differ in their view of the organisation and of conflict. The unitary approach sees the organisation as one happy team with common goals; conflict is abnormal and avoidable, and unions are unnecessary (Fox, 1966); Infosys' "One Infosys" culture and TCS' employee-first approach illustrate this. The pluralistic approach sees multiple interest groups whose differing interests make conflict natural and inevitable; it is managed through collective bargaining and grievance procedures (Fox, Clegg); Tata Steel's union-management partnership is the classic Indian example. The Marxist approach sees IR as class struggle in which capital exploits labour; conflict is structural and inherent in capitalism (Marx, Engels); CITU-led militant unionism in Kerala represents this. The systems approach treats IR as a sub-system of society regulated through rules (Dunlop, 1958); Indian tripartite bodies like the ILC and SLC reflect it.

Key points:

  • Industrial relations refers to the relationship between employers and employees (and their organisations) and the government, arising out of employment.
  • The unitary approach sees the organisation as one happy team with common goals; conflict is abnormal and avoidable, and unions are unnecessary (Fox, 1966); Infosys' "One Infosys" culture and TCS' employee-first approach illustrate this.
  • The ILO defines IR as covering the relationship between the state on the one hand and the employers' and workers' organisations on the other, and the relationship between the occupational organisations themselves.

Takeaway: The systems approach treats IR as a sub-system of society regulated through rules (Dunlop, 1958); Indian tripartite bodies like the ILC and SLC reflect it.

Q2. Explain Dunlop's Systems Theory of industrial relations.

Answer: John T. Dunlop (1958) proposed that an industrial relations system consists of three actors: workers and their organisations, employers and their associations, and the government and its agencies. These actors operate within three contexts: the technological context (the nature of work and production), the market context (competitive forces and wage levels), and the power distribution context (the relative power of the actors). The actors share a common ideology, a set of beliefs that binds the system together; this ideology can be cooperation-oriented or conflict-oriented. Through their interaction, the actors produce two types of rules: substantive rules, which cover wages, hours, and working conditions, and procedural rules, which govern how disputes are resolved and how grievances are handled. The framework can be remembered as C-M-I-R: Contexts, three Actors, Ideology, Rules. Dunlop's model helps analyse Indian IR, where the state plays a strong third-actor role through labour legislation, tripartite forums such as the Indian Labour Conference, and adjudication machinery. The Maruti Suzuki Manesar dispute of 2012 illustrates how technological, market, and power contexts combined with a breakdown of shared ideology to produce conflict and massive production losses.

Key points:

  • Dunlop (1958) proposed that an industrial relations system consists of three actors: workers and their organisations, employers and their associations, and the government and its agencies.
  • Dunlop's model helps analyse Indian IR, where the state plays a strong third-actor role through labour legislation, tripartite forums such as the Indian Labour Conference, and adjudication machinery.
  • These actors operate within three contexts: the technological context (the nature of work and production), the market context (competitive forces and wage levels), and the power distribution context (the relative power of the actors).

Takeaway: The framework can be remembered as C-M-I-R: Contexts, three Actors, Ideology, Rules.

Q3. Discuss the key milestones in the evolution of industrial relations in India.

Answer: Indian IR has evolved through four phases. In the colonial phase (1875-1947), workers in Mumbai textile mills and Kolkata jute mills faced long hours, low wages, and child labour; the first formal union, the Madras Labour Union, was formed by B.P. Wadia in 1918, AITUC was founded in 1920 under Lala Lajpat Rai, and the Trade Unions Act of 1926 provided a legal framework for union registration with a minimum of seven members. The Whitley Commission (1929) recommended conciliation machinery. In the post-independence phase (1947-1991), the Industrial Disputes Act 1947, the Factories Act 1948, and the Minimum Wages Act 1948 created a state-led tripartite model, with PSUs as model employers and the voluntary Code of Discipline adopted in 1958. The liberalisation phase (1991-2014) under the New Economic Policy brought disinvestment, FDI, outsourcing, contract labour, and declining union power. Since 2014, four new labour codes (Code on Wages, IR Code, Social Security Code, OSH Code) consolidate 29 laws, increase employer flexibility, and include gig workers. Key years to remember: 1918, 1926, 1947, 1948, 1958, 1991, 2020.

Q4. What is collective bargaining? Distinguish between distributive and integrative bargaining with examples.

Answer: Collective bargaining is the process of negotiation between employers (or employers' organisations) and workers' representatives (trade unions) for determining terms and conditions of employment, as defined by the ILO. The term was coined by Sidney and Beatrice Webb in 1897. There are four types of bargaining: distributive, integrative, attitudinal, and intra-organisational. Distributive bargaining is win-lose and zero-sum; the pie is fixed, so one side's gain is the other's loss. It is competitive and positional, with information withheld, and it damages trust. The failed wage negotiations at Maruti Suzuki Manesar in 2012, where hardline positions led to violence and a Rs 2,500 crore production loss, illustrate distributive bargaining. Integrative bargaining is win-win; it expands the pie through creative problem-solving focused on interests rather than positions, with open information sharing, and it builds long-term trust. The Tata Steel 2019 settlement, which exchanged productivity-linked pay for job security covering over 15,000 workers, is a classic integrative example. Collective bargaining serves five functions: bipartism, flexibility, peace, industrial democracy, and equalisation of power between capital and labour.

Key points:

  • Collective bargaining is the process of negotiation between employers (or employers' organisations) and workers' representatives (trade unions) for determining terms and conditions of employment, as defined by the ILO.
  • The Tata Steel 2019 settlement, which exchanged productivity-linked pay for job security covering over 15,000 workers, is a classic integrative example.
  • The term was coined by Sidney and Beatrice Webb in 1897.
