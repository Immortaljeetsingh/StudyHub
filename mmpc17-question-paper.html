Solved Answers - Model Answers for Every Question

Answer to Q1: Corporate Management and the Corporate Planning Process

Answer: Corporate management is planning, implementation, and evaluation and control of strategy — it evolved through five paradigm shifts, remembered as A-P-E-C-C, from adhocism to integrated corporate management.

Corporate management encompasses three dimensions: planning, implementation, and evaluation and control of plans. Peter Drucker defined corporate planning as "a continuous process of making entrepreneurial decisions systematically with the best possible knowledge of their futurity", and Hussey added that it is "not a technique, it is a complete way of running a business". Its evolution passed through five paradigm shifts, remembered as "A-P-E-C-C". Adhocism (till the 1930s) meant reactive, exigency-driven decisions. Planned policy followed the Great Depression, anticipating contingencies. The environment-strategy interface recognized that strategy must cope with environmental forces. Corporate planning formalized environmental appraisal into strategic alternatives. Finally, corporate management added implementation and control to planning. Reliance Industries under Mukesh Ambani shows the shift from adhoc decisions to systematic corporate management — Jio’s entry into telecom was planned years in advance, implemented through massive infrastructure investment, and evaluated through subscriber-growth metrics.

The corporate planning process runs in five steps: (1) formulation of strategic intent; (2) environmental appraisal; (3) generation of strategic alternatives; (4) evaluation of alternatives; and (5) decisions on strategy, policies and programmes. Implementation then happens through projects, procedures, resource allocation, structural design, behavioural change and functional integration.

Key points to remember: corporate management = planning + implementation + evaluation and control; five paradigm shifts = Adhocism, Planned policy, Environment-strategy, Corporate planning, Corporate management; five planning steps from intent to decisions.

Takeaway: Corporate management is the full loop — plan, implement, evaluate — and it evolved from firefighting (adhocism) into systematic, future-facing strategy.

Answer to Q2: Ansoff's Grid and Corporate Policy Types

Answer: Ansoff's product-market expansion grid is a 2x2 matrix of current/new products against current/new markets, giving four growth strategies with risk rising from market penetration to diversification.

Ansoff's product-market expansion grid is a 2x2 matrix of products (current or new) against markets (current or new), and it is the most tested framework in this subject. Market penetration (current products, current markets) is the least risky — increase purchase frequency or attract competitors’ customers, as Amazon does by growing Prime subscriptions. Market development (current products, new markets) means expanding to new geographies or segments — Amazon’s entry into India and the Middle East. Product development (new products, current markets) requires R&D — AWS cloud services for Amazon’s existing customers. Diversification (new products, new markets) is the most risky — Amazon Studios in entertainment. Risk increases from penetration to diversification, so firms should climb the grid in that order.

Corporate policy provides broad guidelines for decision-making, and there are four types, remembered as "G-S-R-C". A growth policy expands through new products, markets or acquisitions — Amazon’s move from books to everything. A stability policy maintains current operations without major change. A retrenchment policy downsizes or divests to cut losses — Tata Steel’s sale of UK operations. A combination policy mixes all three at once. Policy provides the framework, while strategy provides the action plan; both sit within a governance framework of board composition, transparency, accountability and ethical conduct.

Key points to remember: Ansoff order of risk = Market penetration (safest), Market development, Product development, Diversification (riskiest); policy types = Growth, Stability, Retrenchment, Combination; policy gives the framework, strategy the action plan.

Takeaway: Climb Ansoff in order — penetrate, then develop markets, then develop products, diversify last — risk rises with every step away from the home base.

Answer to Q3: Integration and Diversification Growth Strategies

Answer: Integration grows the firm along its value chain — backward toward suppliers, forward toward customers, horizontal across competitors — while diversification enters new businesses to gain synergy or spread risk.

Vertical integration expands along the value chain. Backward integration moves toward suppliers — a manufacturer buying its raw-material supplier, like Reliance owning oil fields. Forward integration moves toward customers — a manufacturer opening retail stores, like Amazon owning its delivery fleet. Horizontal integration acquires competitors at the same level of the value chain to gain market share — Facebook’s acquisition of Instagram. Vertical integration controls the supply chain but needs high investment; horizontal integration builds market dominance but raises antitrust concerns.

Diversification enters new businesses. Related diversification leverages existing capabilities and shows high synergy — the Tata Group across steel, autos, IT and hospitality. Unrelated (conglomerate) diversification enters entirely new industries with low synergy but strong portfolio risk reduction — Reliance moving from petrochemicals into telecom (Jio), retail and financial services. Reliance illustrates all strategies at once: backward integration (oil fields), forward integration (Reliance Retail), horizontal integration (acquiring Hamleys), related diversification (Jio from petrochemicals) and unrelated diversification (Jio Financial Services).

The reasons for diversification are: economies of scale and scope; risk reduction through portfolio spread; market dominance; and utilization of surplus resources.

Key points to remember: vertical = up/down the value chain (backward toward suppliers, forward toward customers); horizontal = same level, acquiring competitors; related diversification leverages capabilities, unrelated reduces portfolio risk; B-F-H-R-U covers all five integration types.

Takeaway: Integration builds control of the value chain; diversification builds a risk-spread portfolio — choose by your capabilities and risk appetite.

Answer to Q4: Stages of Internationalization and MNC Advantages and Disadvantages

Answer: Firms internationalize in five stages — Domestic, Foreign (exporting), Joint venture, Multinational, Transnational (D-F-J-M-T) — gaining scale and market reach but also exposure to forex, political and cultural risks.

Firms internationalize in five stages, remembered as "D-F-J-M-T". Stage 1 Domestic: the firm is ethnocentric and serves only the home market. Stage 2 Foreign: it begins exporting to foreign markets. Stage 3 Joint venture: it sets up physical operations abroad through partnerships. Stage 4 Multinational: it operates multiple production facilities worldwide. Stage 5 Transnational: it combines global efficiency with local responsiveness as an integrated network. Infosys followed this path exactly: a domestic IT company, then software exports to US clients, then overseas subsidiaries and JVs, then offices in 50-plus countries, and finally global delivery centres that adapt to local markets. Models explaining the process include Vernon’s product life cycle, the Uppsala model of incremental commitment, transaction cost analysis, and Dunning’s eclectic OLI framework.

MNCs enjoy advantages: superior technical know-how, economies of scale, lower input costs through bulk buying, access to overseas raw materials, strong brand image, low-cost financing, financial flexibility to shift to low-tax locations, information advantages, and risk offset through diversification. Their disadvantages include foreign-exchange risk, host-country regulations, different legal systems, political risks, operational difficulties and cultural differences.

Key points to remember: D-F-J-M-T = Domestic, Foreign, Joint venture, Multinational, Transnational; MNC advantages = know-how, scale, low cost, brand, financing, risk offset; MNC risks = forex, regulation, politics, culture.

Takeaway: Internationalization is a gradual climb from home market to global network — each stage trades a little control for a lot of reach.

Answer to Q5: Entry Modes and Strategic Alliances

Answer: Entry modes form a ladder of rising risk, control and investment — Export, License, Franchise, Joint venture, Subsidiary (E-L-F-J-S) — while alliances are cooperative deals that keep partners independent.

Entry modes form a spectrum of increasing risk, control and investment, remembered as "E-L-F-J-S". Exporting is low risk, low control and low investment — Indian IT services exporting to the US. Licensing grants rights to intangible property for a royalty — McDonald’s-style recipe licensing; low risk, low control. Franchising provides a complete business system plus ongoing support — Subway’s franchise model; medium risk and control. Joint ventures share ownership and risk with a local partner — Maruti-Suzuki in India; medium risk with shared control. Wholly-owned subsidiaries (greenfield or acquisition) give full control at high risk and high investment — Tata’s acquisition of Jaguar Land Rover, or IKEA’s wholly-owned entry into India. Government trade barriers, FDI incentives and trade agreements also shape the choice; firms should match the mode to their risk appetite, control needs and resources.

A strategic alliance is a cooperative agreement between independent firms to achieve shared objectives. Types are remembered as "J-E-N-C": joint ventures (a new shared entity, like Tata Starbucks), equity alliances (cross-shareholding, like Renault-Nissan), non-equity alliances (contracts without ownership, like R&D or distribution partnerships), and consortia. Firms ally to share risk and resources, access new markets and technology, achieve economies of scale, and circumvent government restrictions. Success factors are clear objectives, mutual benefit, trust and transparency, and flexibility — while partner conflict, cultural differences and knowledge leakage are the main risks.

Key points to remember: E-L-F-J-S = Export, License, Franchise, JV, Subsidiary — risk and control rise along the ladder; alliances preserve independence, M&A does not; J-E-N-C = Joint venture, Equity, Non-equity, Consortium.

Takeaway: Pick the entry mode that matches your risk appetite — and use alliances when you need a partner’s market or capability without losing independence.

Answer to Q6: SECI Model and IT for Competitive Advantage

Answer: Nonaka’s SECI model converts knowledge through Socialization, Externalization, Combination and Internalization, while IT reshapes all five of Porter’s forces and decides whether strategy can execute at speed and scale.

Knowledge = information + experience + context. It exists in two forms: tacit knowledge, which is personal and hard to formalize (skills, intuition), and explicit knowledge, which is codified and documented (manuals, databases). Nonaka’s SECI model describes four modes of conversion between them. Socialization (tacit to tacit) transfers knowledge through training, mentoring and observation — an apprentice learning from a master. Externalization (tacit to explicit) captures knowledge by documenting best practices and writing manuals. Combination (explicit to explicit) merges codified knowledge, such as combining databases and reports. Internalization (explicit to tacit) turns codified knowledge into personal skill through learning by doing. Infosys runs all four: employees document project learnings (externalization), share best practices on wikis (combination), learn through mentoring (socialization), and apply knowledge in practice (internalization). The KM cycle is create, capture, store, share, apply — with benefits of less rework and faster decisions, and challenges of cultural resistance and knowledge hoarding.

IT shapes each of Porter’s five forces: it reduces supplier power through e-procurement; increases buyer power through price comparison; changes the threat of new entrants (high tech costs are a barrier, but cloud lowers entry); creates new substitutes (streaming versus DVD); and intensifies rivalry through transparency. Amazon uses IT against all five forces — e-procurement platforms, price-comparison tools, AWS infrastructure as an entry barrier, e-commerce substituting physical retail, and transparent competition.

Key points to remember: SECI = Socialization (T to T), Externalization (T to E), Combination (E to E), Internalization (E to T); KM cycle = create, capture, store, share, apply; IT touches all five of Porter’s forces, typically raising buyer power and rivalry.

Takeaway: S-E-C-I keeps knowledge spiraling between tacit and explicit — and in the digital age, IT is the difference between a strategy that is planned and one that actually executes.

Q1. What are the factors which promote the rise of strategic alliances? Explain.

Answer: Strategic alliances arise when firms want benefits none can achieve alone — shared risk, pooled resources, market access, technology learning, scale economies and ways around entry barriers.

Key points:

  • Risk sharing: partners split the cost and uncertainty of new ventures, especially R&D and new-market entry.
  • Resource and capability pooling: complementary assets combine — Tata + Starbucks merged real estate with coffee expertise.
  • Market access: a local partner opens distribution and government networks (Maruti-Suzuki).
  • Technology transfer: faster learning than building in-house (Renault-Nissan engines).
  • Economies of scale and scope: joint purchasing, shared plants, shared R&D labs.
  • Circumventing restrictions: alliances get around FDI limits and trade barriers.
  • Competitive response: allying to match rivals’ moves and global consolidation.

Takeaway: Alliances rise when 1+1 > 2 — shared risk, shared resources, shared markets and faster learning that no partner could buy alone.

Q2. Describe the Eclectic model with respect to internationalization.

Answer: Dunning’s Eclectic (OLI) paradigm says a firm internationalizes when three conditions hold together — Ownership advantages, Location advantages and Internalization advantages.

Key points:

  • Ownership (O): firm-specific assets — technology, brand, patents, management skill (Infosys’s global delivery capability).
  • Location (L): the host country offers cheaper factors, bigger markets, resources or incentives.
  • Internalization (I): the firm gains more by exploiting advantages through its own operations than by licensing or selling them (Apple keeps iOS in-house).
  • All three must coexist — O and L alone point to exporting or licensing; adding I tips the decision toward FDI.
  • The model integrates three earlier theories: ownership (Hymer), location (trade theory) and internalization (transaction-cost economics).
  • It explains entry-mode choice: export (O+L), licensing (O only), FDI (O+L+I).

Takeaway: OLI answers three questions — Why (O), Where (L), How (I) — and when all three hold, the firm invests directly.

Q3. Why do MNCs invest in different countries? Explain.

Answer: MNCs invest abroad to earn higher returns through new markets, cheaper resources, lower costs, strategic assets and risk diversification that the home country cannot offer.

Key points:

  • Market-seeking: reach larger or faster-growing markets (Amazon entering India).
  • Resource-seeking: secure raw materials and cheap inputs (Reliance’s overseas energy assets).
  • Efficiency-seeking: lower production and logistics costs via cheap labour and favourable tax regimes (Foxconn plants).
  • Strategic-asset seeking: acquire brands, technology and R&D (Tata-Jaguar Land Rover).
  • OLI logic: ownership advantages earn their best returns in foreign locations.
  • Risk diversification: operations across countries cushion any single market’s downturn.
  • Trade barriers: tariffs and quotas push production inside protected markets.

Takeaway: MNCs invest where location advantages convert their ownership advantages into returns the home market cannot match.

Q4. What are the different kinds of entry strategies? Explain any two citing examples.

Answer: Entry strategies form a ladder of rising risk, control and investment — exporting, licensing, franchising, joint ventures, mergers/acquisitions and wholly-owned subsidiaries.

Key points:

  • Exporting: selling home-made goods abroad; low risk, low control (Indian pharma exporting generics).
  • Licensing: selling rights to use technology or patents for a royalty (Coca-Cola’s concentrate licensing).
  • Franchising: handing over the complete business system with ongoing support (Domino’s Pizza India).
  • Joint venture: shared ownership with a local partner — shared control and risk (Maruti-Suzuki).
  • M&A: buying an existing operation for instant presence (Tata’s Jaguar Land Rover).
  • Wholly-owned subsidiary: greenfield investment with full control and highest risk (IKEA’s own Indian stores).
  • Any two with examples: franchising — McDonald’s entered India through franchisees who knew local tastes (McAloo Tikki); joint venture — Maruti-Suzuki gave Suzuki market access and Maruti the technology.

Takeaway: E-L-F-J-W — every step up the entry ladder buys more control at the price of more risk and investment; match the rung to your resources.

Q5. What are the different methods used by the government to protect their business environment?

Answer: Governments protect the domestic business environment through trade barriers, FDI and exchange controls, subsidies and regulatory institutions that shield local industry and consumers.

Key points:

  • Tariff barriers: customs duties on imports make foreign goods costlier.
  • Non-tariff barriers: quotas, licences, quality standards and sanitary norms.
  • FDI restrictions: sectoral caps and approval routes (retail, defence) limit foreign ownership.
  • Exchange controls: managing rupee convertibility to stabilise imports and exports.
  • Subsidies and incentives: export subsidies, tax holidays and PLI schemes for domestic industry.
  • Regulatory institutions: RBI, SEBI, CCI and DPIIT frame and enforce the rules.
  • WTO-compliant safeguards: anti-dumping duties protect against unfair competition.

Takeaway: Protection = tariffs + quotas + FDI caps + subsidies + regulators — a balancing act between shielding home industry and staying open to trade.

Q6. Explain the different components of IT architecture. Describe the factors involved in influencing the choice of a particular IT infrastructure.

Answer: IT architecture has four components — hardware, software, data and networks — and the infrastructure choice depends on strategy, cost, scalability, security and integration needs.

Key points:

  • Hardware: servers, desktops, mobile devices and data centres.
  • Software: operating systems, applications and ERP packages.
  • Data: databases, data warehouses and analytics systems.
  • Networks: LAN/WAN, internet, cloud and communication links.
  • Choice factors — strategy first: infrastructure must serve business strategy (Amazon’s cloud exists for its scale); cost versus performance; scalability and flexibility for growth; security and compliance with data laws; compatibility with existing systems; and available user skills.
  • Vendor and integration issues: lock-in risk and the effort to connect legacy and new systems.

Takeaway: Four components (hardware, software, data, networks) — but the choice of stack is strategy-driven: scale, cost, security and integration decide it.

Q7. Describe the trends and challenges of knowledge management in an organization.

Answer: KM trends are digitalisation, AI and analytics, collaboration platforms and knowledge-sharing cultures; the challenges are tacit-knowledge capture, cultural resistance, technology costs and measuring returns.

Key points:

  • Trends: big data and AI-driven knowledge discovery; cloud and collaboration tools (Teams, wikis); communities of practice; the Chief Knowledge Officer role; social KM via internal networks; knowledge treated as a strategic asset.
  • Challenge — tacit knowledge: converting what experts carry in their heads into explicit form (SECI externalization) is hard.
  • Challenge — culture: knowledge hoarding (“knowledge is power”) and resistance to sharing.
  • Challenge — support and cost: missing top-management sponsorship and high technology/maintenance costs.
  • Challenge — measurement: KM benefits and ROI are difficult to quantify.
  • Challenge — retention and quality: knowledge walks out when employees leave; volume growth creates data-quality and security problems.

Takeaway: KM is moving from storage to AI-driven flow of knowledge — but the biggest challenge stays human: culture and tacit knowledge.

Q8. What is innovation? Explain innovation with respect to the success of an organization.

Answer: Innovation is the successful introduction of new or improved products, processes or business models that create value — and it drives success through differentiation, cost advantage, growth and survival.

Key points:

  • Definition: innovation converts ideas into marketable value — product, process, service or business-model innovation.
  • Types: incremental versus radical; product versus process versus organizational innovation.
  • Differentiation: innovative products command premium prices (Apple’s iPhone).
  • Cost advantage: process innovation cuts costs (Tata Nano’s design-for-cost engineering).
  • Growth: new products open new markets (Jio’s data revolution).
  • Survival: firms that stop innovating lose to movers (Nokia versus Apple).
  • Enablers: R&D investment, innovation culture, intrapreneurship, customer feedback and technology adoption.

Takeaway: Innovation = ideas converted into value; it decides competitive survival — differentiate, cut costs or open new markets, or decline.

Q1. Identify and explain the features of corporate policy. Describe the determinants of the corporate policy which influence an organization.

Answer: Corporate policy is the broad framework of guidelines for decisions — its features are being directional, forward-looking, flexible, top-formulated and consistent — and it is determined by environment, mission, resources, values and stakeholders.

Key points:

  • Features: provides broad guidelines, not specific instructions; directional — gives purpose and unity of action; formulated at the top and cascades into rules, procedures and programmes; forward-looking and anticipatory; flexible and dynamic, revised as the environment changes; simple, understandable and consistent with objectives.
  • Determinants: the external environment (opportunities and threats).
  • Determinants: organizational mission and objectives; resources and capabilities; top-management values and philosophy; stakeholder expectations (shareholders, employees, government).
  • Determinants: past strategies and stage of growth; industry dynamics and competitive conditions.

Takeaway: Policy is the “why” framework — broad, top-level, forward-looking and flexible, shaped by environment, mission, resources and stakeholders.

Q2. What is the nature and scope of various corporate strategies which have an impact on the performance of the organization?

Answer: Corporate strategies — growth, stability and retrenchment, with integration and diversification as growth sub-strategies — decide the business portfolio and resource allocation, so they directly shape performance.

Key points:

  • Nature: corporate-level strategies choose which businesses to be in and how to allocate resources across them, unlike business-level (competitive) strategies.
  • Growth strategy: expands through intensification — Ansoff’s penetration, market development, product development (Reliance Retail’s store rollout).
  • Integration: backward (owning suppliers) and forward (owning distribution) integration strengthen the value chain.
  • Horizontal integration: acquiring rivals builds market share and market power.
  • Diversification: related (synergy across businesses — Tata Group) and unrelated (portfolio risk reduction — Reliance Jio).
  • Stability and retrenchment: holding position in turbulent times, and turnaround/divestment to cut losses (Tata Steel’s UK exit).
  • Performance impact: each strategy changes revenue, margins, risk and competitive position differently.

Takeaway: Strategy type shapes value-chain position — expansion builds scale, integration builds control, diversification spreads risk, retrenchment restores health.

Q3. What do you understand by diversification? Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of diversification.

Answer: Diversification is entering new businesses beyond current products and markets to grow and reduce risk — with clear advantages (synergy, risk spread, scale) and disadvantages (complexity, management stretch, entry costs).

Key points:

  • Advantage — risk reduction: a portfolio across industries cushions any single business (Reliance in petrochem, telecom, retail).
  • Advantage — economies of scale and scope: shared resources, brand and capabilities across businesses.
  • Advantage — utilisation of surplus: spare cash, brand equity and managerial talent put to work.
  • Advantage — growth and market power: escapes saturated markets; cross-subsidisation strengthens bargaining power.
  • Disadvantage — entry costs and barriers: unfamiliar industries demand new skills and capital.
  • Disadvantage — management stretch: attention spread thin; loss of focus and core-competence dilution.
  • Disadvantage — integration and failure risk: clashing cultures and systems (merger failures); unrelated diversification into unknown territory has sunk many conglomerates.

Takeaway: Diversification trades focus for risk-spread — it pays only when you transfer real capabilities (synergy), not just capital.

Q4. Elaborate on the costs and the risks associated with strategic alliances.

Answer: Alliances create value but carry real costs — negotiation, coordination and shared profits — and real risks — partner opportunism, knowledge leakage, conflict and loss of control.

Key points:

  • Cost — negotiation and structuring: lengthy, expensive deal-making.
  • Cost — coordination and monitoring: joint decision-making is slow and expensive.
  • Cost — profit sharing: benefits are split with the partner, plus relationship-management investment.
  • Risk — opportunism and free-riding: a partner may take more than it gives.
  • Risk — knowledge leakage: technology and know-how can walk over to the partner — a future competitor.
  • Risk — control and conflict: lost flexibility, clashing cultures and decision rights (many failed JVs); dependence and lock-in; and termination risk when the alliance breaks (Renault-Nissan strains).

Takeaway: Alliances fail on trust, not terms — budget for coordination costs and protect core knowledge, or the partner walks away with it.

Q5. Explain the concept of a domestic firm. What advantages and disadvantages are faced by the domestic firm as compared to a Multinational Corporation (MNC)?

Answer: A domestic firm operates only within its home country — it enjoys local knowledge, speed and lower exposure, but suffers scale, cost, technology and financing disadvantages against MNCs.

Key points:

  • Concept: serves only the home market with an ethnocentric outlook and a single legal and tax environment (a local textile mill versus Arvind exporting worldwide).
  • Advantage — local knowledge: deep understanding of market, culture and regulations.
  • Advantage — simplicity and speed: lower overheads, simpler operations, faster decisions, local relationships with customers, suppliers and government.
  • Advantage — low exposure: no foreign-exchange or cross-border political risk; benefits from government protection and buy-local preference.
  • Disadvantage — scale and cost: small volumes mean higher unit costs than MNC economies of scale.
  • Disadvantage — technology, finance, talent: limited R&D, capital access and global talent pools; no global risk diversification.
  • Disadvantage — vulnerability: when MNCs enter with deep pockets and global brands, domestic firms lose shelf space and talent.

Takeaway: The domestic firm’s moat is local knowledge and speed; its wall is scale, technology and finance — the exact strengths of an MNC.

Q6. Why do MNCs tend to invest abroad? Explain.

Answer: MNCs invest abroad because push factors at home (saturation, costs, regulation) combine with pull factors abroad (markets, cheap resources, incentives, strategic assets) to promise higher returns.

Key points:

  • Push factors: saturated home market, stiff home competition, rising domestic costs, regulatory pressure and limited growth prospects.
  • Pull factors — markets and costs: attractive host growth rates; cheap labour, land and raw materials; favourable FDI policies, tax incentives and SEZs.
  • Strategic assets: acquiring brands, technology and distribution abroad (Tata’s Corus and JLR purchases).
  • Global efficiency: spreading each production stage to its cheapest location in global value chains.
  • Risk diversification: operations in many economies offset any single market’s downturn.
  • Following customers and rivals: banks and suppliers move abroad with Indian corporates; firms pre-empt competitors in growing markets.

Takeaway: Push (home limits) + pull (host attractions) = FDI — MNCs follow markets, resources, efficiency and strategic assets.

Q7. What is the role of Information Technology (IT) in implementing a strategy? Discuss with the help of examples.

Answer: IT executes strategy by enabling speed, coordination, data-driven decisions, automation and customer reach — it is the nervous system connecting strategy to operations.

Key points:

  • Operational support: ERP systems (SAP) integrate functions so one strategy runs on one platform (Maruti’s supply chain).
  • Competitive advantage: IT reshapes Porter’s five forces — e-procurement cuts supplier power, price comparison raises buyer power, cloud lowers entry barriers (Amazon AWS).
  • Speed and agility: real-time dashboards and analytics speed decisions.
  • Customer relationships: CRM and digital channels execute market strategy (Jio’s digital onboarding).
  • Cost reduction: automation and BPO — the Indian IT industry itself is the proof.
  • Innovation enablers: analytics, AI and IoT feed new products and processes.
  • Global coordination: collaboration platforms run teams across geographies.

Takeaway: IT does not set the strategy — it decides whether the strategy can be executed at speed, cost and scale (Amazon, Jio, Maruti prove it).

Q1. Explain the concept of corporate planning and describe its characteristics.

Answer: Corporate planning is the continuous, systematic process of setting objectives and making entrepreneurial decisions with the best knowledge of their futurity (Drucker) — its characteristics are futurity, continuity, systematicity, integration and formalisation.

Key points:

  • Futurity: decisions are made for the long term (5-10 years), not for today.
  • Continuity: a rolling process, updated as the environment changes, not a one-time event.
  • Systematic and structured: formal appraisal of environment and alternatives, not guesswork.
  • Company-wide scope: integrates all functions and businesses into one direction.
  • Top-management driven: strategic intent and direction come from the top.
  • Uncertainty-based: works with forecasts, scenarios and assumptions; and it feeds the full cycle — planning → implementation → evaluation and control.

Takeaway: Corporate planning is thinking ahead systematically — continuous, top-driven, future-focused and organisation-wide.

Q2. Describe the steps involved in Merger and Acquisition deals.

Answer: An M&A deal runs through strategy, target search, valuation, negotiation, due diligence, financing, approvals and — critically — post-merger integration.

Key points:

  • Corporate strategy: define why and what to acquire — growth, capability or market share.
  • Target identification and screening: search, shortlist and screen candidates.
  • Valuation: DCF, comparable multiples and asset-based methods price the target.
  • Negotiation of terms: price, payment mode (cash or stock) and deal structure.
  • Due diligence: legal, financial, tax and operational checks to uncover hidden liabilities.
  • Financing: cash, debt, equity or share swaps fund the deal.
  • Approvals and integration: regulatory (CCI, SEBI) and shareholder approval, then post-merger integration of systems, culture and teams — where most deals succeed or fail (Tata-JLR succeeded; many cross-border deals fail here).

Takeaway: The deal is won in negotiation but lost in integration — post-merger integration decides value creation.

Q3. List and explain different types of strategic alliances.

Answer: Strategic alliances are cooperative agreements between independent firms, ranging from non-equity contracts to equity-based joint ventures and consortia along a continuum of rising commitment.

Key points:

  • Non-equity alliances: contracts such as licensing, R&D pacts and distribution agreements without ownership — flexible and low-commitment (IndiGo-Qatar codeshare).
  • Equity alliances: partners take cross-shareholdings in each other (Renault-Nissan).
  • Joint ventures: partners create a new jointly owned entity (Tata Starbucks).
  • Consortia: many firms pool resources for a large project (the Airbus consortium).
  • Alliances preserve each firm’s independence; mergers and acquisitions sit beyond them because they end independence.
  • Choice criteria: strategic fit, trust, cultural compatibility and the level of control desired.

Takeaway: Alliance intensity rises along the continuum — contract → equity stake → joint venture → consortium — pick the depth your trust can bear.

Q4. What is the need for continuous evaluation of internationalization as a process in any economy? Discuss.

Answer: Internationalization must be continuously evaluated because markets, technologies and policies keep changing — evaluation keeps entry decisions, commitments and performance aligned with reality.

Key points:

  • Dynamic environment: trade policies, tariffs, exchange rates and competitors shift constantly.
  • Risk monitoring: political, currency and market risks need continuous tracking so firms can withdraw when risk rises.
  • Performance feedback: evaluation shows which markets and products earn and which to exit (Vodafone’s India exit after policy shocks).
  • Learning and adaptation: feedback feeds the incremental-commitment logic of the Uppsala model.
  • Resource reallocation: investment shifts to winning markets and away from losers.
  • Compliance and escalation control: host-country regulations change and must be reviewed; evaluation prevents sunk-cost traps and escalation of commitment in failing markets.

Takeaway: Internationalization is a process, not an event — continuous evaluation is the steering wheel that keeps it profitable.

Q5. Write short notes on the following: (a) Competitive Strategy and Competitiveness (b) Competitive advantage and R&D.

Answer (a): Competitive strategy is how a firm beats rivals within an industry — cost leadership, differentiation or focus — and competitiveness is the ability to sustain that advantage over time.

Key points:

  • Porter’s generic strategies: cost leadership (IndiGo), differentiation (Apple), focus (niche players).
  • Competitive advantage = superior value delivered at lower cost or at a premium price.
  • Sources of advantage: resources, capabilities, technology, brand and scale.
  • Competitiveness is dynamic — advantage erodes as rivals imitate, so it needs constant upgrading and barriers to imitation.
  • At the national level, Porter’s diamond links factor conditions, demand, related industries and firm strategy/rivalry.

Answer (b): R&D creates the proprietary knowledge, products and processes that become competitive advantage.

  • Product R&D: new and improved products drive differentiation (Apple, Samsung).
  • Process R&D: cheaper, better processes drive cost advantage (Tata Nano’s design-for-cost).
  • Technology as a barrier: patents and trade secrets block imitators.
  • R&D spending correlates with innovation output in pharma, IT and autos.
  • Balance in-house and open innovation; Indian examples: ISRO’s frugal engineering, TCS Innovation Labs.

Takeaway: Competitive advantage = strategy + R&D: strategy picks the game, R&D converts investment into value rivals cannot copy.

Q6. What are the different techniques which can be adopted to enhance the creativity and promote innovation in organizations? Discuss.

Answer: Organizations promote creativity and innovation through idea-generation techniques, structural enablers, culture and incentives — brainstorming, suggestion schemes, innovation labs, intrapreneurship, rewards and open innovation.

Key points:

  • Brainstorming and nominal group technique: structured generation of ideas from teams.
  • Suggestion schemes and idea boxes: employee ideas flow upwards (Tata’s InnoVista awards).
  • Skunkworks and innovation labs: separate teams with freedom to experiment (3M’s Post-it, Google X).
  • Intrapreneurship: employees act as entrepreneurs with internal venture funding (3M’s 15% rule).
  • R&D investment and cross-functional teams: dedicated budgets and diverse perspectives.
  • Culture and leadership: tolerance of failure, freedom to experiment.
  • Rewards and open innovation: monetary and non-monetary recognition; hackathons, competitions and customer co-creation.

Takeaway: Creativity is a management choice — give time, tools, tolerance for failure and rewards, and innovation follows.

Q7. Write a brief note on the Knowledge Management (KM) initiatives taken up by Indian Organizations.

Answer: Indian organizations — from IT majors to PSUs — run structured KM programmes built on knowledge portals, communities of practice, learning systems and sharing incentives.

Key points:

  • TCS: KNet portal with expertise directories, communities of practice and best-practice repositories.
  • Infosys: knowledge portal with the “MileStone” expertise locator, knowledge competitions and KM awards.
  • Wipro and Cognizant: KM integrated into project delivery with lessons-learned databases and codified delivery assets.
  • PSUs and banks (ONGC, L&T, SBI): knowledge portals, training academies and documentation of tacit expertise.
  • Common formula: leadership support, incentives for contribution (awards and recognition), technology platforms (wikis, portals, AI search) and culture-building programmes.
  • Benefits: faster project delivery, less reinvention of solutions, and retention of expert knowledge when employees leave.

Takeaway: India’s KM leaders (TCS, Infosys, Wipro) all use the same formula — portal + communities + incentives + top-management support.

Q1. How can businesses operating in dynamic environment recognize and rank strategic opportunities? Explain giving examples.

Answer: Businesses spot and rank opportunities through continuous environmental scanning and structured frameworks — SWOT, PESTLE, the Ansoff grid and BCG matrix — then score each opportunity on attractiveness, fit and risk.

Key points:

  • Scanning: PESTLE and competitor monitoring surface opportunities (Jio reading the data-market opportunity early).
  • SWOT: match strengths to opportunities and neutralise weaknesses.
  • Ansoff grid: rank by risk — penetration, market development, product development, diversification.
  • BCG matrix: rank businesses by market growth and share for resource allocation.
  • Scoring: score on market size, growth, profitability, capability fit, entry barriers and risk.
  • Scenario analysis: test each opportunity under different futures.
  • Agility: dynamic markets reward fast validation and pilots (Zomato’s quick experiments).

Takeaway: Opportunities are recognized by scanning and ranked by risk-return-fit scoring — the Ansoff grid is the ranking ladder.

Q2. How does scenario planning assist businesses in navigating uncertainty? What part does it play in strategic management? Discuss.

Answer: Scenario planning builds several plausible futures and tests strategies against each, so firms act robustly under uncertainty instead of betting on a single forecast.

Key points:

  • Build scenarios: define the question and horizon, identify driving forces (economic, technological, political), and build 2-4 distinct futures around critical uncertainties (oil prices, regulation).
  • Test strategies: strategies that work in every scenario are “robust” and get priority.
  • Early-warning indicators: signals that tell which scenario is unfolding.
  • Contingency plans: pre-decided responses for each future.
  • Role in strategic management: complements conventional planning, improves risk management, creates strategic options and builds organisational learning (Shell pioneered it in the 1970s oil crisis).

Takeaway: Scenario planning does not predict the future — it prepares the organization for all of them, with triggers and contingencies.

Q3. Can business successfully include CSR and sustainability into their strategic management procedures? Justify giving examples.

Answer: Yes — CSR and sustainability become strategic when they are built into vision, products, supply chains and governance, and they then create competitive advantage instead of just compliance.

Key points:

  • Strategic CSR: aligned with core business — Tata’s water conservation linked to its industrial footprint.
  • Sustainability as innovation: energy efficiency cuts costs (Infosys’s green buildings saving crores).
  • Brand and customers: sustainable products command premiums (ITC’s e-Choupal and eco-friendly paper).
  • Risk management: climate and regulatory risks handled proactively (Companies Act 2013 CSR spend, SEBI’s BRSR reporting).
  • Stakeholder trust: ESG investors, employees and communities reward responsible firms.
  • Triple bottom line: people, planet and profit enter KPIs and balanced scorecards.
  • Challenge: greenwashing and cost pressure — but measurable when tied to strategy.

Takeaway: CSR is strategic when it moves from charity to core business — sustainability then becomes a cost saver, brand builder and risk shield.

Q4. What are the main obstacles to managing partnerships and strategic alliances, and how can businesses get past them? Discuss.

Answer: Alliances fail mainly on trust, culture, control and knowledge-leakage problems — and survive through careful partner selection, clear governance, communication and phased commitment.

Key points:

  • Obstacle — partner mismatch: incompatible goals and cultures.
  • Obstacle — trust and opportunism: free-riding and unequal contributions.
  • Obstacle — knowledge leakage: the partner becomes a competitor.
  • Obstacle — decision-making and exit: slow joint decisions, management conflicts, lock-in and breakup costs.
  • Solution — screening and contracts: rigorous partner screening for strategic and cultural fit; clear contracts with decision rights and IP-protection clauses.
  • Solution — relationship and phasing: dedicated alliance managers, communication, balanced benefits; start with non-equity deals and deepen with trust (Uppsala-style incremental commitment); defined exit mechanisms and continuous performance review.

Takeaway: Alliances die from trust and culture gaps, not from lack of opportunity — screen partners, protect IP and deepen commitment gradually.

Q5. How can businesses use innovation and technology to their advantage to obtain a competitive edge in the market? Explain with the help of examples.

Answer: Innovation and technology create competitive edges through differentiation, cost advantage, speed and new business models — from product invention to digital platforms.

Key points:

  • Product innovation: differentiate and command premiums (Apple’s iPhone, Tata Nexon EV).
  • Process and technology: cut costs through automation and AI (IndiGo’s operational efficiency).
  • Digital platforms: scale fast through ecosystems (UPI, Jio’s digital stack).
  • Data and analytics: personalisation and prediction (Flipkart’s recommendations).
  • Speed to market: first-mover advantage (Zomato and Swiggy in food delivery).
  • Barriers and culture: patents and proprietary algorithms raise entry barriers; R&D investment and innovation culture sustain the edge (TCS, ISRO’s frugal engineering).

Takeaway: Tech + innovation gives an edge in one of four ways — differentiate, cut costs, move fast or build platforms — and the edge lasts only while the innovation continues.

Q6. Discuss the impact of globalization on strategic management. What are the approaches that business can adopt to remain competitive in an increasingly competitive marketplace?

Answer: Globalization widens markets but intensifies competition, forcing strategic management to think globally while acting locally — through transnational, alliance-based, innovation-led and digital approaches.

Key points:

  • Impact — bigger stage: larger markets and growth opportunities for Indian firms going global (Tata, Mahindra).
  • Impact — tougher rivals: global players enter home markets; global value chains spread sourcing and production across borders.
  • Impact — more tools and risks: cross-border M&A and alliances become strategy tools; complexity rises from culture, regulation, forex and politics.
  • Approach — global: standardised products for global efficiency.
  • Approach — multi-domestic: localised offerings for each market.
  • Approach — transnational: global efficiency plus local responsiveness — the modern winning model.
  • Approach — enablers: alliances and JVs for entry, innovation-led differentiation, and digital transformation for global reach.

Takeaway: Globalization means a bigger stage and tougher rivals — the winning responses are transnational (“glocal”) thinking, alliances, innovation and digital reach.

Q7. In order to remain relevant in dynamic markets, how should businesses handle strategic change and transformation? Elucidate with the help of examples.

Answer: Businesses stay relevant by treating change as continuous — sensing signals early, setting direction, mobilising people and transforming capabilities in phases, with strong leadership and relentless communication.

Key points:

  • Sense early: environmental scanning catches shifts before they bite (Kodak missed digital photography; Netflix saw streaming).
  • Set direction: a clear transformation vision and roadmap from the top (Google’s AI-first pivot).
  • Mobilise people: Kotter’s 8 steps — urgency, coalition, vision, communication, empowerment, quick wins, consolidation, culture.
  • Reskill and communicate: manage resistance with training and transparent communication.
  • Phase it: staged implementation with quick wins builds momentum (Reliance’s Jio rollout).
  • Measure and adjust: transformation KPIs and agility loops.
  • Examples: Netflix (DVD → streaming → content), Adobe (licences → subscriptions), Maruti (petrol → hybrids/EVs).

Takeaway: Change fails on people, not plans — create urgency, communicate relentlessly, reskill and celebrate quick wins to make transformation stick.

Q8. In order to improve performance, how do businesses match their organizational culture and structure to their strategic goals? Discuss.

Answer: Structure and culture must follow strategy — structure assigns roles, coordination and control, while culture supplies values, behaviour and motivation — and both must point at the strategic goals.

Key points:

  • Structure follows strategy (Chandler): cost leadership suits centralised, functional structures; differentiation and innovation suit flexible structures — divisional, matrix or agile teams.
  • Culture follows strategy: innovation strategies need risk-taking cultures; efficiency strategies need disciplined ones.
  • Alignment tools: the balanced scorecard links strategy to KPIs; hiring, training and rewards reinforce the culture.
  • Culture as advantage: culture is hard to imitate — Tata’s value-driven culture, Zomato’s agility.
  • Mismatch costs: the right strategy with the wrong culture fails in execution (merger culture clashes).
  • Examples: Amazon — frugal, customer-obsessed culture with decentralised teams; Infosys — professional culture and a learning organization.

Takeaway: Strategy wins only when structure and culture carry it — choose structure for coordination and grow culture for behaviour, both aimed at the strategy.
