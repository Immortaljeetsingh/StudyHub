Q1(a) Communication Process and Shannon-Weaver Model[5]

Answer: Communication is a two-way process in which a sender encodes an idea, transmits it through a channel, and a receiver decodes it, with feedback completing the loop — as formalised by the Shannon-Weaver model (1948).

Communication is the exchange of information, ideas, and feelings between two or more persons to achieve mutual understanding. The process has five elements: Sender, Message, Channel, Receiver, and Feedback.

  • The sender converts an idea into words or symbols. This is called encoding.
  • The message is the actual content being transmitted.
  • The channel is the medium used, such as email, phone, or face-to-face talk.
  • The receiver interprets the symbols back into meaning. This is decoding.
  • Feedback is the receiver's response. Without it, communication is not complete.

The Shannon-Weaver model (1948) is a linear model: Information Source → Transmitter → Channel → Receiver → Destination. Its main contribution is the concept of noise — anything that distorts the message. Noise can enter at any stage.

Example: In an office, noise can be physical (construction sound outside during a client call), semantic (using technical jargon the customer does not understand), or technical (a Zoom call dropping during a review).

Key points to remember: Sender encodes → message travels through a channel → receiver decodes → feedback completes the loop; noise distorts the message at any point; communication is incomplete without feedback.

Takeaway: Always close the loop with feedback and design out noise — a message that is sent but not understood is not communication.

Q1(b) The 7 C's of Communication[5]

Answer: The 7 C's are Clarity, Conciseness, Concreteness, Correctness, Coherence, Completeness and Courtesy — the seven standards every effective business message must meet.

The 7 C's are the golden rules of effective communication:

  • Clarity: The message must be easy to understand. Use simple words.
  • Conciseness: Be brief and include only what is needed.
  • Concreteness: Be specific with facts and figures, not vague statements.
  • Correctness: The message must be accurate and error-free.
  • Coherence: Ideas must flow logically from one to the next.
  • Completeness: Give all the information the receiver needs.
  • Courtesy: Be polite and respectful to the receiver.

Example: Instead of writing "Submit the report soon" (vague), write "Please submit the sales report by 5 PM on Friday" — this shows concreteness, completeness, and clarity. Ratan Tata's one-page letter to employees during the Nano setback is a famous example of clarity, conciseness, and courtesy. Following the 7 C's builds trust, prevents misunderstandings, and saves time for both the sender and the receiver in every business exchange.

Key points to remember: 7 C's = Clarity, Conciseness, Concreteness, Correctness, Coherence, Completeness, Courtesy; always answer what, why, when, and how; keep the receiver's needs in mind.

Takeaway: Run every message through the 7 C's checklist before sending — it takes thirty seconds and prevents most workplace misunderstandings.

Q2(a) Verbal vs Non-Verbal Communication and Mehrabian's Rule[5]

Answer: Verbal communication uses words (only 7% of emotional impact), while non-verbal communication — tone at 38% and body language at 55% — carries about 93% of the emotional meaning in face-to-face messages (Mehrabian's 7-38-55 rule).

Verbal communication uses spoken or written words — letters, emails, reports, phone calls, speeches. It is direct, explicit, and can be recorded for future reference. Non-verbal communication uses body language, facial expressions, gestures, tone of voice, and even silence. It is subtle, often unconscious, and culture-specific.

Mehrabian's 7-38-55 rule says that in face-to-face communication of feelings and attitudes:

  • 7% comes from the words (verbal).
  • 38% comes from the tone of voice (vocal).
  • 55% comes from body language (visual).

This means about 93% of emotional communication is non-verbal. Non-verbal dimensions include kinesics (gestures), proxemics (personal space), haptics (touch, like a firm handshake), chronemics (use of time), and paralanguage (pitch and pace).

Example: A manager says "Good job" but speaks in a flat tone without eye contact. The employee will believe the body language, not the words.

Key points to remember: Verbal = words (7%); non-verbal = tone (38%) + body language (55%); non-verbal signals often matter more than words in emotional messages; interpretations are culture-specific.

Takeaway: When words and body language conflict, listeners believe the body language — so align tone and gesture with what you say.

Q2(b) Formal vs Informal Communication and the Grapevine[5]

Answer: Formal communication follows the official hierarchy and is documented, while the informal grapevine is fast, flexible and 75-90% accurate, with four network types identified by Keith Davis.

Formal communication flows through the official hierarchy — downward (instructions from boss to staff), upward (reports and suggestions), horizontal (between peers), and diagonal (across departments). It is slower, documented, and accurate, for example board meeting minutes.

Informal communication (grapevine) flows through personal networks and friendships. It is faster, flexible, and carries no formal record, for example tea-break talk about promotions. Interestingly, research shows the grapevine is 75-90% accurate, so managers use it to gauge employee sentiment.

Keith Davis identified four grapevine networks:

  • Single Strand: A tells B, B tells C, C tells D (a serial chain).
  • Gossip Chain: One person tells everyone else.
  • Probability Chain: Each person shares randomly with others.
  • Cluster Chain: A tells selected friends, who tell their friends. This is the most common.

Example: When a new manager is appointed, news often spreads through the cluster chain before any official announcement.

Key points to remember: Formal = official, slow, documented; grapevine = informal, fast, 75-90% accurate; four networks: single strand, gossip, probability, cluster (most common).

Takeaway: You cannot kill the grapevine — feed it accurate information early instead of letting rumours fill the vacuum.

Q3(a) Listening vs Hearing, Types of Listening, and SOLER[5]

Answer: Hearing is the passive reception of sound, while listening is an active skill of receiving, understanding and responding; the SOLER model (Sit squarely, Open posture, Lean forward, Eye contact, Respond) is the standard framework for active listening.

Hearing is passive — sound simply reaches the ear. Listening is active — it requires receiving, understanding, and responding to the message. We spend 45% of our communication time listening, yet we retain only 25% of a message after 48 hours, and half of that is wrong.

Types of listening:

  • Active: Full engagement with feedback.
  • Passive: Minimal engagement, like background music.
  • Empathic: Understanding the speaker's feelings.
  • Critical: Evaluating content, like an investor on an earnings call.
  • Appreciative: Listening for enjoyment.

The SOLER model of active listening is: Sit squarely facing the speaker, Open posture (no crossed arms), Lean forward to show interest, Eye contact, and Respond with feedback.

Example: A counselor using SOLER while an employee explains stress helps the employee feel heard and understood, which builds trust.

Key points to remember: Listening is active, hearing is passive; retention drops to 25% after 48 hours; SOLER = Sit squarely, Open posture, Lean forward, Eye contact, Respond; overcome internal and external barriers.

Takeaway: Practise SOLER posture and silence your internal reply-planning — retention doubles when you listen to understand rather than to answer.

Q3(b) Reading Skills for Business[5]

Answer: Skimming (main ideas), scanning (specific facts), critical reading (evaluating arguments) and the SQ3R method (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review) are the four reading skills managers use to handle large volumes of documents.

Managers read a huge volume of documents daily, so efficient reading skills save time and improve decisions.

  • Skimming: Reading quickly to grasp the main ideas. Useful for the executive summary of a report or a newspaper's front page.
  • Scanning: Looking for specific information, such as finding a deadline date in a contract or a price in a catalogue.
  • Critical Reading: Analysing arguments and evidence, questioning assumptions. Essential when evaluating a vendor proposal or a market research report.
  • SQ3R Method: Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review. Used for textbooks and manuals: first survey the headings, then turn them into questions, read to answer them, recite the key points, and finally review the material.

Example: IAS officers at LBSNAA are trained in speed reading because they process 200+ files a day — skimming gives the gist, scanning finds the key figure, and critical reading catches weak arguments.

Key points to remember: Skimming = main ideas; scanning = specific details; critical reading = evaluate arguments; SQ3R = Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review.

Takeaway: Match the reading skill to the document — skim routine mail, scan contracts for dates, and read critically only what feeds decisions.

Q4(a) Planning and Conducting Effective Meetings[5]

Answer: An effective meeting is planned with a clear agenda, conducted by a chairperson who keeps discussion on track and manages participation, and followed up with recorded action items.

A meeting is a planned gathering to share information, decide, or solve problems. Good meetings need three stages: preparation, conduction, and follow-up.

  • Preparation: Send a clear agenda 24-48 hours in advance with time for each item. Invite only essential people (Amazon's "two-pizza rule"). Share background material so members can pre-read.
  • Role of the chairperson: Start and end on time, keep the discussion on the agenda, encourage quiet members to speak, manage conflicts diplomatically, and summarise decisions and action items.
  • Etiquette: Arrive 2-3 minutes early, silence phones, listen actively, speak concisely, never interrupt, and stay for the whole meeting.

Common mistakes are running without an agenda, inviting too many people, side conversations, and no follow-up on action items.

Example: Amazon India uses the "6-page memo" format — participants silently read a narrative memo for 20 minutes before discussion, which keeps meetings deep and focused.

Key points to remember: Agenda 24-48 hours early; only essential participants; chairperson keeps focus and summarises; etiquette = punctual, silent phones, no interruptions; ADAP = Agenda, Decisions, Action items, Progress.

Takeaway: A meeting is only as good as its follow-up — end every meeting with named owners and deadlines for each action item.

Q4(b) Minutes of Meeting (MoM)[5]

Answer: Minutes of Meeting are the official written record of a meeting, covering date, attendees, agenda items, decisions and action items — and follow-up within 24-48 hours is what turns those decisions into results.

Minutes of Meeting (MoM) are the official written record of what was discussed and decided in a meeting. They are legally important and prevent arguments about "who said what".

Essential components of MoM:

  • Header: Date, time, venue of the meeting.
  • Attendees: Names of those present, absent, and those who sent apologies.
  • Agenda items discussed: A summary of each topic, not a word-for-word transcript.
  • Decisions made: With voting results if any.
  • Action items: Each task with the responsible person and deadline.
  • Next meeting: Date and time of the next meeting.

Follow-up is critical. Minutes are useless if nobody checks whether the action items were completed. Good practice is to circulate the MoM within 24-48 hours and track progress at the next meeting. Without follow-up, decisions silently die and the same topics get re-discussed.

Example: A marketing team's MoM assigns "Rohit to prepare the festive campaign budget by 15th — status check next Monday." This makes accountability clear.

Key points to remember: MoM = date, attendees, agenda, decisions, action items (person + deadline), next meeting date; circulate within 24-48 hours; follow-up converts decisions into results.

Takeaway: Minutes without follow-up are just paper — track every action item at the next meeting or the same topics return.

Q5(a) Effective Business Writing: Direct vs Indirect Approach[5]

Answer: Effective business writing is clear, concise, complete, correct and courteous; the direct approach leads with the main point (for good news), while the indirect approach opens with a buffer before delivering bad news.

Effective business writing has five characteristics (the 5 C's): Clarity (unambiguous), Conciseness (brief), Completeness (all needed information), Correctness (error-free), and Courtesy (polite tone). Good writers also use the active voice ("We completed the project" not "The project was completed"), short paragraphs of 3-5 sentences, simple words, and bullet points for lists.

The direct approach puts the main point first, followed by details and a courteous close. It is used for good news, routine requests, and positive responses. Example: "Your leave has been approved for December 20-25."

The indirect approach opens with a buffer, then gives reasons, then states the bad news, and ends on a positive note. It is used for bad news, sensitive topics, and persuasive messages. Example: "We appreciate your application. Due to current requirements, we have decided..." — the bad news comes after the reasons, so the reader is prepared.

Example: A rejection letter should never start with "You are rejected". It should soften the blow, explain why, and close with encouragement.

Key points to remember: 5 C's of writing; direct = main point first (good news); indirect = buffer → reasons → bad news → positive close; proofread before sending.

Takeaway: Good news goes direct, bad news goes indirect — readers forgive delayed bad news, never a blunt opening that humiliates them.

Q5(b) Types of Business Reports and Report Structure[5]

Answer: Business reports are informational, analytical, proposal or compliance documents, structured as Title Page → Executive Summary → TOC → Introduction → Findings → Conclusions → Recommendations → Appendices.

Business reports are long, structured documents. There are four types:

  • Informational: Present facts objectively, like a monthly sales report.
  • Analytical: Analyse data and recommend action, like a feasibility study.
  • Proposal: Persuade the reader to act, like a project or funding request.
  • Compliance: Meet regulatory requirements, like an audit report.

The standard structure is: Title Page → Executive Summary → Table of Contents → Introduction → Findings/Analysis (the largest section) → Conclusions → Recommendations → Appendices and References.

The executive summary is the most critical part because many senior executives read only this section. It must be 1-2 pages, standalone, and cover the problem, methodology, key findings, and recommendations.

Example: SEBI-mandated annual reports must follow the Companies Act 2013 format with the Director's Report, Auditor's Report, and financial statements — a compliance report with a fixed structure.

Key points to remember: Four report types (informational, analytical, proposal, compliance); structure = title → executive summary → TOC → introduction → findings → conclusions → recommendations; executives often read only the executive summary.

Takeaway: Write the executive summary last but make it standalone — it is the only part most decision-makers actually read.

Q6(a) Cross-Cultural Communication and Hofstede's Dimensions[5]

Answer: Cross-cultural communication means adapting your style to other cultures; Hofstede's dimensions (power distance, individualism, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation) and Hall's high/low-context distinction explain how communication styles differ across nations.

Cross-cultural communication means recognising how communication styles, values, and norms differ across cultures, and adapting your approach to interact effectively. It is vital for Indian IT companies like TCS and Infosys, whose employees work with clients worldwide.

Hofstede's cultural dimensions compare countries:

  • Power Distance: India is high (77) — hierarchy is respected; the USA is low (40) — more egalitarian.
  • Individualism: India is collective (48), the USA is highly individualistic (91).
  • Uncertainty Avoidance: Japan is high (92) — needs rules; India is low (40) — comfortable with ambiguity.
  • Long-Term Orientation: Japan is high (88), the USA is short-term (26).

High-context cultures (Japan, China, India) rely on context, non-verbal cues, indirect communication, and relationships before business. Low-context cultures (USA, Germany) prefer direct, explicit, written communication and contracts. Misunderstanding this is the number one cause of cross-cultural business failures.

Example: Tata's acquisition of Jaguar-Land Rover succeeded because Tata adapted to the British egalitarian culture instead of imposing Indian ways — adaptation, not assimilation.

Key points to remember: Hofstede = power distance, individualism, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation; high-context = implicit, relationship-first; low-context = explicit, direct; adapt your style to the culture.

Takeaway: Adaptation beats assumption — the same sentence can be blunt in Delhi, vague in Tokyo, and unclear in Berlin.

Q6(b) Ethics in Communication[5]

Answer: Ethical communication is honest, responsible, fair and transparent (the TRRF-T principles) — and managers must avoid lying, exaggeration, omission, manipulation, plagiarism and spin.

Ethical communication is honest, responsible, fair, and respectful. Its principles are summarised as TRRF-T: Truthfulness, Respect, Responsibility, Fairness, and Transparency.

Managers must avoid these unethical practices:

  • Lying: Deliberate falsehoods, like overstating product capabilities.
  • Exaggeration: "Best in class" claims without evidence.
  • Omission: Hiding key information, like not disclosing side effects.
  • Manipulation: Misleading influence, like fake urgency ("Only 2 left!").
  • Plagiarism: Using others' work without credit.
  • Spin: Biased framing, like calling layoffs "right-sizing".

Ethical communication also protects privacy (personal data under the IT Act 2000 and GDPR), respects confidentiality (NDAs for trade secrets), and supports whistleblowing under the Companies Act 2013. In a crisis, communicate with transparency, timeliness, consistency, empathy, and accountability.

Example: Johnson & Johnson's Tylenol recall of 1982 is the gold standard — immediate recall and full transparency, unlike Boeing's 737 MAX crisis where defensive communication worsened the damage.

Key points to remember: TRRF-T = Truthfulness, Respect, Responsibility, Fairness, Transparency; avoid lying, exaggeration, omission, manipulation, plagiarism, spin; crisis communication = transparent, timely, consistent, empathetic, accountable.

Takeaway: In a crisis, transparent communication (Tylenol) builds trust while defensive spin (Boeing 737 MAX) deepens the damage.

June 2022 Exam(MBF) - 8 questions, answer any 5

Q1. Explain the two-way model of communication.

Answer: The two-way model treats communication as an interactive loop in which the sender transmits a message and the receiver responds with feedback, so sender and receiver keep exchanging roles until mutual understanding is reached.

Key points:

  • It adds feedback — the missing element of the linear Shannon-Weaver model — making communication a dialogue, not a monologue.
  • The receiver decodes the message, then becomes a sender and encodes a response (verbal or non-verbal) back through a channel.
  • Both parties share a common field of experience; the more overlapping their backgrounds, the more accurate the decoding.
  • Noise (physical, semantic, psychological) can distort the message in either direction, so each side must confirm understanding.
  • Examples: a manager asking "Any questions?" after briefing a team; a sales call where the customer's objection triggers a revised pitch.

Takeaway: No feedback, no communication — a two-way model is a dialogue that ends only at mutual understanding.

Q2. Distinguish between verbal and non-verbal communication giving examples.

Answer: Verbal communication uses words (spoken or written), while non-verbal communication transmits meaning through body language, tone, space, time and appearance without words.

Key points:

  • Verbal — oral: face-to-face talks, phone calls, meetings, presentations. Verbal — written: letters, emails, reports, memos. Both are explicit and recordable.
  • Non-verbal: kinesics (gestures, posture, facial expressions), proxemics (personal space), haptics (touch, handshake), chronemics (use of time), paralanguage (pitch, pace) and artifacts (dress).
  • Verbal is conscious and structured; non-verbal is often unconscious and continuous — you can stop talking but not stop signalling.
  • Mehrabian's rule: only 7% of emotional impact is words, 38% tone, 55% body language — non-verbal dominates feelings.
  • Example: a manager says "Good work" (verbal) but frowns and checks the phone (non-verbal) — the employee believes the frown.

Takeaway: In emotional messages people trust your tone and body more than your words — keep all three aligned.

Q3. What are the different types of Informal or Grapevine Communication? Explain any two with the help of examples.

Answer: Grapevine communication is the informal network through which information spreads along personal and social contacts, bypassing the official hierarchy. Keith Davis identified four network patterns.

Key points:

  • Single Strand Chain: A tells B, B tells C, C tells D — a serial passing of the message, which gets distorted at each link. Example: a clerk tells a colleague, who tells another, who tells the cashier.
  • Gossip Chain: One person tells everyone else in the group. Example: the PA tells the whole office about the new boss.
  • Probability Chain: Each person passes the message to random individuals. Example: rumour spread at a bus stop among employees who happen to meet.
  • Cluster Chain: A tells selected friends, who each tell their own selected friends — the most common pattern in organisations.
  • The grapevine is fast, informal and 75-90% accurate; managers cannot kill it, but can feed it correct information and monitor it for sentiment.

Takeaway: Rumours travel by cluster chains — announce important news officially and quickly, or the grapevine will fill the gap with distortion.

Q4. Create a mind map for a hypothetical business plan and explain its contents.

Answer: A mind map is a visual brainstorming tool with the central idea in the middle and related ideas branching out. For a business plan (say, an organic snacks start-up), the core branches are the classic plan sections.

Key points:

  • Executive Summary: one-page snapshot of the business, mission and funding need.
  • Product/Service: what is sold, features, uniqueness (organic, preservative-free snacks).
  • Market Analysis: target customers, competitors, market size, trends.
  • Marketing Plan: price, distribution (online + retail), promotion, branding.
  • Operations: production process, suppliers, equipment, quality control.
  • Financials: startup cost, revenue forecast, break-even point, funding sources.
  • Team: founders, key roles, advisors.

Takeaway: A mind map turns a blank page into a structured plan — start from the centre and let every branch ask one question: who, what, how, how much.

Q5. Why do we need written communication? Discuss the role of written communication in organisations. Give examples.

Answer: Written communication is the permanent, precise and formal exchange of information through documents, and it is indispensable for organisations that must record, verify and legally protect their transactions.

Key points:

  • Permanent record: letters, contracts and minutes survive for years and settle disputes — unlike oral words.
  • Legal evidence: written agreements, appointment letters and audit reports are admissible proof in courts and with regulators (e.g. SEBI, RBI).
  • Wide, consistent reach: a circular or policy email reaches thousands of employees with exactly the same wording, eliminating distortion.
  • Precision and accountability: roles, deadlines and targets can be pinned to named people; e.g. an MoM assigning "Rohit to submit budget by 15th".
  • Organisational roles: memos (internal policy), letters (external relations), reports (decisions), proposals (persuasion), notices and minutes (records).
  • Limitations: slow, formal, no immediate feedback — so combine with oral channels for urgent or emotional matters.

Takeaway: Write it down when it matters — a verbal promise is wind, a written record is proof.

Q6. Discuss the concept of e-mail writing. Give suitable examples.

Answer: E-mail writing is the structured, professional use of electronic mail — the most common written channel in business — governed by etiquette that keeps it clear, formal and respectful.

Key points:

  • Subject line: specific and action-oriented, e.g. "Q2 Sales Report — Due 15 July" instead of "Report".
  • Salutation and close: match formality to the relationship — "Dear Mr. Sharma" vs "Hi Ritu"; close with "Regards/Best regards" and your full signature block.
  • Body: one topic per email, short paragraphs, bullets for lists, and a clear call to action with a deadline — "Please revert by Thursday 5 PM".
  • Etiquette: respond within 24 hours, use BCC for mass mailings, never ALL CAPS (shouting), avoid Reply-All unless needed, proofread before sending, keep attachments small.
  • Example: a leave request email opens with purpose, states dates and cover arrangements, and closes with a request for approval — all in six lines.

Takeaway: Every email is a permanent official record — write as if a court, a client or your CEO will read it.

Q7. What are the different components of a business proposal? Explain with examples.

Answer: A business proposal is a persuasive document that presents a plan to solve a problem and asks the reader to approve or fund it — either solicited (in response to an RFP) or unsolicited.

Key points:

  • Executive summary: problem, solution and ask in one page — the section busy decision-makers read.
  • Problem statement: the need the client has, e.g. "30% of calls are going unanswered".
  • Proposed solution: what you will do, how it works, and why it is better than alternatives.
  • Methodology / work plan: steps, phases and timeline with milestones.
  • Benefits and ROI: measurable outcomes, e.g. "cut call-abandonment by half in six months".
  • Budget and payment terms: costs, billing schedule, inclusions/exclusions.
  • Qualifications: team credentials, past projects, testimonials — why you are the right vendor.

Takeaway: A proposal sells a solution, not a service — every section must answer "what is the client's problem and how does this fix it?"

Q8. What are the principles of Ethical Communication? Explain the role of ethics in communication.

Answer: Ethical communication is honest, responsible, fair and respectful messaging — summarised by the TRRF-T principles — and it is the foundation of organisational trust and credibility.

Key points:

  • Principles (TRRF-T): Truthfulness (no lies or exaggeration), Respect (dignity of all parties), Responsibility (own your message), Fairness (balanced, complete views), Transparency (no hidden agendas or selective omission).
  • Role — trust: ethical communication builds stakeholder confidence; unethical spin destroys it in one crisis (compare J&J Tylenol with Boeing 737 MAX).
  • Role — compliance: protects against fraud, misrepresentation and regulatory action (Companies Act 2013, GDPR, IT Act).
  • Role — employees: honest downward communication creates a safe voice culture and supports whistleblowing.
  • Avoid: lying, exaggeration, omission, manipulation, plagiarism, false urgency and biased framing ("right-sizing" for layoffs).

Takeaway: Ethics is not a constraint on communication — it is what makes communication believable in the first place.

December 2022 Exam(MBA) - 8 questions, answer any 5

Q1. Differentiate between Proxemics and Chronemics giving examples.

Answer: Proxemics is the study of personal space and physical distance in communication, while chronemics is the study of how people use and perceive time as a message.

Key points:

  • Proxemics: Edward Hall's distance zones — intimate (0-45 cm), personal (45-120 cm), social (120-360 cm), public (3.6 m+). Business talks sit in the social zone.
  • Proxemics example: standing 30 cm from a client feels aggressive; 4 feet is the formal distance Indians maintain with clients and seniors.
  • Chronemics: punctuality, waiting time, response speed and time allocation all send messages about respect and status.
  • Chronemics example: arriving 20 minutes late for a vendor meeting signals disrespect; replying to an email within an hour signals urgency and commitment.
  • Both are non-verbal dimensions, culture-specific: monochronic cultures (Germany, USA) do one thing at a time; polychronic cultures (India, Latin America) handle several at once.

Takeaway: Space and time talk — control your distance and your punctuality as deliberately as your words.

Q2. What are the 7 C's of communication? Explain each of them briefly.

Answer: The 7 C's are the seven standards a message must meet to be effective: Clarity, Conciseness, Concreteness, Correctness, Coherence, Completeness and Courtesy.

Key points:

  • Clarity: easy to understand; simple words, one idea per sentence.
  • Conciseness: brief; only what the receiver needs — time is money.
  • Concreteness: specific and vivid — "5 PM Friday" not "soon".
  • Correctness: accurate facts, grammar, spelling and figures.
  • Coherence: ideas connected logically so the message flows.
  • Completeness: answers what, why, when, where, who, how.
  • Courtesy: respectful, receiver-focused tone ("You" attitude).

Takeaway: A message weak in any one C fails — use the list as a pre-send checklist.

Q3. Classify different types of verbal communication and discuss them in brief.

Answer: Verbal communication, which uses words, is classified by channel into oral and written forms, and each form serves different purposes in business.

Key points:

  • Oral communication: face-to-face conversations, meetings, telephone calls, presentations, interviews — fast, personal, with instant feedback; suitable for discussion and negotiation.
  • Written communication: letters, emails, memos, reports, proposals, notices — permanent, precise, legal record; suitable for policies, contracts and documentation.
  • Internal verbal: within the organisation — memos, circulars, intranet, team meetings.
  • External verbal: with outsiders — press releases, advertisements, client letters, annual reports.
  • Formal vs informal: official channels (hierarchy) vs casual talk (grapevine, chit-chat).

Q5. Explain the functional theory of communication and its role in effective decision-making process.

  • Goal setting: criteria for an acceptable solution are established (cost, time, feasibility).

  • Evaluation of alternatives: each option is tested against the criteria — positives and negatives weighed.
  • Role: when groups perform all four functions well, decision quality improves; missing any function (e.g. skipping evaluation) produces poor decisions.
  • Built on Dewey's reflective thinking and Janis's work; warns against groupthink when discussion suppresses dissent.

Takeaway: Great decisions need four communication functions — problem, criteria, options, evaluation; skip one and the decision suffers.

Takeaway: Downward commands, upward informs — a healthy organisation keeps both channels open and honest.

Q2(b). Explain the concept of Gang Plank in communication and workplace and its advantages and disadvantages.

Answer: The Gang Plank (Fayol) is a horizontal communication bridge that lets employees at the same level in different departments communicate directly, bypassing the long route up and down the scalar chain.

Key points:

  • Concept: in the scalar chain, F and G (different departments) normally reach each other via E-D-C-B-A; the gang plank lets F talk to G directly for routine matters.
  • Speed: direct contact saves the time of climbing up and down the hierarchy — the biggest advantage.
  • Coordination: same-level peers in sales and production align daily without burdening the vice president.
  • Flexibility: faster problem-solving and decisions at the operating level.
  • Disadvantages: bypasses authority — seniors may lose control; risk of miscommunication because no formal record; may undermine the chain when used for decisions beyond routine; requires discipline to keep seniors informed.

Takeaway: The gang plank is speed with a price — use it for routine coordination, keep authority informed.

Q3. Explain different types of verbal communication giving suitable examples.

Answer: Verbal communication uses words and divides by channel into oral and written, each with further types by direction and formality.

Key points:

  • Oral: face-to-face talks, meetings, phone calls, presentations, interviews — instant feedback; example: a weekly team stand-up.
  • Written: letters, emails, memos, reports, proposals — permanent record; example: a formal report to the board.
  • Internal vs external: memos and intranet inside; press releases, client letters, annual reports outside.
  • Formal vs informal: official hierarchy channels vs casual conversation and grapevine.
  • One-way vs two-way: broadcast announcements (no feedback) vs dialogues (feedback completes the loop).
  • Verbal plus non-verbal: every oral message also carries tone and body language (7-38-55).

Takeaway: Verbal types are channel choices — oral for speed and feeling, written for record and reach.

Q4. How can the managers resolve conflict at workplace? Explain giving examples.

Answer: Managers resolve workplace conflict through diagnosis, dialogue and negotiation — turning disagreement into agreement without damaging relationships.

Key points:

  • Identify the true source: resources, communication, roles, or personalities — e.g. two teams fighting over a room need a booking system, not a referee.
  • Hear all sides: private meetings first, then joint discussion with active listening and ground rules.
  • Separate people from problems: discuss behaviour and impact, not character — "late handoffs delay the project" beats "you're lazy".
  • Focus on interests: what each party really needs (recognition, resources, respect), then seek win-win options.
  • Agree on criteria and follow up: decide by objective rules (cost, fairness, deadlines), document the agreement, and review at the next meeting.
  • Escalate professionally: mediation, then arbitration by a senior with documentation — never let it fester.

Takeaway: A resolved conflict builds trust; an ignored one builds grievances — manage it early and fairly.

Q5. What do you understand by a 'Research Report'? Describe with the help of suitable examples.

Answer: A research report is a formal document that presents a systematic investigation — its objectives, methodology, findings and recommendations — in a structured format for decision-makers.

Key points:

  • Nature: fact-based, objective, evidence-driven — it reports what the study found, not what the writer hopes.
  • Structure: title page → executive summary → TOC → introduction (objectives, scope) → methodology → findings and analysis → conclusions → recommendations → appendices and references.
  • Types: market research reports, feasibility studies, academic dissertations, industry surveys, audit reports.
  • Example: a market research report on "Customer preference for digital payments in tier-2 cities" — survey of 1,000 users, findings on UPI vs cards, recommendations for a bank's app roadmap.
  • Example: a feasibility report on opening a new warehouse — location analysis, cost projections, break-even, and a go/no-go recommendation.
  • Quality: accuracy of data, clear methodology, honest interpretation, actionable recommendations.

Takeaway: A research report converts raw data into decisions — its credibility rests on methodology and honesty.

Q6. What are the two prominent skills which are required for effective reading? Explain with the help of examples.

Answer: The two prominent reading skills are skimming and scanning — skimming for the overall idea, scanning for a specific detail; both save time on the huge volume of documents managers face.

Key points:

  • Skimming: reading quickly through headings, first sentences and summaries to grasp the gist — like checking a report's executive summary before deciding to read the full text.
  • Scanning: moving the eyes deliberately over text to locate one fact — like finding the payment date in a contract or a price in a catalogue.
  • Difference: skimming answers "what is this about?", scanning answers "where is X?"
  • Supporting skills: critical reading (evaluating arguments) and SQ3R (deep study) complete the toolkit.
  • Example: an auditor skims a 200-page annual report, scans for the audit-opinion paragraph, and reads critically only the note on provisions.
  • Example: an IAS officer processing 200+ files a day skims each file for the issue and scans for the deadline.

Takeaway: Skim to understand, scan to find — the two skills together let one person read what used to take a team.

Q7. Distinguish between a video resume and curriculum-vitae. Explain the significance of the two with respect to communication for employment.

Answer: A CV is a comprehensive written document of academic and professional history, while a video resume is a short filmed self-presentation — both are employment communication tools with different strengths.

Key points:

  • CV: detailed, chronological, text-based — education, publications, experience, achievements; long-form and factual.
  • Video resume: 1-2 minute recorded pitch — who you are, key strengths, why you fit — showing personality, voice and confidence.
  • Significance of CV: the formal, verifiable record — ATS-scanned, archived, used for shortlisting and official records.
  • Significance of video: communicates communication skill itself — tone, body language and energy (7-38-55) that paper cannot carry; differentiates the candidate.
  • Combined use: the video gets attention, the CV provides proof — creative and marketing roles favour video, corporate roles favour the CV.
  • Example: a candidate sends a 90-second video introduction with a two-page CV — the video wins the interview, the CV documents the claims.

Takeaway: The CV is your record, the video your impression — in employment communication, send both.

Q8(a). Describe various messaging tools which can be used for different types of communication.

Answer: Messaging tools are the digital channels for exchanging messages; the right tool depends on urgency, formality and the need for a record.

Key points:

  • Email: formal, documented, one-to-many — for official communication, approvals and records; respond within 24 hours.
  • Instant messaging (IM): WhatsApp, Teams chat, Slack — quick queries, team coordination, informal tone; fast feedback, poor record discipline.
  • SMS: short urgent alerts — OTPs, delivery updates, one-line reminders.
  • Video conferencing: Zoom/Teams calls — rich media for discussions, negotiations and remote meetings with non-verbal cues.
  • Collaboration platforms: Slack channels, Microsoft Teams, project tools (Trello, Asana) — structured discussion with threads and searchable history.
  • Choosing: urgent = IM/phone; formal = email; sensitive/emotional = video or face-to-face; routine = email or notice.

Takeaway: The tool is part of the message — match urgency and formality to the channel.

Q8(b). Distinguish between rich media and lean media giving examples.

Answer: Rich media carry multiple cues and instant feedback (high richness); lean media carry few cues with delayed feedback — the match between message ambiguity and media richness determines success (Daft & Lengel).

Key points:

  • Rich media: face-to-face, video call, phone — add tone, body language, immediacy; best for complex, ambiguous or emotional messages.
  • Lean media: email, memo, letter, notice, report — text only, one-way, delayed response; best for routine, clear, well-understood information.
  • Examples of rich use: negotiating a merger clause, delivering performance feedback, apologising to a client.
  • Examples of lean use: circular on office timings, meeting reminder, standard policy document.
  • Mismatch cost: announcing layoffs by email (too lean) feels cold and breeds anger; a routine holiday notice by video call (too rich) wastes time.

Takeaway: Richness should rise with ambiguity — never say in a memo what needs a face.

Q1. Differentiate between intrapersonal and interpersonal barriers giving examples.

Answer: Intrapersonal barriers are obstacles inside the individual (perception, emotions, language), while interpersonal barriers arise between individuals in their interaction (trust, status, listening, relationships).

Key points:

  • Intrapersonal — perception: filtering reality through your own beliefs — a manager hears "suggestion" as "criticism".
  • Intrapersonal — emotions: anger or anxiety blocks reception — an anxious employee misreads a routine email as a warning.
  • Intrapersonal — language and attitude: limited vocabulary, jargon, prejudice and defensive attitude distort encoding and decoding.
  • Interpersonal — trust and credibility: if the sender is distrusted, the message is discounted — a manager with a history of spin gets ignored.
  • Interpersonal — status and hierarchy: juniors withhold questions and bad news from seniors.
  • Interpersonal — listening and clash: interrupting, rehearsing replies, personality clashes and cultural differences distort the exchange.
  • Example contrast: a biased reader misreads a neutral message (intrapersonal); two colleagues who dislike each other misinterpret everything they say (interpersonal).

Takeaway: The enemy is both inside and between us — fix your own filters first, then build trust with the other person.

Q2. What kind of communication is the sign language? Discuss the importance of sign language. Cite relevant examples to support your answer.

Answer: Sign language is a non-verbal (visual-gestural) form of communication — a structured system of hand signs, facial expressions and body movements that conveys complete meaning without spoken words.

Key points:

  • Kind: non-verbal communication in the kinesics family, but systematic — it is a full-fledged language (ISL in India, ASL in America) with grammar and vocabulary, not just gestures.
  • Inclusion: it is the primary language of the deaf community — enabling education, employment and participation.
  • Overcoming noise and distance: hand signals work where voice fails — factory floors, construction sites, air-traffic marshalling, trading floors.
  • Discretion: silent signalling in meetings — a thumbs-up to the speaker, a raised hand to interrupt politely.
  • Examples: crane operators' hand signals, traffic police gestures, sports umpire signals, stock-exchange open-outcry hand signs.
  • Example: in a deaf-inclusive bank branch, tellers trained in basic ISL turn a frustrating visit into a smooth transaction.

Takeaway: Sign language proves communication needs no sound — and its structure shows non-verbal systems can be as precise as speech.

Q3. Explain different types of informal communication. Illustrate with the help of examples.

Answer: Informal communication is the grapevine — messages flowing through personal and social networks outside official channels, with four classic network patterns (Keith Davis).

Key points:

  • Single Strand: A → B → C → D — serial relay that distorts with every link; example: a clerk tells one colleague, who tells another, who tells the cashier.
  • Gossip Chain: one person tells everyone; example: the PA spreads news of the new boss to the whole floor.
  • Probability Chain: random sharing; example: a rumour passed to whoever happens to sit nearby at lunch.
  • Cluster Chain (most common): A tells selected friends, who each tell their friends; example: promotion news spreading through friendship circles.
  • Characteristics: fast, flexible, 75-90% accurate, carries social and emotional content — but also rumours; managers use it to gauge sentiment and must feed it accurate news.

Takeaway: The grapevine runs whether you like it or not — manage it by open, timely official communication.

Q4. Outline the different components of 'Reading' as one of the language skills. Explain with the help of examples, different types of Reading Skills.

Answer: Reading is one of the four language skills (listening, speaking, reading, writing) — the receptive skill of decoding written symbols into meaning, built on several components and practised through distinct techniques.

Key points:

  • Components: word recognition (decoding letters and words), vocabulary (knowing word meanings), fluency (smooth, automatic reading), comprehension (constructing meaning), and critical evaluation (judging the text).
  • Skimming: quick overview for the gist — example: reading a report's summary to decide whether to read it fully.
  • Scanning: searching for one specific fact — example: finding the penalty clause in a vendor contract.
  • Critical reading: evaluating evidence and arguments — example: assessing a market research claim of "90% satisfaction".
  • SQ3R: Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review — example: studying an RBI circular for an exam: survey headings, question them, read, recite key points, review.
  • Purpose: managers read 200+ files daily — matching skill to document (skim routine, scan facts, criticise proposals) saves hours.

Takeaway: Reading is a skill stack — recognition, vocabulary, fluency and comprehension, applied through skimming, scanning, critical reading and SQ3R.

Q5. "The growth of every organization is driven by the ability of management to make effective decisions." Elaborate upon the statement and discuss the role of group communication in effective decision-making.

Answer: Effective decisions are the engine of organisational growth — and because decision quality depends on information and analysis, group communication is the vehicle through which good decisions are produced and owned.

Key points:

  • The statement: growth flows from choosing the right markets, products, people and investments — each a decision; organisations out-decide their competitors.
  • More information: groups pool knowledge no single manager holds — a cross-functional team sees technical, financial and customer angles.
  • Multiple perspectives: dissent and debate test assumptions and prevent groupthink — functional theory: analyse the problem, set criteria, generate options, evaluate them.
  • Commitment: members who participate in the decision implement it with ownership instead of resistance.
  • Communication functions: agenda-setting, information sharing, discussion, voting and documentation — meetings convert individual ideas into collective decisions.
  • Risks: groupthink, domination by vocal members, and time cost — managed by structured discussion, devil's advocate roles and clear decision rules.
  • Example: Tata's decision on Nano's location followed cross-functional feasibility debate; Amazon's "disagree and commit" institutionalises debate then decisiveness.

Takeaway: Decisions grow organisations, and communication grows decisions — structured group dialogue is the highest-leverage management tool.

Q6(a). Write short note on Short Business Correspondence.

Answer: Short business correspondence is the concise, routine written communication used for day-to-day operations — quick to write, quick to read, and formatted for instant scanning.

Key points:

  • Forms: emails, memos, notices, circulars, reminders, acknowledgements, short letters (enquiry, order, confirmation).
  • Features: brief (a few lines to a page), one subject per document, standard formats (To/From/Date/Subject), formal but friendly tone.
  • Memo: internal note for policy, instructions and announcements.
  • Notices and circulars: one announcement to many people — meetings, holidays, rule changes.
  • Email: the dominant digital form — clear subject, one topic, call to action, professional close.
  • Contrast: unlike long correspondence (reports, proposals), short correspondence carries routine information, not analysis.

Takeaway: Short correspondence is the daily pulse of the organisation — keep it short, standard and action-oriented.

Q6(b). Write short note on Foreign words and phrases.

Answer: Foreign words and phrases are Latin, French and other terms used in business correspondence for precision and international convention — they compress legal and financial meaning into single expressions.

Key points:

  • Common Latin terms: per annum (per year), ad hoc (for this purpose), bona fide (in good faith), status quo (existing state), vice versa (the reverse), pro-rata (proportionally), ex-gratia (as a favour, not obligation), prima facie (on first view), in toto (entirely).
  • French/other terms: agenda (things to be done), memorandum (to be remembered), carte blanche (full freedom), en route (on the way).
  • Uses: contracts ("payment pro-rata to work done"), legal notices (bona fide purchaser), official records (ad hoc committee).
  • Caution: use only well-known terms — obscure foreign phrases confuse readers and violate the 7 C's (clarity, courtesy).
  • Example: "The board met ad hoc to approve the ex-gratia payment in toto" — precise to a lawyer, jargon to a new clerk.

Takeaway: Foreign phrases are shorthand for experts — use them for precision, drop them for clarity.

Q7. List and explain the components of letter-text combination form of reports.

Answer: The letter-text combination is a report format in which a letter or memo transmits the report, and the report itself follows as structured text — combining the personal touch of a letter with the rigour of a formal document.

Key points:

  • 1. Letter/memo of transmittal: one page covering why the report was prepared, its highlights, and an offer of further discussion.
  • 2. Title page: title, author, organisation, date of submission.
  • 3. Table of contents: headings and page numbers for navigation.
  • 4. Executive summary: purpose, method, key findings and recommendations in one to two pages — the decision-maker's section.
  • 5. Introduction: background, scope, objectives and method of study.
  • 6. Findings and analysis: the body — facts, data, tables and charts, logically organised.
  • 7. Conclusions and recommendations: what the findings mean and what to do; followed by appendices and references.

Takeaway: Letter-text format = warm cover + rigorous content — the standard way to deliver formal reports to decision-makers.
