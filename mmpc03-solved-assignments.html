Q1. Define business environment. Explain its characteristics and importance for managerial decision-making.

Answer: Business environment refers to the totality of all factors which are external to and beyond the control of individual business enterprises and their managements. It furnishes the macro-context within which firms operate and includes the value system of society, government rules and regulations, monetary policies of the central bank, the institutional setup of the country, and attitudes towards foreign capital and enterprise. The environment has five key characteristics. It is dynamic, changing constantly due to customer preferences, competitors, technology, and government policy. It is uncertain, since factors fluctuate quickly and are hard to predict. It is complex, exposed to technological disruptions, global competition, and shifting regulatory conditions. It shows relativity, varying from country to country and region to region, and it is interrelated, as all factors influence each other. Studying the environment is important because decisions must consider possible environmental reactions; marketing managers study the market before product launches, finance managers study capital and money market structures, and personnel managers must understand government rules on reservations. Environmental analysis helps managers identify opportunities and threats, enabling firms such as Reliance Jio to convert environmental scanning into competitive advantage.

Key points:

  • Business environment refers to the totality of all factors which are external to and beyond the control of individual business enterprises and their managements.
  • Environmental analysis helps managers identify opportunities and threats, enabling firms such as Reliance Jio to convert environmental scanning into competitive advantage.
  • It furnishes the macro-context within which firms operate and includes the value system of society, government rules and regulations, monetary policies of the central bank, the institutional setup of the country, and attitudes towards foreign capital and enterprise.

Takeaway: Environmental analysis helps managers identify opportunities and threats, enabling firms such as Reliance Jio to convert environmental scanning into competitive advantage.

Q2. Distinguish between economic growth and economic development with suitable Indian examples.

Answer: Economic growth refers to the increase in the real output of goods and services over time, typically measured by the GDP growth rate. It is a quantitative concept. Economic development is broader: it encompasses improvements in living standards, including income distribution, health and education outcomes, infrastructure development, and reduction in poverty levels. Development is measured through indicators such as GDP per capita, the Human Development Index (HDI), poverty ratio, Gini coefficient, infant mortality rate, and literacy rate. The determinants of growth include physical capital, human capital, natural resources, technology, and institutional factors such as property rights and political stability. India illustrates the distinction through two contrasting state models. Gujarat focuses on industrialization and infrastructure, achieving high GDP growth of about 10%, while Kerala prioritizes human development, with the highest literacy rate of 96% and the best HDI in India. Gujarat has higher GDP; Kerala has better social indicators. The example shows that a country can have growth without development and that the ideal strategy combines both approaches, which is why NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission in 2015 to pursue outcome-based, bottom-up development planning.

Key points:

  • Economic growth refers to the increase in the real output of goods and services over time, typically measured by the GDP growth rate.
  • Development is measured through indicators such as GDP per capita, the Human Development Index (HDI), poverty ratio, Gini coefficient, infant mortality rate, and literacy rate.
  • It is a quantitative concept.

Takeaway: The example shows that a country can have growth without development and that the ideal strategy combines both approaches, which is why NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission in 2015 to pursue outcome-based, bottom-up development planning.

Q3. Explain the New Economic Policy of 1991 and its three components of LPG reforms.

Answer: The New Economic Policy of 1991 was introduced in response to the severe Balance of Payments crisis, when India's foreign exchange reserves fell to about two weeks of imports and gold had to be pledged. The policy is popularly known as LPG reforms. Liberalization meant removing the license raj, industrial delicensing, opening the economy to FDI, and freeing forex and tax regimes, including the introduction of service tax. Privatization involved disinvestment of government equity in public sector undertakings and reducing the state's role in production. Globalization meant opening Indian markets to international trade and investment and integrating the economy with global markets. The Narasimham Committee's recommendations shaped banking and financial sector reforms that accompanied the policy. The impact was dramatic: average GDP growth rose from the 3.5% Hindu rate of growth to 6-7% in the post-reform period, FDI grew from negligible levels to over 80 billion dollars annually, and India moved from a license raj to a market-oriented economy. The reforms transformed the business environment for firms, which now had to compete with global players while gaining access to technology, capital, and larger markets.

Key points:

  • The New Economic Policy of 1991 was introduced in response to the severe Balance of Payments crisis, when India's foreign exchange reserves fell to about two weeks of imports and gold had to be pledged.
  • Globalization meant opening Indian markets to international trade and investment and integrating the economy with global markets.
  • The policy is popularly known as LPG reforms.

Takeaway: The reforms transformed the business environment for firms, which now had to compete with global players while gaining access to technology, capital, and larger markets.

Q4. What is Balance of Payments? Explain its structure and the causes of India's Current Account Deficit.

Answer: The Balance of Payments (BoP) is a systematic record of all economic transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world during a given period. It has two major components. The Current Account records trade in goods and services, income, and unilateral transfers such as remittances. The Capital Account records investment flows, including foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, and loans. A deficit on the current account, known as the Current Account Deficit (CAD), arises when payments for imports exceed receipts from exports and transfers. In India, the CAD is driven mainly by oil imports and gold imports, which dominate the import bill. It is offset partly by strong services exports, particularly IT and software, and by remittances, where India is the largest recipient globally at over 100 billion dollars annually, with Gulf countries as a major source. The 1991 crisis illustrates the danger of an unsustainable BoP position, when forex reserves fell to 1.2 billion dollars and India approached the IMF. Today, the RBI manages forex reserves and the rupee has moved from a fixed peg to a managed float, with the exchange rate largely market-determined.

  • The Balance of Payments (BoP) is a systematic record of all economic transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world during a given period.
  • The Current Account records trade in goods and services, income, and unilateral transfers such as remittances.
  • It has two major components.
