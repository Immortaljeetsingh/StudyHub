Block-01: Research Fundamentals

Unit 1: Research Methodology — An Overview

▶
What is Research Methodology?
▶
Research Methods vs Methodology
📖 Core Concepts
  • Research = Purposeful investigation using scientific methods to find answers to questions
  • Research Methodology = The systematic approach to conducting research (the "how")
  • Research Methods = Specific techniques/tools for data collection and analysis (the "tools")
  • Research is NOT a "fishing expedition" — it must be focused and purposeful
📊 5 Features of Good Research (S-O-R-C)
#FeatureMeaning
1SystematicFollows a structured plan
2ObjectiveUnbiased, fact-based
3ReproducibleOthers can replicate and verify
4ControlledVariables are managed
5RelevantAddresses a meaningful problem
📖 Types of Research
ClassificationTypesWhen to Use
By ApplicationPure (theoretical) vs Applied (practical)Pure for knowledge, Applied for solving problems
By ObjectiveDescriptive, Correlational, ExplanatoryDescribe, relate, or explain phenomena
By EnquiryQualitative vs QuantitativeWords/meanings vs numbers/statistics
By Data SourcePrimary vs SecondaryNew collection vs existing data
📖 Key Definitions
  • Variable: Any characteristic that can take different values (age, income, satisfaction)
  • Dependent Variable (DV): The outcome being measured (effect)
  • Independent Variable (IV): The factor being manipulated (cause)
  • Hypothesis: A testable statement about the relationship between variables
  • Theory: A set of interrelated concepts that explains phenomena
📖 Business Research Benefits
  • Reduces uncertainty in decision-making
  • Identifies market threats and opportunities
  • Forecasts revenue and demand
  • Provides competitive advantage
  • Supports evidence-based management
💡 Real-World Example

A company launching a new product conducts market research to understand consumer preferences (qualitative), surveys 1000 customers (quantitative), and uses the data to set pricing strategy (applied research). The research team uses both primary data (surveys, focus groups) and secondary data (industry reports, competitor analysis) to build a comprehensive market understanding.

🧠 Memory Aid

"SO-RC" — Systematic, Objective, Reproducible, Controlled (features of good research)

⚠ Exam Tip

Always distinguish between "Research Methods" (specific techniques) and "Research Methodology" (the overall approach/philosophy). This is a frequent exam question. Also know the difference between Pure (theoretical) and Applied (practical) research.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Research = Purposeful, systematic investigation using scientific methods
  • 5 features: Systematic, Objective, Reproducible, Controlled, Relevant
  • Types: Pure vs Applied, Descriptive/Correlational/Explanatory, Qualitative vs Quantitative
  • Key variables: IV (cause), DV (effect), hypothesis, theory
  • Business research reduces uncertainty and supports evidence-based management

📖 Key Definitions
  • Research Problem: A question, issue, or situation that needs investigation
  • Literature Review: Systematic survey of existing published work to identify gaps
  • Null Hypothesis (H₀): Statement of "no effect" or "no difference" (what we test)
  • Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): Statement of "effect exists" (what we want to prove)
  • Research Design: The master plan specifying methods and procedures for data collection and analysis
📖 Types of Hypotheses
  • Descriptive: States existence of something ("60% of customers prefer online shopping")
  • Relational: States relationship between variables ("Price affects demand")
  • Causal: States cause-effect ("Training improves productivity")
📖 Type I and Type II Errors
ErrorWhat HappensAnalogy
Type I (α)Reject H₀ when H₀ is trueFalse positive — convicting an innocent person
Type II (β)Fail to reject H₀ when H₁ is trueFalse negative — letting a guilty person go free
💡 Real-World Example

A manager notices declining sales (problem) → reviews industry reports (literature) → hypothesizes "Poor customer service is causing decline" → designs a customer satisfaction survey → collects data → analyzes with statistical tests → writes report recommending service improvements.

🧠 Memory Aid

"D-L-H-R-D-A-R" — Define, Literature, Hypothesis, Research Design, Data, Analysis, Report

⚠ Exam Tip

The hypothesis testing section is very important. Know the difference between H₀ and H₁, and understand Type I and Type II errors. Also know the 3 types of hypotheses: Descriptive, Relational, Causal.

✅ Unit Summary

  • 7 steps: Define → Literature → Hypothesis → Design → Collect → Analyze → Report
  • Null vs Alternative hypothesis — know the difference
  • Types of hypotheses: Descriptive, Relational, Causal
  • Type I error = false positive; Type II error = false negative

📖 Three Main Research Designs
DesignPurposeMethodsWhen to Use
ExploratoryExplore unclear problemLiterature search, expert surveys, focus groupsProblem is vague, need insights
DescriptiveDescribe population characteristicsSurveys, case studies, observationKnow what, need details
ExperimentalEstablish cause-effectLab experiments, field experimentsNeed to prove causation
📖 Lab vs Field Experiments
FeatureLab ExperimentField Experiment
EnvironmentArtificial, controlledNatural, real-world
Internal ValidityHIGH (controls)LOWER (less control)

CostLowerHigher
ExampleTesting ad recall in labTesting ad impact in real market
📖 Key Definitions
  • Internal Validity: Degree of confidence that the IV caused the observed change in DV
  • External Validity: Degree to which results can be generalized to other settings and populations
  • Construct Validity: Are we measuring what we think we're measuring?
⚠ Threats to Internal Validity
  • History — external events during the study
  • Maturation — natural changes in subjects
  • Testing — being tested affects performance
  • Selection bias — non-random group assignment
  • Mortality — subjects dropping out
💡 Real-World Example

A company wants to test if a new training program improves sales. Exploratory: Interview top salespeople. Descriptive: Survey all salespeople. Experimental: Give training to one group (treatment), not another (control), compare results.

🧠 Memory Aid

"E-D-E" — Exploratory, Descriptive, Experimental (three research designs)

⚠ Exam Tip

Internal vs External validity trade-off is a classic exam question. Lab = high internal, low external. Field = low internal, high external.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Three designs: Exploratory (explore), Descriptive (describe), Experimental (prove cause-effect)
  • Lab = high control, high internal validity; Field = realistic, high external validity
  • Threats to validity: History, Maturation, Testing, Selection, Mortality

📖 Reliability vs Validity
  • Reliability: Consistency — does the scale give the same result repeatedly?
  • Validity: Accuracy — does the scale measure what it claims to measure?
  • A scale can be reliable but not valid (consistent but wrong), but not valid without reliable

  • Reliability = consistency; Validity = accuracy
  • Types: Test-retest, Split-half, Inter-rater, Cronbach's Alpha

📖 Question Wording Rules
  • Use simple, clear, concrete language
  • Avoid leading questions ("Don't you think...")
  • Avoid double-barreled questions (two questions in one)
  • Avoid ambiguous terms ("regularly," "often")
  • Make response options mutually exclusive and exhaustive
💡 Real-World Example

A hospital designing a patient satisfaction questionnaire: Opens with easy questions (visit date, department) → core questions (waiting time, staff behavior) → sensitive questions (income, overall rating) → includes Likert scales and open-ended comments.

🧠 Memory Aid

"S-S-W-P" — Sequence (logical flow), Simplicity (clear language), Wording (avoid bias), Pre-test (validate before use)

✅ Unit Summary

  • Three aspects: General Form, Question Sequence, Question Wording
  • Sequence: Easy → Core → Sensitive → Demographics
  • Pre-test on 10-20 people before full deployment
  • Avoid leading, double-barreled, ambiguous questions

📖 Key Definitions
  • Sampling: Selecting a subset of the population to study
  • Census: Complete enumeration of every element in the population
  • Sampling Frame: The list from which sample is drawn
  • Sampling Error: Difference between sample statistic and population parameter
  • Central Limit Theorem: Sampling distribution of the mean approaches normal as n ≥ 30
💡 Real-World Example

A company surveys employee satisfaction (5000 employees): Stratified sampling by department (HR, Sales, Tech) → random from each → 350 employees (7%) gives 95% confidence with ±5% margin.

  • Non-Probability: Convenience, Judgment, Quota, Snowball
  • Central Limit Theorem: n ≥ 30 → sampling distribution approaches normal
  • Sample size factors: variability, precision, confidence level, budget

▶
Nonparametric Tests
▶
Chi-Square Test
📖 When to Use Nonparametric vs Parametric
FeatureParametricNonparametric
DistributionAssumes normalNo assumption
Data typeInterval/RatioNominal/Ordinal
Sample sizeLarge (30+)Any size
PowerHigherLower
Examplest-test, ANOVAChi-square, Mann-Whitney
📖 Nonparametric Test Selection Guide
SituationTestParametric Equivalent
1 sample, categoricalChi-square Goodness of Fit—
2 independent samplesMann-Whitney UIndependent t-test
2 related samplesWilcoxon Signed-RankPaired t-test
3+ independent samplesKruskal-WallisOne-way ANOVA
Association, categoricalChi-square Test of Independence—
📖 Chi-Square Formula

χ² = ∑ [(O - E)² / E]

  • O = Observed frequency
  • E = Expected frequency
  • Degrees of Freedom: df = (rows - 1) × (columns - 1)
  • Decision: If χ² calculated > χ² critical, reject H₀
💡 Real-World Example

A company compares customer satisfaction across 3 branches (ordinal data: 1-5 scale). Since data is ordinal and may not be normal, use Kruskal-Wallis instead of ANOVA.

🧠 Memory Aid

"C-M-W-K-F" — Chi-square, Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, Friedman

⚠ Exam Tip

Know WHEN to use each test, not just the formula. Examiners often give a scenario and ask which test is appropriate.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Nonparametric = distribution-free, use when data is ordinal/nominal or sample is small
  • Key tests: Chi-square, Mann-Whitney U, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, Friedman
  • Chi-square formula: χ² = ∑ [(O - E)² / E]

📖 Key Multivariate Techniques
TechniquePurposeWhen to Use

Discriminant AnalysisClassify into groupsGroup classification

Cluster AnalysisGroup similar objectsMarket segmentation
MANOVACompare groups on multiple DVsMulti-outcome comparisons
📖 Multiple Regression

Equation: Y = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + ... + bₙXₙ

  • Y = Dependent variable (predicted)
  • a = Intercept (constant)
  • b₁, b₂ = Regression coefficients (slopes)
  • R² = Proportion of variance in Y explained by X variables (0 to 1)
  • Multicollinearity: Problem when IVs are highly correlated (VIF > 10)
📖 Factor Analysis
  • Purpose: Reduce many variables to fewer underlying "factors"
  • Eigenvalue: Amount of variance explained by a factor (>1 is significant)
  • Factor Loading: Correlation between variable and factor (>0.5 is strong)
  • Example: 20 survey questions → 4 underlying factors (quality, price, service, convenience)
💡 Real-World Example

A retailer uses: Multiple Regression to predict sales from advertising, price, season, location. Factor Analysis to reduce 50 survey items to 5 key satisfaction dimensions. Cluster Analysis to segment customers into 4 groups.

🧠 Memory Aid

"R-D-F-C-M" — Regression, Discriminant, Factor, Cluster, MANOVA

⚠ Exam Tip

Multiple Regression is the most commonly tested multivariate technique. Know R², adjusted R², and how to interpret coefficients.

✅ Unit Summary

  • 5 techniques: Multiple Regression, Discriminant, Factor, Cluster, MANOVA
  • Regression: Y = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ — know R² interpretation
  • Factor Analysis: Eigenvalue >1 is significant, Factor Loading >0.5 is strong
  • Cluster Analysis: Hierarchical (tree) or K-means (partition into K clusters)

Block-04: Ethics & Report Writing

Unit 11: Ethics in Research

▶
Research Ethics
▶
Informed Consent in Research
📖 Core Ethical Principles
  • Honesty — Report data truthfully, no fabrication
  • Integrity — Consistency between words and actions
  • Objectivity — Avoid bias in design, analysis, interpretation
  • Informed Consent — Participants must know what they're agreeing to
  • Beneficence — Maximize benefits, minimize harm
  • Confidentiality — Protect participant identity and data
⚠ Research Misconduct Types (F-F-P)
  • Fabrication: Making up data that doesn't exist
  • Falsification: Manipulating research materials, equipment, or processes
  • Plagiarism: Using others' work without proper attribution
📖 Informed Consent Must Include
  • Purpose of the research
  • Procedures involved
  • Risks and benefits
  • Right to withdraw at any time
  • Confidentiality assurances
  • Contact information for questions
💡 Real-World Example

A pharmaceutical company conducting drug trials must: Get IRB approval, obtain informed consent from all participants, report all results (including negative ones), ensure participant safety, never fabricate or falsify efficacy data.

🧠 Memory Aid

"F-F-P" — Fabrication, Falsification, Plagiarism (research misconduct)

⚠ Exam Tip

Be able to name all three types of misconduct (F-F-P) and explain why informed consent is essential.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Core principles: Honesty, Integrity, Objectivity, Informed Consent, Beneficence, Confidentiality
  • Misconduct: Fabrication, Falsification, Plagiarism (F-F-P)
  • IRB reviews research proposals before data collection

Unit 13: Formats of Reports

Report Formatting

APA Referencing
📖 Three Parts of a Report
  • Front Matter: Title Page, Acknowledgements, Table of Contents, List of Tables/Figures, Abstract
  • Body: Introduction → Literature Review → Methodology → Findings → Discussion → Conclusions
  • Back Matter: References/Bibliography, Appendices
📖 Typing Guidelines
  • Margins: 1.5" left, 1" on other sides

  • APA: Author, A.A. (Year). Title. Publisher. (most common in social sciences)
  • MLA: Author. Title. Publisher, Year. (humanities)
  • Chicago: Footnotes/endnotes (history, some business)

Presentation Skills
▶
How to Present Research
📖 Presentation Skills Components
  • Organization — Logical flow, clear structure
  • Clarity — Simple language, avoid jargon
  • Confidence — Know your material, prepare thoroughly
  • Timing — Respect time limits, pace yourself
  • Engagement — Make it interesting, use examples
📖 Slide Design Rules
  • Maximum 6-7 lines per slide
  • Use bullet points, not paragraphs
  • Consistent font and color scheme
  • High contrast (dark text, light background)
  • One idea per slide
  • Avoid excessive animation
📖 Handling Questions
  • Listen carefully to the full question
  • Repeat the question for the audience
  • Be honest if you don't know
  • Bridge back to your key points
  • Keep answers concise
💡 Real-World Example

A 15-minute thesis defense: Slide 1: Title (30s) → Slides 2-3: Problem & objectives (2 min) → Slides 4-5: Literature gap (2 min) → Slides 6-7: Methodology (3 min) → Slides 8-12: Key findings (5 min) → Slides 13-14: Conclusions (2 min) → Q&A (5 min).
