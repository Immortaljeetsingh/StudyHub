Q1. Define Management Control Systems. Explain goal congruence with an example. (Unit 1, 10 Marks)

Answer: A Management Control System (MCS) is the process by which managers influence other members of the organization to implement the organization's strategies (Anthony & Govindarajan). It is the middle level in the hierarchy of control: strategic planning → management control → operational control. Merchant classifies controls into three types: action controls (rules, procedures, surveillance, e.g., SOPs in manufacturing), results controls (KPIs, bonuses, penalties, e.g., sales targets with commission) and personnel/cultural controls (values, trust, training, code of conduct). Goal congruence means individual goals align with organizational goals — it is the fundamental purpose of an MCS. A well-designed system ensures that managers acting in their own self-interest also serve the organization's interests. For example, if a sales manager's bonus depends only on revenue, he may offer excessive discounts, breaking goal congruence; linking the bonus to profit or margin restores alignment. Control systems must also prevent unethical behaviour through codes of conduct and whistleblower policies — Indian firms such as Infosys and TCS embed such ethics frameworks in their MCS. Closed-loop systems use feedback to self-correct, like a thermostat.

Key points:

  • A Management Control System (MCS) is the process by which managers influence other members of the organization to implement the organization's strategies (Anthony & Govindarajan).
  • For example, if a sales manager's bonus depends only on revenue, he may offer excessive discounts, breaking goal congruence; linking the bonus to profit or margin restores alignment.
  • It is the middle level in the hierarchy of control: strategic planning → management control → operational control.

Takeaway: Closed-loop systems use feedback to self-correct, like a thermostat.

Q2. Explain the four types of responsibility centres and the basis on which each is evaluated. (Unit 4, 10 Marks)

Answer: Responsibility accounting traces costs and revenues to the managers responsible for them; when authority is delegated downwards, responsibility centres are created. There are four types, increasing in scope (mnemonic CRPI). A cost centre controls costs only and is evaluated on budget versus actual cost, e.g., a production department. A revenue centre controls revenue only and is evaluated on sales targets, e.g., a regional sales office. A profit centre controls both costs and revenue and is evaluated on profit, e.g., a product division. An investment centre controls costs, revenue and assets and is evaluated on ROI, RI or EVA, e.g., a subsidiary company. The controllability principle states that a manager should be evaluated only on items within his control, which ensures fairness and prevents dysfunctional behaviour. In the Tata Group, plants operate as cost centres, sales offices as revenue centres, divisions as profit centres and subsidiaries as investment centres evaluated on ROCE. The choice of centre type should match the manager's authority: giving responsibility without authority leads to unfair evaluation and poor decisions.

Key points:

  • Responsibility accounting traces costs and revenues to the managers responsible for them; when authority is delegated downwards, responsibility centres are created.
  • In the Tata Group, plants operate as cost centres, sales offices as revenue centres, divisions as profit centres and subsidiaries as investment centres evaluated on ROCE.
  • A cost centre controls costs only and is evaluated on budget versus actual cost, e.g., a production department.

Takeaway: There are four types, increasing in scope (mnemonic CRPI).

Q3. Distinguish between ROI, RI and EVA. Division A has an ROI of 20% while the company's required rate is 12%, and a new project offers a 15% return. How does each measure treat this project? (Unit 7, 10 Marks)

Answer: ROI = Profit / Investment × 100. It is simple and widely used but can cause dysfunctional behaviour because managers reject projects that earn below their current ROI. RI = Profit − (Investment × Required Rate); it deducts a charge for the cost of capital, so it accepts any project earning above the required rate. EVA = NOPAT − (WACC × Capital); it measures true economic profit and is the most comprehensive metric. In the example: under ROI, Division A rejects the 15% project because 15% < its current ROI of 20% — this is dysfunctional, since the project earns above the 12% required rate. Under RI, the project adds positive residual income (15% > 12%) and is accepted. Under EVA, the project creates positive economic value whenever its return exceeds WACC, so it is accepted too. Hence RI and EVA are superior to ROI for goal congruence. Worked example from the notes: a division with ₹50 lakh profit on ₹200 lakh investment, required rate 12% and WACC 10%, has ROI 25%, RI ₹26 lakh and EVA ₹25 lakh.

Key points:

  • ROI = Profit / Investment × 100.
  • In the example: under ROI, Division A rejects the 15% project because 15% < its current ROI of 20% — this is dysfunctional, since the project earns above the 12% required rate.
  • It is simple and widely used but can cause dysfunctional behaviour because managers reject projects that earn below their current ROI.

Q4. Explain the Balanced Scorecard and its four perspectives, and show how they are linked in a cause-effect chain. (Unit 10, 10 Marks)

Answer: The Balanced Scorecard of Kaplan & Norton translates strategy into measurable objectives across four perspectives. Financial: how do shareholders see us — ROI, revenue growth, EVA (Infosys tracks EPS growth and ROE). Customer: how do customers see us — satisfaction, retention, market share (HDFC Bank tracks NPS and wallet share). Internal Process: what must we excel at — quality, cycle time, efficiency (Tata Steel tracks yield and energy per ton). Learning & Growth: can we continue to improve — innovation, training, IT (Wipro tracks training hours per employee and patents filed). The four perspectives are not separate; they form a cause-effect chain: Learning & Growth (training employees, upgrading IT) improves Internal Processes (faster, better quality), which improves Customer outcomes (satisfaction, retention), which finally drives Financial results (revenue and profit). Example: Infosys invested in digital skills training → improved project delivery time → higher client satisfaction → increased revenue per employee. The BSC therefore balances financial and non-financial measures and links them to strategy, unlike a single metric such as ROI. Memory aid: FCIL.

Key points:

  • The Balanced Scorecard of Kaplan & Norton translates strategy into measurable objectives across four perspectives.
  • Financial: how do shareholders see us — ROI, revenue growth, EVA (Infosys tracks EPS growth and ROE).
  • Customer: how do customers see us — satisfaction, retention, market share (HDFC Bank tracks NPS and wallet share).

Takeaway: The BSC therefore balances financial and non-financial measures and links them to strategy, unlike a single metric such as ROI.
