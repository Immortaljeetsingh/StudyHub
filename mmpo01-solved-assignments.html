Solved Assignment Questions & Answers - IGNOU MMPO-01

Q1. Explain the steps of the simplex method for solving a linear programming problem.

Answer: The simplex method is an iterative algebraic procedure that solves LP problems with any number of variables by moving from one basic feasible solution to a better adjacent one until optimality is reached. Step 1: convert the problem to standard form by adding slack variables for ≤ constraints, and surplus and artificial variables for ≥ constraints. Step 2: set up the initial tableau with an identity matrix as the starting basis. Step 3: identify the entering variable - the non-basic variable with the most negative Zj - Cj value in a maximization problem. Step 4: identify the leaving variable by the minimum ratio test - divide each RHS value by the corresponding coefficient of the entering column and choose the smallest positive ratio. Step 5: pivot (Gauss-Jordan elimination) so the entering variable becomes basic. Step 6: repeat until all Zj - Cj values are non-negative, which signals optimality. Big-M and the two-phase method handle artificial variables when constraints require them. The final tableau gives the optimal values of the decision variables and the objective function. A furniture manufacturer maximizing profit from tables and chairs under wood, labour and machine constraints is a typical application.

Key points:

  • The simplex method is an iterative algebraic procedure that solves LP problems with any number of variables by moving from one basic feasible solution to a better adjacent one until optimality is reached.
  • Step 1: convert the problem to standard form by adding slack variables for ≤ constraints, and surplus and artificial variables for ≥ constraints.
  • A furniture manufacturer maximizing profit from tables and chairs under wood, labour and machine constraints is a typical application.

Takeaway: A furniture manufacturer maximizing profit from tables and chairs under wood, labour and machine constraints is a typical application.

Q2. What is the difference between the VAM and MODI methods in the transportation problem?

Answer: The transportation problem seeks the minimum-cost shipping plan from m sources to n destinations, solved in two stages: obtaining an initial basic feasible solution, then testing and improving it for optimality. Vogel’s Approximation Method (VAM) builds the initial solution. For every row and column it computes the penalty - the difference between the two lowest costs - and allocates the maximum possible quantity to the lowest-cost cell in the row or column with the highest penalty. The satisfied row or column is crossed out and the process repeats. VAM typically produces a solution very close to the optimum. The MODI method (Modified Distribution) is the improvement stage. It assigns ui and vj values to the occupied cells of the current solution and computes opportunity costs cij - (ui + vj) for every unoccupied cell. If all opportunity costs are non-negative, the solution is optimal; otherwise the cell with the most negative value enters the basis and the solution is improved. If occupied cells are fewer than m + n - 1, the solution is degenerate and an epsilon allocation is added before applying MODI. Indian logistics firms use these methods to minimize freight costs across factory-warehouse networks.

Key points:

  • The transportation problem seeks the minimum-cost shipping plan from m sources to n destinations, solved in two stages: obtaining an initial basic feasible solution, then testing and improving it for optimality.
  • Indian logistics firms use these methods to minimize freight costs across factory-warehouse networks.
  • Vogel’s Approximation Method (VAM) builds the initial solution.

Takeaway: Indian logistics firms use these methods to minimize freight costs across factory-warehouse networks.

Q3. Explain the M/M/1 queuing model and its important formulas.

Answer: The M/M/1 model describes a single-server queuing system with Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, unlimited queue capacity and FCFS discipline. λ is the mean arrival rate and μ the mean service rate; the system is stable only when utilization ρ = λ/μ is less than 1. Key measures: average number of customers in the system L = λ/(μ - λ) and in the queue Lq = λ²/[μ(μ - λ)]; average time in the system W = 1/(μ - λ) and in the queue Wq = λ/[μ(μ - λ)]. The probability of n customers in the system is Pn = (1 - ρ) × ρn, and the idle probability is P0 = 1 - ρ. Little’s Law, L = λ × W and Lq = λ × Wq, holds for every queuing system, not just M/M/1. Example: a bank teller with 10 arrivals per hour and a service rate of 15 per hour gives ρ = 0.67, L = 2 customers and W = 12 minutes. Managers use these formulas to balance the cost of customer waiting against the cost of adding service capacity in banks, hospitals and call centres.

Key points:

  • The M/M/1 model describes a single-server queuing system with Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, unlimited queue capacity and FCFS discipline.

Q4. Differentiate between PERT and CPM.

Answer: PERT and CPM are network analysis techniques for planning, scheduling and controlling projects. PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique) is probabilistic: it uses three time estimates - optimistic (a), most likely (m) and pessimistic (b) - and computes expected time te = (a + 4m + b)/6 with variance σ² = [(b - a)/6]². It suits R&D, new product development and other projects with uncertain activity times, where the probability of completing by a target date is found using Z = (Ts - Te)/√(sum of variances on the critical path). CPM (Critical Path Method) is deterministic: it uses a single time estimate per activity and suits construction and well-understood projects. Both identify the critical path - the longest path through the network, which has zero float and determines the minimum project duration. Forward and backward passes compute ES, EF, LS and LF, and slack = LS - ES. PERT emphasizes time and probability; CPM emphasizes the time-cost trade-off and supports crashing. Networks may be drawn as AOA or AON, and float types include total, free and independent float.

Key points:

  • PERT and CPM are network analysis techniques for planning, scheduling and controlling projects.
  • PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique) is probabilistic: it uses three time estimates - optimistic (a), most likely (m) and pessimistic (b) - and computes expected time te = (a + 4m + b)/6 with variance σ² = [(b - a)/6]².
  • Networks may be drawn as AOA or AON, and float types include total, free and independent float.
