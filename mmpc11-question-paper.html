Q1. Briefly discuss social man theory and its impact on organisational behaviour.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the theory and the Hawthorne studies, 5 marks for its impact on OB.

Answer: The social man theory, born from Elton Mayo's Hawthorne studies (1924-1932), holds that workers are motivated by belonging, recognition and group norms as much as by pay - and it founded the human relations movement and today's people-first management.

The social man theory emerged from Elton Mayo's Hawthorne experiments (1924-1932). It says workers are motivated not only by money but by social needs - belonging, recognition and group membership. People at work behave as members of informal groups, and group norms often matter more than financial incentives.

Key points:

  • The Hawthorne studies showed that when lighting was changed, output rose in both the experimental and control groups - workers responded to being observed and cared about.
  • Interviews revealed that informal groups set their own norms about a "fair day's work", and individuals adjusted output to match the group.
  • This shifted management thinking from the economic man (Taylor) to the social man - the foundation of the Human Relations movement.

Impact on OB: it created the human relations school, led to participative supervision, open communication and team-building practices, and made organisations focus on employee morale, informal groups and social needs alongside pay. Indian organisations use this today - TCS's team outings, Infosys's family-like culture and Google India's open offices all treat employees as social beings whose relationships affect productivity.

Maslow's hierarchy of needs - belonging and esteem, the social man's core, sit mid-pyramid between survival and growth

Takeaway: Social man theory comes from the Hawthorne studies; workers value belonging and group norms, not just pay; it founded the human relations movement and today's team-based, people-first HR practices.

Q2. Identify and explain the factors influencing perception.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the three sets of factors with explanations, 5 marks for examples and perceptual errors.

Answer: Perception is shaped by three interacting sets of factors - the perceiver, the target and the situation - and is distorted by errors such as halo, stereotyping, projection and primacy/recency effects.

Perception is the process of organising and interpreting sensory information to give meaning to the environment. Three sets of factors shape how a person perceives:

Key points:

  • Factors in the perceiver: attitudes, motives, interests, past experience and expectations. A sales manager who expects staff to be lazy will interpret a late report as laziness.
  • Factors in the target: size, colour, movement, novelty, sound and similarity. A loud, aggressive presenter is noticed more than a quiet one; a familiar brand name is perceived more favourably.
  • Factors in the situation: time, workplace setting and social context. The same joke is funny at a party but offensive in a board meeting; a deadline changes how a request is perceived.

Perception is also distorted by errors: halo effect (one trait colours the whole impression - a good speaker seen as a competent manager), stereotyping ("young employees are lazy"), projection ("I'm stressed, so everyone is") and primacy/recency effects in interviews and appraisals. Attribution theory adds that we explain others' behaviour by consistency, distinctiveness and consensus - and the fundamental attribution error makes us blame internal factors for others' failures while excusing our own with the self-serving bias.

Takeaway: Perception = perceiver + target + situation; errors - halo, stereotyping, projection, primacy/recency; attribution explains how we judge causes of behaviour.

Q3. Briefly discuss types of stress and the factors contributing to the situation of burnout and rustout.

Marks guide: 10 marks for stress types and the burnout/rustout comparison, 5 marks for contributing factors.

Answer: Stress types are eustress, distress, hyperstress and hypostress - hyperstress builds toward burnout and hypostress toward rustout, both fed by role problems, workload, insecurity, conflict and poor work-life balance.

Stress is the body's response to demands. Its types are:

Key points:

  • Eustress - positive stress that motivates: a promotion, a challenging new project.
  • Distress - negative stress that harms health and performance: job insecurity, overload.
  • Hyperstress - over-stimulation from excessive demands, leading to burnout.
  • Hypostress - under-stimulation from boredom and monotony, leading to rustout.

Burnout is physical and emotional exhaustion from prolonged hyperstress - chronic workload, role conflict, impossible deadlines and lack of control. Rustout (ROSS - rust out stress syndrome) is the opposite: an unchallenging job, no growth, repetitive tasks and low stimulation, which makes employees feel stagnant and demotivated.

Common contributing factors: role ambiguity and role conflict, excessive workload, job insecurity, interpersonal conflict, poor work-life balance and lack of career development. The General Adaptation Syndrome explains the path - alarm, resistance, then exhaustion when resources are depleted. Google India counters burnout with meditation rooms and flexible hours; TCS offers employee assistance programmes with counselling.

Takeaway: Eustress motivates, distress harms; hyperstress → burnout, hypostress → rustout; sources - role ambiguity, workload, insecurity, conflict; GAS ends in exhaustion.

Q4. What is organisational citizenship behaviour? Discuss its implications in an organisation. Give examples.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the concept and dimensions, 5 marks for implications and examples.

Answer: OCB is voluntary, discretionary extra-role behaviour - altruism, conscientiousness, sportsmanship, courtesy and civic virtue - that measurably raises organisational effectiveness and grows from fair treatment, not coercion.

Organisational citizenship behaviour (OCB) is voluntary, discretionary behaviour that helps the organisation but is not part of the employee's formal job duties or reward system. It is the "extra mile" - work performed out of goodwill, not compulsion.

Key points:

  • Dimensions: altruism (helping colleagues), conscientiousness (punctuality, extra effort), sportsmanship (not complaining about small issues), courtesy (informing others in advance) and civic virtue (participating in meetings and organisation life).
  • Implications: research shows OCB raises organisational effectiveness by 18-38% - it reduces supervision needs, improves cooperation, helps new employees learn faster and keeps the workplace pleasant, which lowers turnover.

Examples: a senior accountant staying back to help a junior finish a GST return; a TCS engineer documenting code so teammates can maintain it; an Infosys employee mentoring a fresher during onboarding week without being asked; an HUL salesperson covering a colleague's route when the colleague is on leave.

Managers encourage OCB through fair treatment (organisational justice), supportive leadership and recognition, because citizenship behaviour appears when employees feel valued and committed - not when they are coerced.

Takeaway: OCB = voluntary extra-role behaviour; dimensions - altruism, conscientiousness, sportsmanship, courtesy, civic virtue; raises effectiveness 18-38%; grows from fair treatment, not force.

Q5. Identify and explain the problems affecting teamwork. Briefly discuss the strategies to make team work effectively.

Marks guide: 10 marks for team problems, 5 marks for effectiveness strategies.

Answer: Teams fail from social loafing, unclear goals and roles, poor communication, low trust and groupthink - and succeed through shared goals, complementary roles, trust, conflict management and disciplined progression through Tuckman's stages.

Teams fail when the problems below are ignored:

Key points:

  • Social loafing - individual effort falls in a group because members feel their contribution is invisible ("someone else will do it").
  • Unclear goals and roles - members do not know what the team must achieve or who does what; groups (individual goals) never become teams (shared goals).
  • Poor communication and conflict - information silos, personality clashes and unresolved disagreements block decisions.
  • Lack of trust and accountability - members blame each other instead of taking mutual responsibility.
  • Groupthink - the desire for harmony suppresses honest dissent, producing bad decisions.

Strategies for effective teamwork: set clear shared goals and complementary roles (Belbin's nine team roles - Plant, Coordinator, Shaper and others - build balanced teams); build trust through transparency; agree communication norms; manage conflict with the Thomas-Kilmann styles, using collaborating (win-win) for important issues; and move the team through Tuckman's stages - forming, storming, norming, performing - instead of skipping conflict. Wipro applies the Tuckman model in team development programmes, and the Indian cricket team is a classic high-performing team with complementary roles.

Tuckman's five stages - teams must pass through storming, not skip it, to reach performing

Takeaway: Team killers - social loafing, unclear roles, poor communication, no trust, groupthink; fixes - shared goals, Belbin roles, trust, conflict resolution, Tuckman stages; teams need mutual accountability, groups do not.

Q6. What are the characteristics of a learning organisation? Explain the approaches to incorporate learning in an organisation.

Marks guide: 10 marks for characteristics, 5 marks for learning approaches.

Answer: A learning organisation (Peter Senge) pairs systems thinking, personal mastery, mental models, shared vision and team learning - and embeds learning through training, knowledge management, mentoring, reviews and OD interventions.

A learning organisation (Peter Senge) continuously builds its capacity to create, acquire and transfer knowledge, and changes its behaviour to reflect new insights. Its characteristics are:

Key points:

  • Systems thinking - employees see the organisation as connected parts, not isolated functions.
  • Personal mastery - every member keeps developing skills and learning.
  • Mental models - people question old assumptions and are open to new ways of working.
  • Shared vision - a common picture of the future that motivates everyone.
  • Team learning - groups learn together through dialogue and reflection, moving from group to team.

Approaches to incorporate learning: training and development programmes, job rotation and cross-functional projects; knowledge management systems that capture and share expertise; mentoring and coaching (as at Infosys's Global Education Centre in Mysore); after-action reviews and feedback loops; and OD interventions such as surveys, workshops and change programmes (Lewin's unfreeze-change-refreeze) that build learning into daily work. TCS's knowledge base and communities of practice spread lessons across projects, so one team's mistake becomes everyone's teaching.

Takeaway: Learning organisation = systems thinking, personal mastery, mental models, shared vision, team learning; build it with training, knowledge management, mentoring, reviews and OD; learning must change behaviour, not just add information.

Q7. How do personality traits have an impact on organisational behaviour?

Marks guide: 10 marks for the trait models and their organisational impact, 5 marks for applications and examples.

Answer: Personality traits - the Big Five (OCEAN) and Type A/B - shape selection, performance, teamwork, leadership emergence and stress outcomes; conscientiousness is the strongest performance predictor.

Personality is the set of relatively stable psychological characteristics that makes each person unique. Traits shape how people perceive, decide, interact and perform at work.

Key points:

  • Big Five (OCEAN): Openness (curiosity, creativity), Conscientiousness (discipline, reliability), Extraversion (sociability, energy), Agreeableness (cooperation, warmth) and Neuroticism (anxiety, emotional instability). Conscientiousness is the strongest predictor of job performance across occupations; extraversion suits sales and leadership; openness drives innovation.
  • Type A/B: Type A people are competitive, impatient and prone to stress; Type B people are relaxed. Type A employees achieve more but face higher burnout risk.
  • Attitudes and values - unlike stable personality, attitudes are changeable and directly affect job satisfaction, commitment and engagement.

Organisational impact: personality guides recruitment (Infosys uses Big Five assessments for cultural fit), team composition (balanced traits reduce conflict), leadership style, and stress and career management. Tata Group famously hires for attitude - skills can be taught, attitude cannot. Perceptual errors like the halo effect, however, distort how traits are judged, so organisations rely on structured interviews and assessments rather than first impressions.

Takeaway: Big Five = OCEAN; conscientiousness predicts performance best; Type A drives achievement but raises stress; personality is stable, attitudes change; use assessments for fit, avoid halo-effect judgments.

Q8. Describe different models of organisational behaviour and their relevance.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the four models with their focus and result, 5 marks for relevance.

Answer: The four classic OB models - autocratic, custodial, supportive and collegial - each produce different employee responses; modern knowledge organisations lean toward supportive and collegial styles.

Models of OB describe how organisations manage people and what employees give in return. The four classic models (Keith Davis) are:

Key points:

  • Autocratic model: management holds all power and directs with authority; employees obey. Result: dependence on the boss. Relevance: still seen in traditional manufacturing and small firms, but breeds low commitment.
  • Custodial model: management provides economic security - salary, benefits, job protection - and employees respond with dependence on the organisation. Result: passive compliance, not passion.
  • Supportive model: leadership focuses on supporting employees' performance, not control; managers help people succeed. Result: participation and recognition of contributions - the basis of modern Indian IT companies like Infosys.
  • Collegial model: employees work as partners with mutual respect, aiming at the same goal like a cricket team. Result: self-discipline and a team culture - the model high-performing organisations such as Google India and Tata Group strive for.

Relevance: no single model is best - mature, skilled workforces respond to collegial styles, while emergency or routine operations may need directive ones. Understanding OB at individual, group and organisational levels (the three levels of analysis) lets managers choose the right model for their context and culture, matching leadership style to the situation.

Takeaway: Four models - autocratic (obedience), custodial (security), supportive (performance), collegial (partnership); modern knowledge organisations move toward supportive and collegial; match the model to workforce maturity and culture.

December 2022 7 questions · answer any five

Q1Explain which social processes act as foundations of Human Behaviour in organizations. Briefly discuss how individuals who work in the organisation operate at different levels of society i.e. from micro to macro.

Answer: Human behaviour in organisations is built on social processes - socialisation, roles, status, norms, culture and communication - and every employee simultaneously operates at micro, meso and macro levels of society.

Key points:

  • Socialisation: individuals learn the organisation's values, language and expected behaviour, just as they learned society's norms at home and school.
  • Roles and role sets: each position carries expected behaviours (role), defined by others' expectations (role set); conflicting expectations create role conflict.
  • Status: formal status (designation, authority) and informal status (expertise, seniority) shape influence and interaction patterns.
  • Norms: unwritten group standards about effort, dress and communication regulate daily behaviour more than formal rules do.
  • Micro to macro levels: micro (individual perceptions, attitudes, motivation) → meso (group, department, organisation) → macro (industry, economy, society) - the same person is citizen, employee, team member and professional at once.
  • Culture and communication: shared assumptions and communication channels carry social processes from society into the organisation.

Takeaway: Behaviour = socialisation + roles + norms + status + culture, played out simultaneously at micro, meso and macro levels.

Q2What do you understand by the term "Behaviour"? Explain. Describe models to understand Human Behaviour and their limitations. Give examples.

Answer: Behaviour is the observable activity of individuals in response to inner needs and outer stimuli; the cognitive, behaviourist, psychoanalytic, trait and humanistic models each explain part of it - and each has clear limitations.

Key points:

  • Meaning: behaviour includes overt acts (talking, working) and covert states (thinking, feeling); it is caused, not random - a response to needs, stimuli and learning.
  • Cognitive model: behaviour follows perception, beliefs and information processing; example: a worker stays because he believes the job pays fairly. Limitation: understates habit and emotion.
  • Behaviourist (S-R) model: behaviour is learned responses to stimuli and reinforcement; example: bonus-linked performance. Limitation: treats the person as a black box, ignores meaning.
  • Psychoanalytic model: unconscious motives and defence mechanisms drive behaviour. Limitation: hard to test, little direct workplace application.
  • Trait model: the Big Five describe stable dispositions predicting patterns of behaviour. Limitation: describes, does not explain causes; situation matters.
  • Humanistic model: people seek growth and self-actualisation (Maslow, Rogers). Limitation: idealistic, difficult to operationalise.

Takeaway: No single model explains behaviour - combine the lenses and know each model's blind spot.

Q3Describe Behaviour modification. Explain its process. How does behaviour modification work in organisations?

Answer: Behaviour modification (OB Mod) systematically changes work behaviour by applying reinforcement through the ABC sequence - antecedents, behaviour and consequences - following Lewin's unfreeze-change-refreeze.

Lewin's change model - behaviour modification maps onto unfreeze → change → refreeze

Key points:

  • Step 1 - identify: define the target behaviour to change, e.g., late attendance, low sales calls, unsafe shortcuts.
  • Step 2 - measure: record the baseline frequency and set a realistic improvement goal.
  • Step 3 - analyse: functional analysis of the antecedents and consequences that maintain the behaviour (ABC).
  • Step 4 - intervene: positively reinforce desired behaviour (bonus, praise, points); extinguish undesired behaviour; use punishment sparingly and fairly.
  • Step 5 - evaluate and maintain: monitor results and lock in the new behaviour with reinforcement schedules (fixed/variable, ratio/interval).
  • In organisations: attendance incentives, sales commission targets, safety compliance charts, quality-circles rewards - e.g., retail chains rewarding on-time store opening.

Takeaway: OB Mod = identify, measure, reinforce the right behaviour - change works when the new behaviour is rewarded until it becomes habit.

Q4Briefly describe and discuss different sources of conflict and their effects on organisations.

Answer: Conflict springs from scarce resources, task interdependence, goal incompatibility, structural differentiation, ambiguity and personal differences - and its effects can be constructive or destructive.

Key points:

  • Resource scarcity: competition for budgets, promotions, machines and time.
  • Task interdependence: sequential or reciprocal tasks force coordination where friction is likely.
  • Goal incompatibility: sales vs production, cost vs quality - departments pull in different directions.
  • Communication and role ambiguity: unclear boundaries, information gaps and jargon breed misunderstanding.
  • Personal and value differences: age, culture, personality and perception clashes.
  • Effects - negative: stress, office politics, wasted time, low morale, high turnover. Effects - positive: surfaces hidden problems, sharpens decisions, sparks innovation, breaks complacency.

Takeaway: Conflict is neutral - its outcome depends on whether it is suppressed or managed constructively.

Q5Write short notes on any four of the following: (a) Dimensions of OCB  (b) Attribution  (c) Stages of Group Development  (d) Negotiation  (e) Empowerment

Answer: All five short notes are answered below - each in point form.

Key points:

  • (a) Dimensions of OCB: altruism (voluntary help to colleagues), conscientiousness (punctuality, extra effort beyond minimum), sportsmanship (tolerating minor frustrations without complaint), courtesy (informing others before actions that affect them) and civic virtue (constructive participation in organisational life - meetings, issues, committees).
  • (b) Attribution: how we assign causes - internal (ability, effort) or external (luck, task) - to behaviour, judged on consistency, distinctiveness and consensus; the fundamental attribution error blames others' failures on character while the self-serving bias excuses our own with circumstances; example: a missed deadline read as laziness though the system failed.
  • (c) Stages of Group Development: Tuckman's five stages - forming (orientation, politeness), storming (conflict over roles and power), norming (cohesion, agreed rules), performing (high productivity and synergy) and adjourning (disbanding after the task ends); storming is normal, not failure.
  • (d) Negotiation: a give-and-take decision process between interdependent parties; distributive bargaining (fixed pie, positions, win-lose) vs integrative bargaining (expanding the pie, interests, win-win); BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement) and ZOPA (zone of possible agreement) guide strategy - union-management wage talks in India mix both.
  • (e) Empowerment: giving employees authority, information, resources and accountability to act; structural (delegation, flat structures, information sharing) and psychological (meaning, competence, self-determination, impact); it raises speed and motivation but needs training and clear limits.

Takeaway: OCB = the unasked extra mile; attribution explains judgment errors; groups develop in stages; negotiation needs BATNA and interests; empowerment = authority + capability + accountability.

Q6Evaluate the concept and dynamics of group formation. Cite suitable examples.

Answer: A group is two or more interdependent people interacting for shared goals; formation is driven by propinquity, similarity, balance and exchange, and its dynamics are roles, norms, status, cohesion and development stages.

Group dynamics model - inputs → processes → outcomes

Key points:

  • Propinquity: physical closeness creates interaction and attraction - same floor, same shift, same training batch.
  • Similarity and attraction: people bond over common values, backgrounds, interests and attitudes.
  • Balance (equilibrium) theory: people seek consistency among their attitudes, affiliations and behaviour.
  • Exchange theory: membership continues while rewards (satisfaction, status, safety) exceed costs.
  • Dynamics after formation: roles and role expectations, status hierarchies, group norms, cohesion, and progress through Tuckman's forming-storming-norming-performing stages.
  • Examples: an Infosys project team formed for a client brief; a bank's loan-recovery committee; a neighbourhood WhatsApp group.

Takeaway: Groups form by closeness, similarity, balance and exchange - and their dynamics are roles, norms, status and cohesion.

Q7Discuss the salient features of employee empowerment. Cite the process of measuring empowerment.

Answer: Empowerment's salient features are authority transfer, information sharing, capability building, autonomy and accountability - and it is measured through employees' experienced meaning, competence, self-determination and impact.

Key points:

  • Features: real decision authority over one's work; access to information and resources; skill development; clarity of limits; accountability matched to authority.
  • Psychological measurement (Spreitzer's scale): meaning (work matters), competence (confidence in abilities), self-determination (freedom to choose how to work) and impact (visible influence on outcomes).
  • Behavioural measurement: number of decisions taken at lower levels, response speed to customers, engagement and initiative scores.
  • Organisational measurement: delegation norms, flat structure indices, suggestion-scheme participation.
  • Indian examples: HCL's "Employee First" philosophy, Tata group's suggestion schemes, frontline bank staff authorised to approve small loans.

Takeaway: Empowerment is measured by felt power and visible decisions, not by delegated titles.

June 2023 7 questions · answer any five

Q1Briefly describe and discuss the determinants and characteristics of motivation. How can organisations motivate employees to be more productive? Illustrate with examples.

Answer: Motivation is the force that energises, directs and sustains behaviour - determined by individual, job and organisational factors - and organisations raise it by meeting needs, designing meaningful jobs and rewarding performance.

Maslow's hierarchy of needs - the starting point of motivation

Key points:

  • Characteristics: intensity (level of effort), direction (effort toward goals) and persistence (sustained effort over time).
  • Individual determinants: needs and drives (Maslow), values, personality, self-efficacy, expectancy and equity perceptions.
  • Job determinants: challenge, variety, autonomy and feedback - the core of job design.
  • Organisational determinants: reward system, recognition, culture, leadership style and career growth paths.
  • Ways to motivate: fair pay and benefits, recognition programmes, job enrichment and rotation, growth paths, flexible work and participation in goal setting.
  • Examples: performance-linked incentives in Indian IT and BFSI, recognition awards at Infosys and TCS, Google India's perks and open culture.

Takeaway: Motivate by matching needs, designing jobs and rewarding fairly - Maslow's pyramid shows the needs ladder to climb.

Q2Identify and elaborate the determinants of personality? How do traits of personality produce an impact on organisational behaviour? Give examples.

Answer: Personality is shaped by heredity, environment and situation; Big Five traits measurably affect selection fit, performance, teamwork, leadership and stress outcomes.

Key points:

  • Heredity: inborn temperament and physical traits; twin studies show strong heritability of personality.
  • Environment: family upbringing, schooling, culture and life experiences shape values and habits.
  • Situation: context moderates which traits surface - the same person is assertive with juniors and deferential with seniors.
  • Trait impact: conscientiousness best predicts job performance; extraversion suits sales and leadership; openness drives creativity; agreeableness builds cooperation; neuroticism raises stress and turnover risk.
  • Applications: assessment-based selection (Infosys uses Big Five for cultural fit), balanced team composition, coaching and career planning.
  • Examples: a high-conscientiousness, high-extraversion candidate chosen for a client-facing team lead role.

Takeaway: Personality = heredity + environment + situation; use traits for fit and development, not for quick judgement.

Q3Explain in detail the aspects to be considered for building effective teams. Cite suitable examples.

Answer: Effective teams are built on clear shared goals, complementary skills and roles, trust, mutual accountability and coaching leadership.

Key points:

  • Shared vision and measurable goals: teams exist for outcomes, not activity; goals must be owned by members, not imposed.
  • Right skill mix: technical, functional and interpersonal skills, balanced through Belbin's nine team roles.
  • Clear roles and interdependence: everyone knows who does what and how work links together.
  • Trust and psychological safety: members speak up, admit errors and challenge ideas without fear.
  • Agreed norms and processes: explicit rules for communication, decisions and conflict.
  • Rewards for team performance: not only individual incentives, plus leadership that coaches rather than commands.
  • Examples: the 2011 World Cup-winning Indian cricket team, Wipro's project teams, Tata's cross-functional plant improvement teams.

Takeaway: Effective teams = purpose + complementary roles + trust + accountability + coaching leadership.

Q4Discuss the strategies of conflict management as well as resolution.

Answer: Conflict strategies span the Thomas-Kilmann grid - competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding and accommodating - chosen by assertiveness and cooperativeness; resolution uses negotiation, mediation and arbitration.

Thomas-Kilmann grid - five conflict styles from assertiveness x cooperativeness

Key points:

  • Competing: assert own position - for crises, rights violations, quick decisions.
  • Collaborating: joint problem-solving for win-win on important issues.
  • Compromising: split the difference when time is short and stakes moderate.
  • Avoiding: withdraw when the issue is trivial or emotions run high.
  • Accommodating: yield to preserve the relationship.
  • Resolution mechanisms: informal dialogue, negotiation (interests, BATNA), mediation by a neutral third party, arbitration with a binding decision, and superordinate goals that force cooperation.
  • Indian examples: trade-union wage negotiations, industrial tribunals, family-business dispute mediation, grievance committees.

Takeaway: Match the style to the situation - and escalate from dialogue to mediation to arbitration when needed.

Q5Write short notes on any four of the following: (a) Strategies for bargaining  (b) Rust out stress syndrome  (c) Dimensions of OCB  (d) Strategies to break the glass ceiling effect  (e) Emotional intelligence

Answer: All five short notes are answered below in point form.

Key points:

  • (a) Strategies for bargaining: distributive bargaining (fixed pie - anchors, bluffing, deadlines, win-lose) vs integrative bargaining (expanding the pie - information sharing, trade-offs, packaging, win-win); interest-based bargaining (separate people from the problem); guard the BATNA and know the ZOPA; concede slowly and link every concession to reciprocation - union-management wage talks in Indian industry mix both approaches.
  • (b) Rust Out Stress Syndrome (ROSS): the stress of chronic under-stimulation - boredom, monotony and no growth; opposite of burnout; causes: repetitive tasks, unchallenging work, no promotion paths, low autonomy; symptoms: apathy, absenteeism, disengagement; remedies: job enrichment, rotation, stretch assignments, career conversations and skill development.
  • (c) Dimensions of OCB: altruism, conscientiousness, sportsmanship, courtesy and civic virtue - voluntary extra-role behaviour that raises organisational effectiveness by 18-38%.
  • (d) Breaking the glass ceiling: the invisible barrier to women's top leadership; strategies - mentoring and sponsorship, transparent criteria-based promotions, flexible work and returnship programmes, leadership training and stretch assignments, diversity targets (Companies Act 2013 board norms, SEBI diversity rules) and culture change with zero tolerance for bias; examples - women leaders at HDFC Bank, Biocon and Infosys.
  • (e) Emotional intelligence: Goleman's five components - self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills; EI predicts leadership success beyond IQ; example - a branch manager who calmly defuses an angry customer and steadies the team.

Takeaway: Bargain on interests with a strong BATNA; enrich jobs to beat ROSS; break ceilings with sponsorship; lead with EI.

Q6What is your understanding of organizational behaviour? Identify the components which affect it.

Answer: Organisational behaviour is the study of what people think, feel and do in organisations - shaped by people, structure, technology and environment at individual, group and organisational levels.

Key points:

  • Definition: OB applies the understanding of individuals, groups and structures to make organisations work effectively.
  • People component: individual differences, attitudes, needs, perception and motivation.
  • Structure component: roles, authority, reporting lines and formal systems.
  • Technology component: tools, processes and work methods that enable or constrain behaviour.
  • Environment component: external forces - economic, social, legal and cultural - that press on the organisation.
  • Three levels of analysis: individual (perception, personality, motivation), group (dynamics, leadership, conflict) and organisation (culture, structure, change) - the OB model runs inputs → processes → outcomes.

Takeaway: OB = people + structure + technology + environment, studied at three interlocking levels.

Q7Discuss the methods for resolving organizational conflict. Explain using Indian examples.

Answer: Organisational conflict is resolved by problem-solving dialogue, negotiation, mediation, arbitration, structural change and superordinate goals - Indian practice leans heavily on mediation and third-party institutions.

Key points:

  • Confrontation / problem-solving: parties talk through interests and generate win-win options.
  • Negotiation: give-and-take between parties with interdependent goals.
  • Mediation: a neutral facilitator helps parties settle - common in Indian family-business disputes and panchayat-level resolution.
  • Arbitration and tribunals: binding third-party decisions under industrial law.
  • Structural remedies: clearer roles, changed reporting lines, rotated personnel.
  • Superordinate goals: shared targets that force departments to cooperate.
  • Indian examples: bank unions negotiating wage revisions with the Indian Banks' Association, Tata group's historic harmonious industrial relations, the Reliance family settlement mediation (2005), statutory grievance machinery under the Industrial Disputes Act.

Takeaway: Resolve by escalating dialogue → mediation → arbitration; prevent with structure and shared goals.

Q1Briefly discuss different models to understand organisational behaviour.

Answer: OB is understood through the four classic management models - autocratic, custodial, supportive and collegial - plus cognitive and behaviourist lenses, each fitting different contexts.

Key points:

  • Autocratic: authority and obedience; employees depend on the boss; suits routine, urgent operations.
  • Custodial: economic security - salary, benefits, job protection; employees depend on the organisation; breeds passive compliance.
  • Supportive: leadership supports performance; employees participate and contribute; the basis of modern IT culture.
  • Collegial: partnership and mutual respect; employees show self-discipline like a team.
  • Cognitive and behaviourist lenses: behaviour follows perception and interpretation, or reinforcement and consequences.
  • Relevance: PSUs historically custodial, Infosys supportive, Google India collegial - match the model to workforce maturity and culture.

Takeaway: No single model is best - choose by workforce maturity, task and culture.

Q2Describe and discuss the impact of personality traits on organisational behaviour. Give examples.

Answer: Personality traits shape selection, performance, teamwork, leadership and stress through the Big Five (OCEAN) framework, with conscientiousness the strongest performance predictor.

Key points:

  • OCEAN: openness (innovation), conscientiousness (reliability), extraversion (sales and leadership), agreeableness (cooperation), neuroticism (stress, turnover).
  • Traits predict patterns, not single acts: a conscientious person is usually punctual and thorough across tasks.
  • Situation moderates traits: strong situations (tight rules) suppress trait expression; weak situations let traits show.
  • Applications: structured assessments for recruitment fit, balanced team composition, career counselling.
  • Examples: an extroverted, high-conscientiousness candidate selected for client-facing team lead; a high-neuroticism employee offered stress coaching.

Takeaway: Traits shape behaviour patterns - use them for fit, teams and development.

Q3Describe process of behaviour modification and how it can be applied in organisational context?

Answer: Behaviour modification follows identify → measure → analyse → reinforce → maintain, applying consequences to shape workplace behaviour.

Key points:

  • Identify and define the target behaviour objectively - "attendance below 90%" rather than "careless attitude".
  • Measure baseline frequency and set a realistic goal.
  • Analyse antecedents and consequences (ABC - functional analysis) that keep the behaviour alive.
  • Intervene: positive reinforcement (bonus, praise, points) and negative reinforcement (remove a disliked condition) to increase behaviour; extinction (withdraw the reward) and punishment to decrease it.
  • Maintain with reinforcement schedules - variable-ratio schedules resist extinction best.
  • Organisational applications: attendance incentives, sales commission schemes, safety compliance rewards, punctuality programmes.

Takeaway: OB Mod = measure the behaviour, reinforce the desired, maintain with schedules.

Q4Briefly discuss sources of conflict and its effect on organisations. Give examples.

Answer: Conflict arises from scarce resources, task interdependence, goal incompatibility, ambiguity and personal differences - with both destructive and constructive effects.

Key points:

  • Sources: resource competition (budgets, promotions), task interdependence, incompatible goals (sales vs production), role and communication ambiguity, values and personality clashes.
  • Destructive effects: stress, politics, wasted time, low morale, turnover, coordination failure.
  • Constructive effects: surfaces problems, improves decisions, sparks innovation, breaks complacency.
  • Examples: a production-sales clash over delivery promises in a consumer goods firm; two managers competing for the same promotion; functional conflict in a design review that improves the product.
  • Management implication: suppress destructive conflict, stimulate constructive debate.

Takeaway: Conflict cuts both ways - the skill is managing it, not eliminating it.

Key points:

  • (a) ROSS: rust out stress syndrome - chronic under-stimulation from boredom, monotony and no growth; the opposite of burnout; symptoms: apathy, absenteeism, disengagement; remedies: job enrichment, rotation, stretch assignments, L&D and career conversations.
  • (b) Gender diversity: balanced representation and inclusion of women and men at all levels; benefits - varied perspectives, better decisions, larger talent pool; challenges - unconscious bias, glass ceiling, pay gaps, work-family conflict; interventions - inclusive recruitment, flexible work, POSH Act compliance, leadership pipelines and sensitisation; Indian context - women's labour force participation rising, board diversity norms.

Answer:

Case (condensed): Arun, a star salesperson at ZAPTO stores, is promoted to Assistant Marketing Manager with 8 sales representatives reporting to him. He unilaterally sets very ambitious targets, announces them firmly, and never invites discussion. The team feels the targets are too high but stays silent. Q1Briefly discuss social man theory and its impact on organizational behaviour.

Answer: Social man theory (Hawthorne studies, Elton Mayo) says workers are motivated by belonging, recognition and group norms as much as pay.

Key points:

  • Workers respond to being observed and cared about (Hawthorne effect).

Q2Identify and explain the factors influencing perception.

Answer: Perception is shaped by factors in the perceiver, the target and the situation, and distorted by halo, stereotyping, projection and primacy/recency errors.

Key points:

  • Perceiver: attitudes, motives, experience, expectations.
  • Target: size, movement, novelty, sound, similarity.
  • Situation: time, setting, social context.

Q1Explain how social processes contribute in shaping human behaviour.

Answer: Social processes - socialisation, roles, status, norms, culture and communication - shape behaviour inside organisations exactly as they do in society.

Key points:

  • Socialisation teaches organisational values, language and expected behaviour.
  • Roles and role sets define expected behaviour; status orders influence and interaction.
  • Norms - the unwritten rules of effort and conduct - regulate daily behaviour.
  • Culture transmits assumptions and values; communication channels carry all of them.
  • Individuals operate at micro (self), meso (group, organisation) and macro (society) levels simultaneously.

Takeaway: People bring society's processes to work - behaviour is social before it is individual.

Q2Briefly describe and discuss the classical movement and its contribution to organizational behaviour.

Answer: The classical movement - Taylor's scientific management, Fayol's administrative theory and Weber's bureaucracy - built management's foundation of efficiency, structure and control, which OB later humanised.

Key points:

  • Taylor: time-and-motion study, differential piece rates, scientific selection - efficiency first.
  • Fayol: universal functions and 14 principles - management as a teachable discipline.
  • Weber: rational bureaucracy - rules, hierarchy, impersonality.
  • Contribution: management separated from ownership, systematic work design, formal structure, coordination.
  • Limitation: ignored the human and social side - the Hawthorne studies exposed it and gave birth to the Human Relations school and modern OB.

Takeaway: Classical movement = efficiency and structure; OB = its correction with the human dimension.

Q3What determines personality? Explain how attitudes and values impact organizational behaviour.

Answer: Personality is determined by heredity, environment and situation; attitudes and values - the more changeable parts of the person - directly affect satisfaction, commitment and ethical behaviour.

Key points:

  • Determinants: heredity (temperament), environment (family, culture, experience), situation (context moderates expression).
  • Attitudes: cognition + affect + behaviour toward an object; job satisfaction, involvement and organisational commitment are the workplace attitudes.
  • Values: terminal vs instrumental (Rokeach); personal-organisational value fit drives culture and ethical climate.
  • Impact: satisfied, value-aligned employees stay, perform and engage; misalignment raises turnover and deviance.
  • Example: an employee who values work-life balance thrives at flexible HUL roles but burns out in a 24/7 startup.

Takeaway: Personality is largely given; values and attitudes are chosen - hire for values fit.

Q4Briefly discuss the role of reinforcement in organizations. Cite examples.

Answer: Reinforcement uses consequences to increase desired and decrease undesired behaviour - the engine of performance management.

Key points:

  • Positive reinforcement: reward after desired behaviour - bonuses, praise, spot awards.
  • Negative reinforcement: remove an unpleasant condition after compliance - no overtime once target met.
  • Punishment: unpleasant consequence for undesired behaviour - penalties, warnings; use sparingly and fairly.
  • Extinction: withdraw the reward that maintained undesired behaviour.
  • Schedules: continuous for fast learning; variable-ratio partial schedules resist extinction longest.
  • Examples: TCS spot awards, retail sales commissions, attendance-linked perks, safety compliance programmes.

Takeaway: Behaviour follows consequences - reward what you want repeated.

Q5Identify and explain the elements in the process of perception. Discuss the perceptual errors and how can they be minimized.

Answer: Perception runs stimulus → attention → organisation → interpretation → response, and its errors - halo, stereotyping, projection, primacy/recency, contrast - can be minimised by structured, self-aware practices.

Key points:

  • Elements: exposure/attention (selective), organisation (grouping, figure-ground), interpretation (attribution of meaning), response (action based on the interpretation).
  • Errors: halo (one trait colours all), stereotyping (group-based judgement), projection (attributing own feelings to others), primacy/recency (first or last impressions dominate), contrast effect (judging against neighbours).
  • Minimising: self-awareness and feedback; structured interviews and appraisals with objective criteria; diversity and bias training; suspending judgement; multiple raters (360-degree feedback).
  • Example: panel interviews with scorecards reduce the halo of a good speaker.

Takeaway: Perception is constructive, not photographic - build checks into judgments.

Q6Explain different stages of group development and discuss the determinants of group productivity.

Answer: Groups develop through Tuckman's forming-storming-norming-performing-adjourning; productivity depends on task clarity, member capability, cohesion, leadership, resources and motivation.

Tuckman's five stages of group development

Key points:

  • Stages: forming (orientation), storming (conflict over roles and power), norming (cohesion and agreed norms), performing (high output and synergy), adjourning (task ends, group disbands).
  • Determinants of group productivity: task clarity and goal specificity; member skills and role fit; cohesion and performance norms; quality of leadership; adequate resources and technology; motivation and reward structure; external support.
  • Application: new product teams, project teams and committees pass through the same stages - e.g., a Maruti product team moves from polite forming to storming over design choices before performing.

Takeaway: Manage the stage, not the symptom - productivity = clarity + capability + cohesion.

Q7Briefly discuss how to resolve the conflicts existing in the organization. Cite examples.

Answer: Conflicts are resolved through dialogue and problem-solving, negotiation, mediation, arbitration, structural changes and superordinate goals - with Indian practice favouring mediation and third-party institutions.

Key points:

  • Problem-solving/confrontation: parties discuss interests openly and search for win-win options.
  • Negotiation: give-and-take settlement between interdependent parties (positions vs interests, BATNA).
  • Mediation: neutral facilitator helps settlement - panchayat-style and family-business mediation.
  • Arbitration and tribunals: binding decisions by third parties (industrial tribunals).
  • Structural remedies: clarify roles, change reporting, rotate people; superordinate goals force cooperation.
  • Examples: bank unions negotiating with the Indian Banks' Association, Tata group's harmonious industrial relations, statutory grievance machinery.

Takeaway: Escalate from dialogue to mediation to arbitration - and prevent with structure and shared goals.

Q8Write an essay on the pros and cons of empowerment in organizations.

Answer: Empowerment - giving employees authority, information, resources and accountability - brings motivation, speed and innovation, but risks misuse, inconsistency and ambiguity when poorly designed.

Key points:

  • Pros: faster decisions at the front line (bank cashiers, shop-floor teams); higher motivation and ownership; innovation and learning; better customer service; retention of talent.
  • Cons: unprepared employees misuse authority; inconsistent decisions across teams; ambiguous accountability; training and restructuring costs; threatened middle managers.
  • Balance: train before delegating, define limits clearly, align rewards with risk, review regularly.
  • Examples: Tata group's decentralised responsibility philosophy; Indian banks empowering frontline staff for small loans within defined limits; a startup's open-budget policy failing without finance training.

Takeaway: Empowerment fails without training, limits and aligned rewards - design it or skip it.

Q1What was classical movement of management? Discuss its role in the evolution of organizational behaviour.

Answer: The classical movement (Taylor, Fayol, Weber) built management on efficiency, structure and control, and its blind spot - the human factor - triggered the research that created organisational behaviour.

Key points:

  • Taylor's scientific management: time-and-motion, differential piece rates, scientific selection.
  • Fayol's administrative theory: universal functions and 14 principles.
  • Weber's bureaucracy: rational rules, hierarchy, impersonality.
  • Role in OB's evolution: classical efficiency ignored workers' social needs; the Hawthorne studies exposed the human factor and birthed the Human Relations school; OB then integrated both - structure plus people.

Takeaway: OB is classical efficiency plus the human dimension it left out.

Q2Explain the systems view of OB and describe the various OB modules.

Answer: The systems view treats the organisation as an open system - inputs → transformation → outputs → feedback - and OB is organised into individual, group and organisational modules.

Key points:

  • Open system: exchanges with the environment - customers, suppliers, regulators, society.
  • Features: synergy (the whole exceeds the parts), interdependence, feedback loops, entropy (tendency to decay) and equifinality (many paths to the same outcome).
  • Micro module: individual behaviour - perception, personality, motivation, learning, attitudes.
  • Meso module: group behaviour - group dynamics, teams, conflict, leadership, power, communication.
  • Macro module: organisational behaviour - culture, structure, change, HR systems.
  • Integration: individual inputs flow through group processes to organisational outcomes - Infosys converts talent and knowledge into services with client feedback loops.

Takeaway: See the organisation as a whole - OB works at three interlocking levels with feedback.

Q3Explain the concept and process of behaviour modification with suitable examples.

Answer: Behaviour modification applies reinforcement principles through identify → measure → analyse → intervene → maintain, shaping observable work behaviour with consequences.

Key points:

  • Concept: behaviour is a function of its consequences (ABC - antecedents, behaviour, consequences).
  • Process: define target behaviour; measure baseline; functional analysis; apply positive/negative reinforcement, extinction or punishment; evaluate; maintain with schedules.
  • Examples: punctuality bonuses reducing late arrival; sales commissions increasing call volumes; safety compliance rewards reducing accidents; ignoring (extinction) chronic complaint-seeking behaviour.

Takeaway: OB Mod = measure behaviour, reinforce the desired, maintain with schedules.

Q4Explain any two theories of motivation and their relevance in Indian organisations.

Answer: Maslow's need hierarchy and Herzberg's two-factor theory explain Indian motivation: pay and security are hygiene, while recognition and growth are the true motivators.

Herzberg's two-factor theory - hygiene vs motivators

Key points:

  • Maslow: five need levels - physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation; a satisfied need stops motivating; Indian relevance - job security and pay dominate when unemployment is high, then belonging and esteem take over.
  • Herzberg: hygiene factors (pay, security, conditions, policy, supervision) prevent dissatisfaction but never motivate; motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth, the work itself) create satisfaction.
  • Indian relevance: government jobs and PSU perks appeal to hygiene; IT firms motivate with recognition, growth tracks and challenging projects; performance-linked pay is a motivator only when it signals achievement, not mere entitlement.
  • Lesson: fix hygiene first, then build motivators - money prevents unhappiness, recognition creates engagement.

Takeaway: Maslow orders the needs; Herzberg separates the dissatisfiers from the motivators - manage both.

Q5What do you understand by perception? Describe the factors influencing perception.

Answer: Perception is the process of organising and interpreting sensory input to give meaning; it is influenced by the perceiver, the target and the situation.

Key points:

  • Perceiver: attitudes, motives, interests, past experience, expectations.
  • Target: size, colour, movement, novelty, sound, similarity - loud and novel stimuli attract notice.
  • Situation: time, place, social context - the same act is read differently in a party and a boardroom.
  • Perception is selective and constructive, not a camera - errors (halo, stereotyping, projection, primacy/recency) distort it.
  • Example: an auditor reads a late report as laziness; a flexible manager reads it as overload.

Takeaway: Two people see the same event differently - manage the perceiver, target and situation.

Q6What are the sources of conflict? Explain any two frameworks of conflict management.

Answer: Conflict comes from scarce resources, interdependence, goal incompatibility, ambiguity and personal differences; the Thomas-Kilmann grid and the dual-concern model are two frameworks to manage it.

Key points:

  • Sources: resource scarcity, task interdependence, incompatible goals, role and communication ambiguity, values and personality differences.
  • Framework 1 - Thomas-Kilmann grid: five styles from assertiveness x cooperativeness - competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating; each fits a situation (competing for crises, collaborating for important issues, avoiding for trivia).
  • Framework 2 - Dual-concern model: style choice follows concern for self (assertiveness) vs concern for others (cooperativeness) - high concern for both produces problem-solving, low for both produces avoidance, one-sided concern produces forcing or yielding.
  • Bonus framework - conflict process: latent → perceived → felt → manifest → aftermath; early intervention at the latent or perceived stage prevents escalation.

Takeaway: Diagnose the source, then choose the style that fits the stakes and the relationship.

Q7Describe the concept of spiritual intelligence quotient and discuss spiritual intelligence at workplace with examples.

Answer: Spiritual intelligence (SQ) is the capacity to act from meaning, values and transcendence - the "why" layer above IQ and EQ - and at work it shows as purpose, ethics, compassion and calm.

Key points:

  • Concept: IQ solves problems, EQ manages emotions, SQ asks why and connects work to deeper purpose; SQ = quotient of spiritual intelligence.
  • Components: self-awareness of values, compassion, ethical courage, perspective-taking, transcendence of ego.
  • At work: purpose-driven decision-making, ethical leadership under pressure, calm crisis response, respect and inclusion of people.
  • Examples: mindfulness and meditation rooms at Google India; Ratan Tata's values-led decisions and philanthropy; servant leadership in Indian family businesses; NGOs and social enterprises run on purpose.
  • Development: reflective practice, values conversations, coaching and purpose-aligned roles.

Takeaway: SQ turns competent managers into inspiring, trusted leaders.

Q8Explain the following: (i) Psychological Stressors  (ii) Inclusive Organization

Answer: Psychological stressors are the mental demands that strain employees - roles, relationships, careers, change; an inclusive organisation values and leverages diversity so everyone can fully participate and grow.

Key points:

  • (i) Psychological stressors: role ambiguity (unclear duties) and role conflict (competing demands); overload (too much) and underload (boredom); interpersonal stress (harassment, politics, difficult bosses); career stress (stagnation, insecurity); change stress (restructuring, new technology); outcomes - anxiety, burnout, psychosomatic illness, disengagement; responses - stress audits, employee assistance programmes and counselling (TCS EAP), workload redesign, autonomy, support networks.
  • (ii) Inclusive organisation: equitable policies and pay; psychological safety; accessible workplaces; representation at every level; zero tolerance for bias and harassment; practices - blind hiring, flexible work, employee resource groups, sensitisation training, diverse leadership pipelines; benefits - larger talent pool, better decisions, innovation, retention; Indian examples - Lemon Tree's disability inclusion, Tata women-leadership programmes, MNC LGBTQ+ policies in India.
