Solved Assignment Questions & Answers - MMPC-10

Q1. Define Managerial Economics and explain its scope and nature. (10 Marks)

Answer: Managerial Economics is applied microeconomics for business decisions. It bridges the gap between economic theory and business practice, focusing on how firms react to changes in the economic environment. It addresses three central economic problems: what to produce, how to produce, and for whom to produce. Its scope covers resource allocation, inventory management, pricing decisions, and capital budgeting, and it applies concepts such as demand, supply, elasticity, and market structure to guide decisions. The subject is normative, or prescriptive: it tells managers what they should do, whereas pure economics is positive, or descriptive, and states what is. It combines microeconomic theory with decision sciences such as operations research and statistics. Managers must also understand the four forms of market failure: monopoly, which creates deadweight loss; public goods, which are non-excludable; externalities, such as pollution costs borne by third parties; and information asymmetry, the classic market-for-lemons problem. Reliance Jio's entry into telecom illustrates the discipline: the company analysed demand elasticity, pricing, and market structure before launching free voice and cheap data, which disrupted the entire industry.

Key points:

  • Managerial Economics is applied microeconomics for business decisions.
  • Its scope covers resource allocation, inventory management, pricing decisions, and capital budgeting, and it applies concepts such as demand, supply, elasticity, and market structure to guide decisions.
  • It bridges the gap between economic theory and business practice, focusing on how firms react to changes in the economic environment.

Takeaway: Reliance Jio's entry into telecom illustrates the discipline: the company analysed demand elasticity, pricing, and market structure before launching free voice and cheap data, which disrupted the entire industry.

Q2. Explain the Law of Demand and discuss its exceptions. (10 Marks)

Answer: The Law of Demand states that, other things remaining the same, the quantity demanded of a good falls when its price rises and rises when its price falls; price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions. Two effects explain the law. The income effect says that when price falls, real income rises, so consumers buy more. The substitution effect says that a price fall makes the good relatively cheaper, so consumers substitute towards it. The demand function Qd = f(Po, Pc, Ps, Yd, T, A, CR, R, E, N, O) shows that demand also depends on the prices of complements and substitutes, disposable income, tastes, advertising, credit, interest rates, expectations, and the number of buyers. There are, however, important exceptions. Giffen goods are inferior goods in which the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, so demand rises with price, as with staple foods during a famine. Veblen goods are luxury status goods, such as expensive watches, which are demanded more at higher prices. Speculative goods, such as shares or real estate in a rising market, are bought more when prices are expected to rise further. A further distinction is that a change in own price causes movement along the demand curve, while a change in any other determinant shifts the curve.

Key points:

  • The Law of Demand states that, other things remaining the same, the quantity demanded of a good falls when its price rises and rises when its price falls; price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions.
  • Veblen goods are luxury status goods, such as expensive watches, which are demanded more at higher prices.
  • Two effects explain the law.

Takeaway: A further distinction is that a change in own price causes movement along the demand curve, while a change in any other determinant shifts the curve.

Q3. What is price elasticity of demand? Explain its relationship with total revenue. (10 Marks)

Answer: Price elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price, computed as the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is elastic when the absolute value of elasticity exceeds one, unit elastic when it equals one, and inelastic when it is less than one. The relationship with total revenue is crucial for pricing decisions. When demand is elastic, price and total revenue move in opposite directions: raising price reduces quantity demanded proportionately more, so revenue falls. When demand is inelastic, price and revenue move in the same direction: raising price raises revenue because quantity falls proportionately less. At unit elasticity, total revenue is at its maximum. In India, petrol is highly inelastic, with elasticity near 0.3, because commuters have few alternatives even when prices cross Rs 100 per litre, which is why the government taxes it heavily without losing much consumption. Air travel and branded clothing are elastic, with values near 1.5 and 1.8, so airlines discount heavily to fill seats. Arc elasticity uses the midpoint method: Ed = [(Q2-Q1)/(Q2+Q1)] / [(P2-P1)/(P2+P1)], while point elasticity is (dQ/dP) x (P/Q).

Key points:

  • Price elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price, computed as the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price.
  • In India, petrol is highly inelastic, with elasticity near 0.3, because commuters have few alternatives even when prices cross Rs 100 per litre, which is why the government taxes it heavily without losing much consumption.
  • Demand is elastic when the absolute value of elasticity exceeds one, unit elastic when it equals one, and inelastic when it is less than one.

Q4. Distinguish between Perfect Competition and Monopoly. (10 Marks)

Answer: Perfect competition is a market structure with many buyers and sellers dealing in a homogeneous product and free entry and exit, so every firm is a price taker facing a horizontal demand curve where P = MR = AR. A monopoly has a single seller of a unique product with blocked entry, so the firm is a price maker facing the downward-sloping market demand curve and MR is less than P. Under perfect competition, profit is maximised where P = MR = MC, and in the long run free entry drives economic profit to zero, with allocative efficiency (P = MC) and productive efficiency (production at minimum ATC). A monopoly maximises profit where MR = MC and then charges the price from the demand curve, so P is greater than MC, creating deadweight loss and reduced consumer surplus, which is partly transferred to the producer. Long-run supernormal profits are possible because natural, strategic, or legal barriers to entry protect the firm. Indian Railways is a natural monopoly in long-distance rail travel and practises third-degree price discrimination across sleeper and air-conditioned classes. MR = MC remains the universal profit-maximising rule across all market structures, and the efficiency comparison between the two structures is a frequent examination question.

Key points:

  • Perfect competition is a market structure with many buyers and sellers dealing in a homogeneous product and free entry and exit, so every firm is a price taker facing a horizontal demand curve where P = MR = AR.
  • Indian Railways is a natural monopoly in long-distance rail travel and practises third-degree price discrimination across sleeper and air-conditioned classes.
  • A monopoly has a single seller of a unique product with blocked entry, so the firm is a price maker facing the downward-sloping market demand curve and MR is less than P.
