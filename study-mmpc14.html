Block-01: Foundations of Financial Management

Unit 1: Financial Management — An Introduction

📖 Financial Management

Financial Management: Planning, organizing, directing, and controlling financial activities in an organization. Finance is the lifeblood of any business — needed even before a firm begins operations.

Three key decisions determine firm value: V = f [I, F, D] — Investment Decision (where to invest funds), Financing Decision (how to raise funds — debt vs equity mix), Dividend Decision (how much profit to distribute vs retain).

Net Worth = Assets — Liabilities (market value basis)

⚠ Agency Theory

Principal-Agent Problem: Owners (principals) hire managers (agents) who may pursue self-interest. Agency Cost: Monitoring costs + bonding costs + residual loss. Solution: Incentive alignment, performance-linked pay, governance mechanisms.

💡 Real-World Example

Infosys uses stock options (ESOPs) to align manager interests with shareholders — reducing agency costs. Tata Group prioritizes wealth maximization over short-term profits, investing heavily in R&D and sustainability.

🧠 Memory Aid

"I-F-D" = Investment, Financing, Dividend — the 3 key financial decisions that determine firm value.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Finance = Lifeblood of business; 3 decisions: Investment, Financing, Dividend
  • Traditional vs Modern: Raising funds vs Efficient fund management
  • Wealth maximization > Profit maximization (considers TVM and risk)
  • Agency theory: Aligning manager interests with owner interests

Unit 2: Time Value of Money

📖 Time Value of Money

A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow due to earning power, inflation, and uncertainty. Compounding: Finding future value of present money. Discounting: Finding present value of future money.

Annuity: Equal cash flows occurring at regular intervals for a specified period. Perpetuity: Annuity that continues forever; PV = A / r.

▶
Time Value of Money Explained
▶
Present Value and Future Value
FormulaEquationExample
FV (Simple)FV = P × (1 + R × T)₹1000 at 10% for 3 yrs = ₹1,300
FV (Compound)FV = P × (1 + R)^T₹1000 at 10% for 3 yrs = ₹1,331
Present ValuePV = FV / (1 + r)^n₹1,331 in 3 yrs at 10% = ₹1,000
FV of AnnuityFVA = A × [(1+r)^n - 1]/r₹100/yr for 5 yrs at 10% = ₹610.51

Rule of 72Doubling Period = 72 / RateAt 8% → doubles in 9 years
⚠ Compounding Frequency Impact

More frequent compounding = Higher FV. Effective Annual Rate (EAR) = (1 + r/m)^m - 1. Order: Continuous > Daily > Monthly > Quarterly > Annual.

💡 Real-World Example

SIP Investment: ₹5,000/month for 20 years at 12% returns = ₹49.9 lakhs (invested ₹12 lakhs). Power of compounding!

🧠 Memory Aid

"72/r" = Quick doubling time calculation. At 12%, money doubles in 6 years.

✅ Unit Summary

  • TVM: A rupee today > rupee tomorrow (earning power + inflation + uncertainty)
  • Compounding: FV = PV × (1+r)^n; Discounting: PV = FV / (1+r)^n
  • Annuity: Equal cash flows at regular intervals; Perpetuity: PV = A/r
  • Rule of 72: Doubling period = 72 / rate
  • EAR: More frequent compounding = Higher effective return

Unit 3: Risk and Return

📖 Risk and Return

Risk = Variability/uncertainty of returns (not just negative outcomes). Higher risk demands higher return (risk-return tradeoff). Beta (β) measures systematic risk relative to the market.

Risk Preferences: Risk-Averse (prefers lower risk for same return — most investors), Risk-Indifferent (only cares about return), Risk-Seeking (prefers higher risk for higher return).

✅ Unit Summary

  • Systematic risk (market, can't diversify) vs Unsystematic risk (firm-specific, can diversify)
  • Beta (β): Systematic risk measure; β = 1 is market benchmark
  • Risk preferences: Averse (most), Indifferent, Seeking
  • Diversification reduces unsystematic risk only

Block-02: Investment Decisions

Unit 4: Valuation of Securities

📖 Valuation Approaches

Three approaches: Income (DCF), Market (comparable companies), Asset (book value). Three-step analysis: Economy → Industry → Company.

Intrinsic Value: True worth of a security based on fundamental analysis, may differ from market price. Required Rate of Return: Minimum return an investor expects for investing in a security.

⚠ Bond Pricing Rule

Coupon rate > Market rate → PREMIUM. Coupon rate < Market rate → DISCOUNT. Coupon rate = Market rate → PAR.

⚠ Cootner Hypothesis

Market prices adjust to new information gradually in steps, not instantaneously. This explains why prices don't always reflect all available information immediately.

💡 Real-World Example

Government of India Bonds: 7.25% coupon when market rate is 7% → trades at PREMIUM. TCS Share: Using Gordon model with D₁ = ₹40, Ke = 12%, g = 8% → P = 40/(0.12-0.08) = ₹1,000.

🧠 Memory Aid

"I-M-A" = Income, Market, Asset — three valuation approaches.

✅ Unit Summary

  • 3 approaches: Income (DCF), Market (comparable), Asset (book value)
  • Bond valuation: PV of coupons + PV of redemption
  • Gordon Growth Model: P = D₁/(Ke - g) for constant growth
  • Cootner Hypothesis: Prices adjust to new info gradually in steps
  • Bond pricing: Premium, Discount, or Par based on coupon vs market rate

Unit 5: Cost of Capital

📖 Cost of Capital

Cost of Capital = Minimum rate of return to keep firm value intact. It is the opportunity cost of investing in a project vs alternative investments. Used as discount rate in capital budgeting.

Components Formula: K = r₀ + b + f (Risk-free return + Business risk premium + Financial risk premium). Hierarchy: Cost with financial risk > Cost with business risk > Risk-free cost.

⚠ WACC Formula

WACC = ∑ (wᵢ × kᵢ). Weights based on market value. Example: 30% Equity at 12% + 70% Debt at 10% → WACC = 3.6% + 7% = 10.6%.

💡 Real-World Example

Reliance Industries WACC ~9-10% due to high debt component (lower cost). TCS WACC ~12-13% due to zero debt (equity-heavy). Lower WACC → More projects viable.

🧠 Memory Aid

"K = r₀ + b + f" = Risk-free + Business risk + Financial risk — cost of capital components.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Cost of Capital = Opportunity cost of investing in project vs alternatives
  • Components: Kd (debt), Kp (preference), Ke (equity), Kr (retained)
  • WACC = Weighted average of all source costs (market value weights)
  • WACC numericals are guaranteed in exams
  • Flotation costs: Costs incurred in issuing new securities

Unit 6: Investment Appraisal Methods

📖 Capital Budgeting

Capital Budgeting = Long-term investment decision-making. Evaluating projects where cash flows occur over multiple years. Hurdle Rate = Minimum acceptable rate (usually WACC).

Payback Period Formula: Payback = Year before full recovery + (Unrecovered cost / Cash flow in recovery year)

⚠ NPV vs IRR — When They Conflict

NPV is ALWAYS preferred — directly measures wealth increase. Conflicts arise with mutually exclusive projects or different scales. Reinvestment assumption: NPV assumes reinvestment at WACC (realistic); IRR assumes at IRR (unrealistic).

💡 Real-World Example

Mumbai Metro project: Government used NPV analysis to justify ₹33,000 crore investment. NPV = ₹8,500 crore positive over 30 years → Project approved.

🧠 Memory Aid

"N-I-P" = NPV, IRR, PI — the three DCF methods. NPV is king!

✅ Unit Summary

  • 5 methods: Payback, ARR, NPV, IRR, PI
  • NPV > 0 → Accept; IRR > WACC → Accept; PI > 1 → Accept
  • NPV is preferred over IRR (considers project size, realistic reinvestment)
  • Payback ignores TVM; ARR uses accounting data not cash flows
  • Payback formula: Year before recovery + (Unrecovered/CF in recovery year)

Unit 7: Management of Working Capital

📖 Working Capital

Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities. Gross WC = Total Current Assets; Net WC = Current Assets - Current Liabilities. Balance between profitability and liquidity.

Operating Cycle: Cash → Raw Materials → WIP → Finished Goods → Debtors → Cash. Longer cycle = More WC needed.

⚠ WC Components

Cash Management: Baumol model, Miller-Orr model. Receivables Management: Credit policy (5 Cs: Character, Capacity, Capital, Conditions, Collateral). Inventory Management: EOQ model, JIT, ABC analysis.

💡 Real-World Example

DMart follows aggressive WC — minimal inventory, fast collection. Reliance Retail uses conservative WC with large cash reserves. Both successful with different strategies.

🧠 Memory Aid

"C-R-I-M" = Cash, Receivables, Inventory, Marketable securities — WC components.

✅ Unit Summary

  • WC = Current Assets - Current Liabilities; balance profitability vs liquidity
  • Operating cycle: Cash → RM → WIP → FG → Debtors → Cash
  • 3 approaches: Conservative (low risk), Moderate, Aggressive (high risk)
  • Components: Cash, Receivables, Inventory management
  • Credit 5 Cs: Character, Capacity, Capital, Conditions, Collateral

Block-04: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

Unit 10: Capital Structure

📖 Capital Structure

Capital Structure = Mix of long-term debt and equity financing. Optimal Capital Structure = Mix that minimizes WACC and maximizes firm value.

Features of Appropriate Structure: 1. Profitability (minimize cost of capital) 2. Solvency (ability to meet obligations) 3. Flexibility (ability to raise additional funds)

▶
Capital Structure Theories
FactorImpact
LeverageDebt boosts EPS when ROI > cost of debt (Trading on Equity)
Cost of CapitalDebt is cheaper (tax shield on interest)
Cash FlowMust service fixed charges (interest + principal)
ControlEquity dilutes control; debt doesn't

⚠ EBIT-EPS Analysis

Indifference Point: EBIT level where EPS is same under two financing alternatives. Formula: (EBIT - I₁)(1-T)/N₁ = (EBIT - I₂)(1-T)/N₂

💡 Real-World Example

Infrastructure companies (Adani, L&T) use high debt (leverage) because their ROI consistently exceeds cost of debt. IT companies (TCS, Infosys) prefer equity to maintain flexibility.

🧠 Memory Aid

"P-S-C-F" = Profitability, Solvency, Control, Flexibility — capital structure determinants.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Capital Structure = Mix of long-term debt and equity
  • Optimal structure minimizes WACC, maximizes firm value
  • EBIT-EPS indifference point for financing decisions
  • Trading on equity: Using debt to boost equity returns
  • Determinants: Profitability, Solvency, Control, Flexibility

Unit 11: Leverage Analysis

📖 Leverage Analysis

Leverage = Use of fixed costs to magnify returns. Three types: Operating (DOL), Financial (DFL), and Combined (DCL).

Leverage Measures: Debt-Equity Ratio = Total Debt / Total Equity. Interest Coverage Ratio = EBIT / Interest (higher = safer).

▶
Leverage Explained
LeverageFormulaMeasures
Operating (DOL)Contribution / EBITSensitivity of EBIT to Sales changes
Financial (DFL)EBIT / (EBIT - Interest)Sensitivity of EPS to EBIT changes
Combined (DCL)DOL × DFLSensitivity of EPS to Sales changes
⚠ Leverage Interpretation

High DOL → Small sales change → Large EBIT change (high business risk). High DFL → Small EBIT change → Large EPS change (high financial risk). High DCL → Small sales change → Large EPS change (high total risk).

💡 Real-World Example

High DOL firms (airlines, telecom) have high fixed costs — small sales drops cause large profit swings. High DFL firms (leveraged buyouts) amplify EPS volatility through debt.

🧠 Memory Aid

O-F-C = C/E × E/(E-I) × C/(E-I) — Operating, Financial, Combined leverage formulas.

✅ Unit Summary

  • DOL measures EBIT sensitivity to sales changes
  • DFL measures EPS sensitivity to EBIT changes
  • DCL = DOL × DFL measures EPS sensitivity to sales changes
  • Debt-Equity Ratio and Interest Coverage are key leverage measures
  • High leverage = high risk but potential for higher returns

Unit 12: Dividend Theories

📖 Dividend Theories

Does dividend policy affect firm value? Two schools: Relevance (Walter, Gordon) says YES; Irrelevance (MM) says NO in perfect markets.

Dividend Payout Ratio: Proportion of earnings distributed as dividends (D/E). Retention Ratio: Proportion of earnings retained in the business (1 - D/E).

▶
Dividend Theories
TheoryKey IdeaFormula
Graham & DoddLiberal dividends preferred; weight to dividends = 4× retained earnings—
WalterIf r > k → retain; r < k → distributeP = D/K + (E-D)r/K²
Gordon"Bird in the hand" — dividends less riskyP = E(1-b)/(k-br)
MM HypothesisDividend irrelevant in perfect marketsHomemade dividends
⚠ MM Hypothesis Assumptions

No taxes, no transaction costs, no flotation costs, rational investors. Investors can create "homemade dividends" by selling shares.

💡 Real-World Example

Indian IT companies like Infosys and TCS pay generous dividends (relevance theory), while high-growth startups retain all earnings (supports Gordon's model when r > k).

🧠 Memory Aid

W-G-M = Walter, Gordon, MM — Walter & Gordon = Relevance, MM = Irrelevance.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Walter & Gordon: dividend policy affects firm value (Relevance)
  • MM: in perfect markets, dividend policy is irrelevant
  • Dividend Payout Ratio = D/E; Retention Ratio = 1 - D/E
  • "Bird in the hand" — dividends are less risky than capital gains
  • MM assumes: No taxes, no transaction costs, rational investors

Unit 13: Dividend Policies

📖 Dividend Policies

Forms: Cash Dividend, Stock Dividend (Bonus Shares), Stock Split, Stock Buyback, Property Dividend, Scrip Dividend.

Policies: Stable Rupee Dividend (fixed amount per share), Incremental Dividend (gradually increasing), Stable Payout Ratio (fixed % of earnings), Residual Dividend (pay from leftover after investment needs).

Block-05: Advanced Topics

Unit 14: Behavioural Finance

📖 Behavioural Finance

Traditional finance assumes rational investors and efficient markets. Behavioural finance recognizes cognitive biases and irrational behavior. Prospect Theory (Kahneman & Tversky): People evaluate gains and losses relative to a reference point. Loss aversion: Losses hurt ~2x more than equivalent gains feel good.

▶
Behavioural Finance Biases
BiasDescriptionExample
OverconfidenceOverestimate own abilitiesTrading too frequently
AnchoringRely on first piece of infoSticking to IPO price
HerdingFollow the crowdBubble formation
Loss AversionFeel losses 2x more than gainsHolding losers too long
Mental AccountingTreat money differently by sourceSpending bonus frivolously
RepresentativenessJudge by stereotypes"Past performance = future results"
⚠ Market Anomalies

January Effect: Stocks tend to rise in January. Momentum Effect: Past winners continue winning short-term. Value Effect: Value stocks outperform growth stocks long-term.

💡 Real-World Example

2008 financial crisis = herding + overconfidence. Indian retail investors holding loss-making stocks hoping to "recover" = loss aversion. IPO frenzy = anchoring to listing gains.

🧠 Memory Aid

O-A-H-L-M-R = Overconfidence, Anchoring, Herding, Loss aversion, Mental accounting, Representativeness.

✅ Unit Summary

  • Prospect Theory: losses hurt 2x more than equivalent gains
  • Market anomalies: January Effect, Momentum Effect, Value Effect
  • Heuristics = mental shortcuts that can lead to systematic errors
  • Behavioural finance challenges the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)
  • 6 key biases: Overconfidence, Anchoring, Herding, Loss Aversion, Mental Accounting, Representativeness

Working Capital

One-Liner Concepts

📖 Key Concepts

• Finance = Lifeblood of business

• V = f[I,F,D] — Value depends on Investment, Financing, Dividend decisions

• Wealth maximization > Profit maximization (considers TVM and risk)

• Risk-free rate = Government securities yield

• WACC = Weighted average of all source costs (market value weights)

• NPV > 0 → Accept project; NPV < 0 → Reject

• IRR > WACC → Accept; IRR < WACC → Reject

• Payback Period ignores time value of money

• Operating Leverage = Fixed costs in operations

• Financial Leverage = Debt in capital structure

• Gordon's Model: P = E(1-b)/(k-br) — Bird in hand theory

• MM Hypothesis: Dividend irrelevant in perfect markets

• Behavioural Finance: Investors are NOT always rational

• Prospect Theory: Losses hurt 2x more than gains feel good

• Buyback limit: Max 25% of paid-up capital
