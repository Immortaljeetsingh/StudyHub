Q1(a) Operations Management and Production Systems[5]

Answer: Operations Management designs, operates and improves the systems that convert inputs (men, materials, machines, money, methods) into outputs (goods + services), and it is broader than production management, which covers manufacturing alone — the four production systems differ by volume and variety.

Fig: The OM transformation model and the five decision areas

Operations Management (OM) designs, operates, and improves the systems that create and deliver a firm's products and services. It manages resources — people, equipment, and technology — to transform inputs into outputs efficiently. Productivity = Output / Input, and lead time is the total time from order to delivery.

Production management vs operations management:

  • Production management covers manufacturing only, focuses on the factory floor, and produces tangible goods.
  • Operations management covers manufacturing plus services (hospitals, banks, IT), looks at the entire value chain, and produces both goods and services.

The four types of production systems are:

  • Job Shop: Low volume, high variety, e.g., a custom furniture maker.
  • Batch: Medium volume and variety, e.g., a bakery or clothing factory.
  • Mass/Line: High volume, low variety, e.g., a car assembly line.
  • Continuous: Very high volume, no variety, e.g., cement and chemicals.

Example: Maruti Suzuki produces 1.5M+ cars a year — one car every 45 seconds at Manesar — a mass production system coordinating 500+ suppliers and 2,000+ dealers.

Key points:
  • OM spans goods and services; production management is the manufacturing-only subset.
  • Four production systems: job shop, batch, mass (line), continuous — chosen by volume and variety.
  • Productivity = output / input; lead time = order to delivery.
  • OM covers the whole value chain; production management stops at the factory floor.

Takeaway: OM is the whole value chain; production management is just the factory floor — volume and variety pick the production system.

Q1(b) Five Key Decisions of Operations Management[5]

Answer: The five OM decisions — process, quality, capacity, inventory and workforce (OM-5D) — jointly create competitive advantage through cost, quality, flexibility and speed.

OM decisions span five areas (mnemonic: OM-5D):

  • Process: Choosing the production process and technology. Example: a furniture maker chooses a job shop process; a car maker chooses an assembly line.
  • Quality: Setting standards, inspection methods, and tools like TQM, Six Sigma, and ISO certification. Example: Maruti's quality checks at every assembly station.
  • Capacity: Deciding facility size for the long term, workforce levels for the medium term, and scheduling for the short term. Example: Jio built 4G capacity for 400M+ subscribers before launch.
  • Inventory: Deciding how much to order and when, using EOQ or JIT, safety stock, and ABC analysis. Example: Amazon India predicting demand with AI across 60+ fulfillment centres.
  • Workforce: Deciding how many people, with what skills, on which shifts. Example: a textile factory planning a second shift during the festive season.

These decisions together create competitive advantage through cost efficiency, quality, flexibility, and speed. They apply equally to manufacturing and service operations.

Example: A hospital applies the same five decisions: treatment process design, quality of care, bed capacity, medicine inventory, and nurse staffing.

Key points:
  • Process: choose production technology and method (job shop vs assembly line).
  • Quality: standards, inspection, TQM / Six Sigma / ISO.
  • Capacity: long-term facility size, medium-term workforce, short-term scheduling.
  • Inventory: how much and when — EOQ, JIT, safety stock, ABC.
  • Workforce: numbers, skills, shifts.

Takeaway: Five decision areas — process, quality, capacity, inventory, workforce — each is a lever for cost, quality, flexibility or speed.

Q2(a) Method Study vs Time Study, Therbligs, Standard Time[5]

Answer: Work study has two sequential parts — method study finds the best way to do a job first, then time study fixes the standard time (Normal Time + Allowances) for that improved method.

Work study systematically examines work to improve productivity. It has two components:

  • Method study: Finds the best way to do a job by analysing each step. Tools: flow process charts and therbligs. It comes FIRST.
  • Time study: Determines the standard time to perform the improved method at normal pace. Tools: stopwatch and PMTS. It comes SECOND.

Remember the sequence: first improve the method, then time it.

Therbligs are the 17 basic motions identified by Frank Gilbreth (Search, Find, Select, Grasp, Hold, Transport, Position, Assemble, Use, Disassemble, Inspect, Release, and others). Each motion is classified as effective (productive) or ineffective (wasteful); eliminating ineffective therbligs improves productivity.

The standard time formula: Standard Time = Normal Time + Allowances, where Normal Time = Observed Time × Rating Factor, and allowances (10-15%) cover fatigue, personal needs, and delays.

Example: Tata Steel used method study to improve material flow and time study to set standards, raising Jamshedpur productivity by 25%.

Key points:
  • Method study = find the best method (flow process charts, therbligs) — always FIRST.
  • Time study = standard time for the improved method (stopwatch, PMTS) — SECOND.
  • Therbligs = 17 basic motions (Gilbreth); effective ones add value, ineffective ones are waste.
  • Standard Time = Normal Time + Allowances; Normal Time = Observed Time × Rating Factor.
  • Allowances of 10-15% cover fatigue, personal needs and delays.

Takeaway: Improve the method first, then time it: Standard Time = (Observed Time × Rating) + Allowances.

Q2(b) Job Design Approaches[5]

Answer: The four job-design approaches are simplification (narrow tasks), rotation (move between tasks), enlargement (more tasks horizontally) and enrichment (more responsibility vertically).

Job design determines the content and methods of jobs. There are four approaches:

  • Job Simplification: Breaking jobs into small, repetitive tasks. High efficiency but low motivation. Suitable for mass production.
  • Job Rotation: Moving workers between different tasks to reduce monotony and build versatility. Used at Toyota and Tata Motors.
  • Job Enlargement: Expanding the scope of tasks horizontally — more variety at the same skill level, which increases task significance.
  • Job Enrichment: Adding responsibility vertically (Herzberg's approach) — more autonomy and decision-making, which increases motivation and satisfaction.

The first two focus on the quantity of tasks; the last two focus on the quality and depth of responsibility. Modern workplaces combine rotation with enrichment: a call-centre agent also takes ownership of a customer segment end to end.

Example: A bank cashier who only counts money is simplified; the same cashier rotated to loan processing every week is rotated; adding handling of customer queries is enlargement; letting the cashier decide daily cash limits is enrichment.

Key points:
  • Simplification: small repetitive tasks — high efficiency, low motivation.
  • Rotation: workers swap tasks — reduces monotony, builds versatility (Toyota, Tata Motors).
  • Enlargement: wider scope at the same skill level — horizontal expansion.
  • Enrichment: added autonomy and decision power (Herzberg) — vertical expansion.

Takeaway: Horizontal = enlargement, vertical = enrichment; modern workplaces mix rotation with enrichment.

Q3(a) Value Engineering and the Job Plan[5]

Answer: Value Engineering raises Value = Function / Cost through a systematic six-phase job plan that analyses what a product must do before what it costs.

Value Engineering (VE) is a systematic method to improve the value of a product by examining its functions. Developed by Lawrence Miles at GE in the 1940s, it asks "what does it do?" rather than "what does it cost?".

The core formula is: Value = Function / Cost. Value increases when we improve function, reduce cost, or both.

The VE job plan has six phases:

  • 1. Information: Gather all data about the product and its functions.
  • 2. Function Analysis: Define each function clearly.
  • 3. Creative: Generate alternative ways to perform the functions.
  • 4. Evaluation: Select the best alternative.
  • 5. Development: Prepare the detailed implementation plan.
  • 6. Presentation: Present and implement the plan.

Example: Tata Motors evaluates every vehicle component for function versus cost; Infosys saved Rs 500 crore in software projects by analysing functions and removing unnecessary complexity.

Key points:
  • Value = Function / Cost — improve function, cut cost, or both.
  • Six phases: Information, Function Analysis, Creative, Evaluation, Development, Presentation.
  • Function analysis is the heart: "what does it do?" before "what does it cost?".
  • Born at GE (Lawrence Miles, 1940s); applies to products, systems and procedures.

Takeaway: Ask what the item must do before what it costs — function analysis is the engine of VE.

Q3(b) Seven QC Tools and TQM Principles[5]

Answer: The seven QC tools (check sheet, histogram, Pareto, cause-effect, control chart, scatter, flowchart) diagnose quality problems, and TQM rests on customer focus plus continuous improvement (Kaizen/PDCA).

The seven quality control tools are:

  • Check Sheet: Simple form to record data as it is collected.
  • Histogram: Bar chart showing the frequency distribution of data.
  • Pareto Chart: Arranges problems by frequency — the 80/20 rule: 80% of problems come from 20% of causes.
  • Cause-and-Effect (Ishikawa) Diagram: Fishbone diagram showing possible causes of a problem.
  • Control Chart: Tracks process variation over time against control limits.
  • Scatter Diagram: Shows the relationship between two variables.
  • Flowchart: Visual map of a process, step by step.

Total Quality Management (TQM) rests on two core principles:

  • Customer Focus: Quality is defined by customer needs; both internal and external customers matter, with continuous feedback loops.
  • Continuous Improvement: The Kaizen philosophy of small, incremental improvements using the PDCA cycle — Plan, Do, Check, Act.

Example: A garment factory uses a Pareto chart and finds that 80% of defects come from stitching; the cause-effect diagram traces it to machine tension; a control chart then monitors the fix.

Key points:
  • 7 tools = check sheet, histogram, Pareto, cause-effect (Ishikawa), control chart, scatter, flowchart.
  • Pareto = 80/20 rule: 80% of problems come from 20% of causes.
  • TQM pillar 1: customer focus — internal and external customers, feedback loops.
  • TQM pillar 2: continuous improvement — Kaizen via PDCA (Plan-Do-Check-Act).

Takeaway: Pareto pinpoints the vital few problems; PDCA keeps the improvement loop running.

Q4(a) Inventory Management Techniques[5]

Answer: ABC analysis, EOQ, JIT and safety stock together balance holding cost against stockout risk; EOQ = √(2DS/H) gives the order size at which total inventory cost is minimum.

Inventory management controls material flow to minimise costs while ensuring availability. The key techniques are:

  • ABC Analysis: Classify items by value. A items (about 20% of items) carry 80% of value and need the tightest control; B items get moderate control; C items get simple control. This is the 80/20 rule applied to inventory.
  • EOQ (Economic Order Quantity): The optimal order quantity that minimises total inventory cost. Formula: EOQ = sqrt(2DS/H), where D = annual demand, S = ordering cost per order, H = holding cost per unit per year.
  • JIT (Just-In-Time): Order materials only when needed, which reduces holding costs but requires reliable suppliers.
  • Safety Stock: Buffer stock kept for demand uncertainty and lead time, preventing stockouts.

Example: A store with D = 1,200 units a year, ordering cost Rs 100 per order, and holding cost Rs 6 per unit per year has EOQ = sqrt(2 × 1200 × 100 / 6) = sqrt(40,000) = 200 units per order. Amazon India uses AI prediction plus ABC and JIT across 60+ fulfilment centres.

Fig: EOQ minimises total cost where the falling ordering curve crosses the rising holding curve

Key points:
  • ABC: ~20% of items (A class) carry ~80% of value — tightest control on A.
  • EOQ = √(2DS/H); worked example: D=1200, S=100, H=6 → EOQ = √40,000 = 200 units.
  • JIT = order only when needed — low holding cost, needs reliable suppliers.
  • Safety stock = buffer against demand and lead-time uncertainty.

Takeaway: Order where holding cost meets ordering cost: EOQ = √(2DS/H); ABC concentrates control on the 20% of items worth 80%.

Q4(b) Standardisation, Simplification, and 5S[5]

Answer: Standardisation fixes uniform specifications, simplification cuts product variety, and the 5S methodology (Seiri-Seiton-Seiso-Seiketsu-Shitsuke) builds the disciplined lean workplace.

The 3S framework: Standardisation establishes uniform specifications (benefits: interchangeability and lower costs); Simplification reduces the variety of products and parts (benefits: economies of scale and easier inventory); Specialisation focuses on core competencies (benefits: quality and efficiency).

The 5S methodology is the foundation of lean manufacturing and organises the workplace in five steps:

  • Seiri (Sort): Remove unnecessary items from the workplace.
  • Seiton (Set in Order): Organise remaining items for easy access.
  • Seiso (Shine): Clean the workplace regularly.
  • Seiketsu (Standardize): Create rules to maintain the first three S's.
  • Shitsuke (Sustain): Build discipline to follow the standards.

Waste (Muda) is any activity that adds no value, and Kaizen events remove it continuously.

Example: Hindustan Unilever cut product variants by 30% through simplification, lowering inventory costs; Indian Railways saved Rs 1,000 crore by standardising spare parts across zones.

Key points:
  • 3S = Standardisation, Simplification, Specialisation.
  • 5S = Seiri (Sort), Seiton (Set in Order), Seiso (Shine), Seiketsu (Standardize), Shitsuke (Sustain).
  • 5S is the foundation of lean — waste (muda) is removed continuously via Kaizen.
  • Benefits: safer workplace, less search time, visible problems, disciplined habits.

Takeaway: 5S = Sort, Set in Order, Shine, Standardise, Sustain — housekeeping discipline that kills muda.

Q5(a) Production Planning and Control (PPC) Cycle[5]

Answer: The PPC cycle runs through five stages — planning, routing, scheduling, dispatching, follow-up — with Gantt charts visualising time and MRP calculating material needs.

Production Planning decides what, when, and how much to produce; Production Control ensures actual production matches the plan. PPC coordinates materials, machines, and manpower.

The PPC cycle has five stages:

  • Planning: Decide what and when to produce.
  • Routing: Decide the path of operations and where each operation happens.
  • Scheduling: Assign time to each operation and machine.
  • Dispatching: Execute the plan — issue orders to the shop floor.
  • Follow-up: Compare actual output with the plan and take corrective action.

Scheduling tools: a Gantt chart is a visual bar chart showing planned versus actual progress of activities (developed by Henry Gantt); MRP (Material Requirements Planning) is a computer-based system that calculates what materials are needed and when, using the master schedule, bill of materials, and inventory records.

Example: Larsen & Toubro manages 100+ construction projects with advanced PPC and ERP, tracking material flow across 50+ factories so materials arrive exactly when needed.

Key points:
  • PPC cycle = Planning, Routing, Scheduling, Dispatching, Follow-up (PPC-RSDF).
  • Gantt chart (Henry Gantt): bar chart of planned vs actual progress of activities.
  • MRP: computer-based material planning from master schedule + bill of materials + inventory records.
  • Objectives: minimise idle time, meet delivery dates, balance machine loads.

Takeaway: PPC-RSDF: plan, route, schedule, dispatch, follow up — Gantt for time, MRP for materials.

Q5(b) Types of Plant Layouts[5]

Answer: The four plant layouts — product, process, fixed position, cellular — match volume and variety; the product (line) layout is best for mass production of standardised goods.

Plant layout arranges machines, equipment, and work areas for optimal material flow. There are four types:

  • Product (Line) Layout: Equipment is arranged in the sequence of operations. Best for mass production of standardised products with high volume and low variety. Example: Maruti Suzuki's assembly line.
  • Process (Functional) Layout: Similar machines are grouped together. Best for job shops with low volume and high variety. Example: machine shops in Ludhiana.
  • Fixed Position Layout: The product stays in one place while workers and machines move to it. Best for large, heavy products. Example: L&T construction sites, shipbuilding.
  • Cellular Layout: Machines are grouped into cells that produce part families. Best for medium volume and medium variety. Example: Tata Motors component cells.

Selection criteria: high volume and low variety favour the product layout; low volume and high variety favour the process layout; cellular layout balances efficiency and flexibility.

Example: Reliance's Jamnagar refinery, the world's largest single-location refinery, handles 1.4 million barrels a day using an optimised continuous-flow layout.

Key points:
  • Product layout: machines in operation sequence — mass production (Maruti assembly line).
  • Process layout: similar machines grouped — job shops, high variety.
  • Fixed position: product stays, workers and machines move — ships, construction.
  • Cellular: machine cells for part families — medium volume and variety.

Takeaway: Match layout to volume and variety — product line wins for mass production.

Q6(a) Supply Chain Management and the Bullwhip Effect[5]

Answer: SCM integrates suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, retailers and customers, driven by facilities, inventory, transportation and information — and fights the bullwhip effect with information sharing.

Supply Chain Management (SCM) manages the flow of goods from raw materials to end customers, integrating suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, and retailers.

The chain has five components: Suppliers → Manufacturers → Distributors → Retailers → Customers.

SCM is driven by four factors (mnemonic: SCM-FIT):

  • Facilities: Where products are made or stored.
  • Inventory: What is kept in the chain and how much.
  • Transportation: How products move between locations.
  • Information: The data that drives all other decisions.

The bullwhip effect is the amplification of small demand changes as orders move upstream: a 5% change in consumer demand can become a 50% swing in factory orders. Causes: forecasting errors, order batching, price fluctuations, and rationing. Solutions: information sharing, EDI, and VMI (Vendor Managed Inventory).

Push systems produce on forecast; pull systems produce on actual demand (JIT, Dell's build-to-order).

Example: Flipkart manages 100M+ products across 50+ warehouses, delivering 80% of orders within two days using push for fast movers and pull for slow movers.

Key points:
  • Chain = suppliers → manufacturers → distributors → retailers → customers.
  • Drivers (SCM-FIT): Facilities, Inventory, Transportation, Information.
  • Bullwhip = small demand changes amplify upstream (5% consumer swing → 50% factory orders).
  • Causes: forecast errors, order batching, price fluctuations; cures: EDI, VMI, information sharing.

Takeaway: Demand distortion amplifies upstream — share information and the whip cracks less.

Q6(b) Lean Manufacturing, TIMWOOD, and JIT-Kanban[5]

Answer: Lean (from Toyota's TPS) maximises customer value by eliminating the seven TIMWOOD wastes through five principles, pulled by demand via JIT and Kanban signals.

Lean manufacturing eliminates waste while maximising customer value. It comes from the Toyota Production System (TPS) developed by Taiichi Ohno. The five lean principles are: Define Value, Map the Value Stream, Create Flow, Establish Pull, and Seek Perfection.

The seven wastes are remembered as TIMWOOD:

  • Transport: unnecessary movement of materials.
  • Inventory: excess stock hiding problems.
  • Motion: unnecessary movement of people.
  • Waiting: idle time between steps.
  • Overproduction: making more than needed (the most dangerous waste).
  • Overprocessing: doing more work than required.
  • Defects: rework and scrap.

JIT (Just-In-Time) produces only what is needed, when it is needed, in the quantity needed. It requires reliable suppliers, quality at the source, and small batch sizes. Kanban is the visual signal system that controls production flow — a card or light that says "produce more" only when the next stage needs it. Kaizen means continuous improvement through small incremental changes.

Example: Toyota Kirloskar in Bengaluru runs JIT with only 2 hours of inventory; suppliers deliver parts several times a day directly to the assembly line.

Key points:
  • Five principles: value, value stream, flow, pull, perfection.
  • TIMWOOD wastes: Transport, Inventory, Motion, Waiting, Overproduction, Overprocessing, Defects.
  • Overproduction is the most dangerous waste — it hides the others.
  • JIT = produce only what is needed, when needed; Kanban = visual pull signal (card/light).
  • Kaizen = continuous improvement in small steps; origin = Toyota, Taiichi Ohno.

Takeaway: Overproduction is the worst waste; Kanban pulls only what the next stage needs.

Q1. Services vs goods production

“A clear line of demarcation between the products and services is much less apparent.” Explain the differences between services and goods production.

Answer: Goods are tangible, storable and made away from the customer, while services are intangible, perishable and produced-consumed simultaneously — and the line keeps blurring because most offers today bundle both.

Key points:
  • Tangibility: goods can be touched and inspected before sale; services cannot.
  • Storability: goods can be inventoried; services perish the moment they are produced.
  • Customer contact: goods are made without the customer; services need the customer's presence or participation.
  • Quality: goods are measurable and standardisable; services are judged during delivery (variability of people).
  • Blurred line: a restaurant sells food (good) with hospitality (service); a car is sold with warranty, finance and service contracts.

Takeaway: Tangible + storable + no customer = goods; intangible + perishable + simultaneous = services — most products are now a bundle of both.

Q2. Mass production — advantages and disadvantages

What do you understand by mass production? Explain the advantages and disadvantages of mass production.

Answer: Mass production makes large quantities of a standardised product on a flow line with specialised machines and division of labour, giving the lowest unit cost but little flexibility.

Key points:
  • Definition: high volume, low variety, standardised products, dedicated line, semi-skilled operators.
  • Advantages: lowest unit cost (economies of scale), high output speed, uniform quality, easier production control, less skilled labour.
  • Disadvantages: huge capital investment, inflexible to design change, worker monotony, line breakdown stops everything, heavy inventory of standard parts.
  • Requires steady, large demand — example: Maruti Suzuki (one car every ~45 seconds at Manesar).

Takeaway: Mass production is cheapest per unit but rigid — any change in demand or design is expensive.

Q3. Forecasting methods for long-term and medium-term demand

What are the various forecasting methods to forecast long-term and medium-term demand?

Answer: Long-term demand is forecast with judgemental and causal methods (Delphi, scenario building, econometric models), while medium-term demand uses time-series and causal methods (moving average, exponential smoothing, regression).

Fig: Forecasting toolkit by planning horizon

Key points:
  • Long-term: Delphi technique, scenario building, econometric models, market research — for capacity and location.
  • Medium-term: moving average, exponential smoothing, trend projection, regression — for aggregate planning.
  • Causal methods (regression, leading indicators, input-output analysis) link demand to economic drivers.
  • Qualitative: executive judgement, sales-force composite, buyer surveys.
  • Longer the horizon, the less accurate the number — update forecasts periodically.

Takeaway: Long term = judgement + causal; medium term = time series + causal; short term = time series.

Q4. Waste management and resource management

Explain the concept of waste management. “Waste management is complementary to resource management.” Comment.

Answer: Waste management treats waste as a misplaced resource — prevent, reduce, reuse, recycle, recover — and is the output-side twin of resource management, which secures the input side.

Fig: The I-O-W model and the recycling loop

Key points:
  • Waste = any input that leaves the system unused (material, time, energy).
  • Hierarchy: prevent → reduce → reuse → recycle → recover energy → dispose.
  • Complementary: resource management secures supply; waste management recovers value from what supply did not use.
  • Every tonne recycled is a tonne not purchased — waste cuts raw-material cost and pollution.
  • Examples: fly-ash bricks, plastic recycling, zero-liquid-discharge plants, e-waste recovery.

Takeaway: Waste is a misplaced resource — manage the output side and you extend the input side.

Q5. Value engineering vs cost reduction

“Value engineering is a powerful approach for improvement in the performance of the products, systems or procedures and reduction in costs without jeopardizing their function.” Explain the concept of value engineering. Also, state the difference between value engineering and cost reduction.

Answer: VE is a team-based, function-driven method that raises Value = Function / Cost without cutting function, whereas cost reduction attacks cost alone and may damage value.

Fig: The six-phase VE job plan and the value formula

Key points:
  • VE is systematic: information → function analysis → creative → evaluation → development → presentation.
  • VE asks “what must it do?” first; cost reduction asks “how can it cost less?” first.
  • VE protects function by design; cost reduction may unknowingly reduce function.
  • VE uses cross-functional teams and is most effective at the design stage.
  • Both end with lower cost — but only VE guarantees performance is preserved.

Takeaway: Cost reduction cuts cost and hopes function survives; VE cuts cost by design and guarantees function.

Q6. Flow (continuous) processing — advantages and disadvantages

What do you understand by Flow Processing? Explain the advantages and disadvantages of continuous form of processing.

Answer: Flow processing runs a standardised product continuously through a fixed operation sequence, 24×7, giving the highest volume and lowest unit cost but almost zero flexibility.

Key points:
  • Definition: material flows non-stop through the same sequence; typical of process industries (cement, chemicals, refineries).
  • Advantages: very high output, lowest unit cost, uniform quality, low labour content, minimal material handling.
  • Disadvantages: enormous capital, single-product rigidity, any breakdown stops the whole plant, needs guaranteed raw-material supply and steady huge demand.
  • Control is automated; runs round the clock on shifts.

Takeaway: Continuous processing suits one product, endless demand and heavy capital — change anything and it stops paying.

Q7. Short notes (any three) — all five solved

Write short notes on any three of the following: (a) Product Design (b) Critical Path Method (c) Work Design (d) Benefits of Standardization (e) Materials Handling System.

Answer: All five short notes are solved below so you can pick any three.

(a) Product Design: Product design converts a customer need into a manufacturable specification through idea generation, feasibility study, detailed design, prototyping, testing and pilot launch.

Key points:
  • Stages: concept → feasibility → detailed design → prototype → testing → launch.
  • Inputs: customer requirements, market research, technology, cost targets.
  • Tools: QFD (house of quality), design for manufacturability (DFM), value analysis.
  • Good design cuts production cost and raises reliability — example: Maruti's platform strategy.

(b) Critical Path Method (CPM): CPM builds the project network, finds the longest path of dependent activities — the critical path — which sets the minimum project duration; delays on it delay the whole project.

Fig: Critical path 1→2→4 sets the 9-day project duration

Key points:
  • Network of activities: forward pass gives earliest times, backward pass gives latest times.
  • Critical path = zero float; total float = latest start − earliest start.
  • Crashing = time-cost trade-off on critical activities only.
  • CPM uses deterministic times; PERT uses probabilistic times.

(c) Work Design: Work design sets the tasks, methods, tools and conditions of a job, combining job design, method study and ergonomics for productivity and satisfaction.

Key points:
  • Scope: content of tasks, work methods, tools, workplace layout, environment.
  • Objectives: higher productivity, quality, safety and worker satisfaction.
  • Tools: flow process charts, therbligs, ergonomic analysis.
  • Good work design reduces fatigue and defect rates simultaneously.

(d) Benefits of Standardization: Standardisation fixes uniform specifications for products, parts and processes — yielding interchangeability, consistent quality and lower cost.

Key points:
  • Interchangeability of parts simplifies repair and assembly.
  • Fewer varieties → smaller inventories and bulk buying discounts.
  • Consistent quality and easier training of operators.
  • Example: Indian Railways saved crores standardising spares across zones.

(e) Materials Handling System: Materials handling moves materials between operations safely, quickly and at least cost, using equipment matched to the load and flow.

Key points:

Worked solution (illustrative data):
  • Given: Fixed cost (FC) = Rs 5,00,000; Variable cost (VC) = Rs 50/unit; Selling price (SP) = Rs 100/unit.

  • Substitution: BEP = 5,00,000 ÷ (100 − 50) = 5,00,000 ÷ 50.
  • Result: BEP = 10,000 units — the plant must sell 10,000 units before profit starts.
  • Location choice: Location A (FC 2,00,000, VC 60) vs Location B (FC 4,00,000, VC 30): 2,00,000 + 60Q = 4,00,000 + 30Q → 30Q = 2,00,000 → indifference volume = 6,667 units — below it A is cheaper, above it B is cheaper.

Fig: Break-even and profit zone (illustrative data)

Key points:
  • BEP = FC ÷ (SP − VC); contribution = SP − VC.
  • Total cost line = FC + VC×Q; revenue line = SP×Q; their crossing = break-even.

  • MRP II adds capacity planning, shop-floor control, purchasing and finance — a closed loop.
  • PPC connection: master schedule is the heart; execution feeds back to correct the plan.
  • ERP systems extend MRP II across the whole enterprise.

(c) Process for Capacity Planning: The same six steps as Q4: assess capacity, forecast demand, find the gap, generate alternatives, evaluate, implement and review.

Key points:
  • Horizons: long (facilities), medium (equipment), short (workforce and schedules).
  • Tools: break-even analysis, decision trees, NPV of expansion options.
  • Review utilisation regularly — capacity is a moving target.

(d) Job Design: Defining tasks, methods and responsibilities of a job, balancing technological efficiency with human needs (skill, autonomy, satisfaction).

Key points:
  • Approaches: simplification, rotation, enlargement, enrichment.
  • Factors: technological, human, ergonomic, environmental.
  • Good design raises productivity, quality and retention together.

(e) Inventory related costs — with EOQ curve: Three cost types drive inventory policy: ordering cost, carrying (holding) cost and shortage cost; EOQ balances ordering against carrying at minimum total cost.

Fig: EOQ where ordering cost meets carrying cost

Key points:
  • Ordering cost: per-order clerical and procurement cost (S).
  • Carrying cost: storage, insurance, obsolescence, capital locked (H per unit per year).
  • Shortage cost: stockout, lost sales, expediting; reduced by safety stock.
  • Worked: EOQ = √(2 × 1200 × 100 ÷ 6) = 200 units; total cost = Rs 1200 per year.

Takeaway: Ordering falls, carrying rises; order where they meet — EOQ = √(2DS/H) — and carry safety stock against shortage cost.

Q1. Product development and design as an R&D activity

“Product development and design is basically a research and development activity.” Elaborate the statement with suitable examples.

Answer: Product development is an R&D activity: it converts research and customer insight into a manufacturable, sellable product through a staged pipeline — idea → feasibility → design → prototype → testing → launch.

Fig: The R&D-to-launch pipeline

Key points:
  • Pipeline: research → idea → feasibility → design → prototype/test → pilot/launch.
  • R&D nature: high uncertainty, long horizons, big costs, iterative learning.
  • Sources of ideas: customers, market research, competitor analysis, technology scanning.
  • Design inputs: function, cost, DFM, QFD, sustainability.
  • Risks: cost overruns, market rejection — stage-gate reviews filter them.

Takeaway: New products are born in R&D and die at the market gate — design them for the customer, not the lab.

Q2. Mass vs batch production — when batch is justified

Distinguish between mass and batch production. Under what conditions is batch production justified?

Answer: Mass production flows standard products continuously on dedicated lines; batch production makes limited lots on flexible machines — choose batch when demand is moderate, variety is real and changeover costs are acceptable.

Key points:
  • Mass: huge volume, low variety, dedicated equipment, low unit cost, rigid.
  • Batch: moderate volume and variety, general-purpose machines, setup between lots, higher unit cost, flexible.
  • Batch justified when: demand is too low for a dedicated line, product range is needed, demand is seasonal, customisation matters, or products change often.
  • Examples: mass = cars and soaps; batch = pharmaceuticals, garments, bakery.

Takeaway: Dedicated line only if volume is huge and stable; otherwise batch gives variety at a price you can plan.

Q3. Projects as non-repetitive tasks — activity networks

“Projects are non-repetitive large tasks. A systematic way of describing a project is through identification of activities and their inter-relationships.” Comment.

Answer: A project is a unique, one-time, large task with a clear objective and defined start and end; it is best described by breaking it into activities and mapping their inter-relationships in a network that reveals duration and criticality.

Key points:
  • Features: uniqueness, non-repetition, complexity, budget and time limits, cross-functional teams.
  • Description: WBS → activities → dependencies → durations → network (AOA/AON).
  • Forward/backward pass gives earliest/latest times, float and the critical path.
  • Tools: PERT/CPM, Gantt charts; examples: metro line, merger, software launch.

Takeaway: Projects are managed through their activity network — the critical path is the project's heartbeat.

Q4. Job design — management's changing view and key factors

Define job design. How has management viewed job design since the industrial revolution? List the important factors that must be addressed in job design.

Answer: Job design decides the tasks, methods and responsibilities of a job; management's view swung from Taylor's narrow specialisation to modern enrichment, teamwork and empowerment, and job design must address technological, human and ergonomic factors.

Fig: From craft to empowerment — and the factors that matter

Key points:
  • Definition: content of tasks, methods, tools and responsibilities of a job.
  • Evolution: craft → Taylor's specialisation → Ford's line → Hawthorne/Herzberg human factors → enrichment/rotation → socio-technical teams and empowerment.
  • Factors: technological (automation, equipment), human (skills, motivation), ergonomic (physical demands, safety), organisational.
  • Outcomes: productivity, quality, satisfaction, lower turnover.

Takeaway: Job design history is a pendulum from task efficiency to people motivation — today both swing together.

Q5. Who should be responsible for inventory control?

Who should be responsible for inventory control? Discuss this statement from a departmental as well as top management point of view.

Answer: Inventory control is a shared responsibility: departments control their own stocks within policies, while top management sets the policy, investment limits and review system — one group alone cannot do it.

Fig: ABC ranking — control effort follows value

Key points:
  • Departmental view: stores (recording, physical control), purchase (ordering, lead time), production (WIP), sales (finished goods, forecasts), accounts (valuation).
  • Top management: total investment target, ABC/EOQ/JIT policy, system approval, periodic review.
  • Conflict of goals: sales wants stock, finance wants none — coordination is essential.
  • Enablers: ABC, EOQ, reorder level, perpetual inventory, physical stocktaking.
  • ABC: 20% of items (A) hold ~80% of value — control effort follows value.

Takeaway: Departments own the day-to-day control; top management owns the policy — both must talk.

Q6. Why classify, codify and standardise materials?

What are reasons for classifications, codifications and standardisation of materials? Also mention the advantages and possible disadvantages of codification.

Answer: Materials are classified (grouped), codified (coded uniquely) and standardised (specifications fixed) so that identification, purchase, storage and control become fast, accurate and cheap; codification's advantages outweigh its few costs.

Key points:
  • Reasons: variety reduction, easy identification, computerisation, common spares, bulk buying, accurate control.
  • Advantages of codification: unique identity, no duplication, fast stores and purchase, enables ABC/FSN, supports standardisation.
  • Disadvantages: rigid codes may not fit new items, training needed, manual coding errors, initial cost and effort.
  • Standardisation gives interchangeability, lower inventory and consistent quality.

Takeaway: Classify to understand, codify to identify, standardise to economise — the three keys to material control.

Q1 (reconstructed). Production planning and control — meaning, objectives and cycle

Explain the meaning and objectives of production planning and control (PPC). Describe the PPC cycle in a manufacturing organisation.

Answer: PPC decides what, when and how much to produce (planning) and ensures the shop floor actually matches the plan (control); its cycle runs planning → routing → scheduling → dispatching → follow-up.

Fig: The five-stage PPC cycle with feedback

Key points:
  • PPC-RSDF: planning, routing, scheduling, dispatching, follow-up.
  • Planning decides quantities and timing; control ensures conformance.
  • Objectives: minimise idle time and WIP, meet due dates, level machine utilisation.
  • Tools: Gantt charts, MRP, loading sheets, dispatch registers.

Takeaway: PPC is the nervous system of the factory — plan, route, schedule, dispatch, follow up, correct.

Q2 (reconstructed). Plant location — factors

What is plant location? Explain the factors influencing the plant location decision.

Answer: Plant location selects the region and site for a facility to minimise total cost and maximise service; the choice weighs raw materials, market, labour, transport, power, climate and government incentives.

Key points:
  • Proximity to raw materials (weight-losing industries) and market (bulky, perishable products).
  • Labour availability and wages; transport and communication infrastructure.
  • Power and water supply; climate for process industries.
  • Government incentives (backward-area subsidies), land cost, community facilities.
  • Method: weighted-factor rating, break-even analysis, cost comparison.

Takeaway: Location is the most irreversible decision — weigh material, market, labour, transport, power and incentives.

Q3 (reconstructed). Forecasting — concept and techniques

Explain the concept of demand forecasting. Describe the qualitative and quantitative techniques of forecasting.

Answer: Forecasting estimates future demand from past data and judgement; qualitative techniques (Delphi, surveys, jury of executives) suit new products, while quantitative techniques (time series and causal) suit established products.

Key points:
  • Qualitative: Delphi, sales-force composite, consumer surveys, expert opinion.
  • Quantitative time series: moving average, exponential smoothing, trend projection.
  • Quantitative causal: regression, econometric models, leading indicators.
  • Forecast error is inevitable — monitor and update.

Takeaway: Forecasts are always wrong; the art is knowing how wrong — and updating.

Q4 (reconstructed). Inventory management — EOQ model

Explain the meaning and importance of inventory management. Describe the EOQ model and state its assumptions.

Answer: Inventory management balances holding, ordering and shortage costs; the EOQ model finds the order quantity minimising total cost, assuming constant demand, known costs and instantaneous replenishment.

Worked solution (illustrative data):
  • Given: D = 2,400 units/yr; S = Rs 150 per order; H = Rs 8 per unit/yr.
  • Formula: EOQ = √(2DS/H).
  • Substitution: EOQ = √(2 × 2400 × 150 ÷ 8) = √90,000.
  • Result: EOQ = 300 units per order; ordering cost = carrying cost = 2400/300×150 = 8×300/2 = Rs 1,200 each; total = Rs 2,400/yr.
  • Assumptions: constant demand rate, constant lead time, known costs, no quantity discounts, instantaneous receipt, no stockouts.

Takeaway: EOQ = √(2DS/H) — order where ordering cost equals carrying cost; relax the assumptions for real-world models.

Q5 (reconstructed). Purchasing department — functions

Explain the functions and duties of the purchasing department in a manufacturing organisation.

Answer: The purchasing department secures the five rights — right quality, quantity, price, time and source — through requisitioning, sourcing, ordering, follow-up, receiving and payment.

Key points:
  • Functions: requisition analysis, supplier search and rating, tendering, order placement, follow-up/expediting, receiving and inspection, invoice clearance.
  • Also: vendor development, price negotiation, contract administration, value analysis in buying.
  • Interface: with stores (requirements), production (schedules), accounts (payments), design (specifications).

Takeaway: Purchase is the front door of materials management — the five rights delivered at minimum total cost.
