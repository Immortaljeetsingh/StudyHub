Solved Assignment Questions & Answers

Q1. What is R&D? Explain the three types of R&D and their characteristics.

Answer: R&D is systematic investigation to discover new knowledge and applications. There are three types. Basic research is original investigation to gain new knowledge without a specific application in view; it carries the highest risk and the longest timeline, is typically funded by government, and its output is publications. Applied research is directed toward a specific practical aim or objective; it carries medium risk, is funded by mixed sources, and produces patents and prototypes. Experimental development is systematic work using existing knowledge to produce new materials, products, or processes; it has the lowest risk and shortest timeline, is corporate-funded, and produces commercial products. The lifecycle runs from basic research through applied research and development to commercialisation, remembered as BAD-C. R&D intensity, the ratio of R&D expenditure to sales revenue, indicates a firm's innovation commitment; pharma companies spend 15-20% of revenue on R&D. A pharmaceutical example illustrates the sequence: basic research discovers the molecule, applied research develops the drug formulation, and development conducts the clinical trials. R&D is viewed as a long-term investment rather than a short-term cost, and it must be aligned with business strategy.

Key points:

  • R&D is systematic investigation to discover new knowledge and applications.
  • A pharmaceutical example illustrates the sequence: basic research discovers the molecule, applied research develops the drug formulation, and development conducts the clinical trials.
  • There are three types.

Takeaway: The lifecycle runs from basic research through applied research and development to commercialisation, remembered as BAD-C.

Q2. Distinguish between incremental, radical, and disruptive innovation with suitable examples.

Answer: Innovation is the implementation of a new or significantly improved product, process, or method. Incremental innovation makes small improvements to existing products or processes, such as each new iPhone model after the original. Radical innovation is a breakthrough that creates new markets or disrupts existing ones. Disruptive innovation, as defined by Christensen, creates a new market by displacing established competitors, typically starting at the low end. The iPhone was disruptive innovation: it created a new smartphone market that displaced Nokia and BlackBerry, while subsequent models were incremental innovations. The innovation matrix classifies opportunities by market and technology: existing technology in an existing market is incremental, existing technology in a new market is market development, new technology in an existing market is radical, and new technology in a new market is disruptive. Innovation can also be classified by type: product, process, service, business model, and organisational. Open innovation (Chesbrough) uses external ideas and paths to market alongside internal R&D; closed innovation at Bell Labs relied purely on internal R&D, while Procter & Gamble's "Connect and Develop" is the classic open innovation example. The innovation funnel shows the reality: many ideas (1000) become few successful launches (1).

Key points:

  • Innovation is the implementation of a new or significantly improved product, process, or method.
  • Incremental innovation makes small improvements to existing products or processes, such as each new iPhone model after the original.
  • Radical innovation is a breakthrough that creates new markets or disrupts existing ones.

Takeaway: The innovation funnel shows the reality: many ideas (1000) become few successful launches (1).

Q3. Explain Rogers' adoption categories and the concept of crossing the chasm.

Answer: Technology evolves through an S-curve with four stages: introduction, growth, maturity, and decline. Its diffusion follows a bell curve across five adopter categories, remembered as innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards. Innovators are the first 2.5% to adopt, followed by early adopters (13.5%), the early majority (34%), the late majority (34%), and finally laggards (16%). Geoffrey Moore's "crossing the chasm" refers to the gap between early adopters and the early majority: early adopters buy for the vision and are tolerant of imperfections, while the early majority are pragmatists who need proven, complete solutions; many technologies fail because they cannot cross this gap. Electric vehicles followed the S-curve with slow initial adoption from 2010-2018, rapid growth from 2019-2025, and Tesla crossed the chasm from early adopters to early majority. Smartphones similarly followed Rogers' diffusion curve from innovators in 2007 to laggards by 2015. Innovation can be driven by technology push (R&D capability, supply-side, high risk, as with Post-it Notes) or market pull (customer needs, demand-side, lower risk, as with fuel-efficient cars).

Key points:

  • Technology evolves through an S-curve with four stages: introduction, growth, maturity, and decline.
  • Innovators are the first 2.5% to adopt, followed by early adopters (13.5%), the early majority (34%), the late majority (34%), and finally laggards (16%).
  • Its diffusion follows a bell curve across five adopter categories, remembered as innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards.

Takeaway: Its diffusion follows a bell curve across five adopter categories, remembered as innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards.

Q4. Explain the various modes of technology transfer with Indian examples.

Answer: Technology transfer is the movement of technology, knowledge, and skills from a source organisation to a recipient. The main modes are licensing, joint ventures, FDI, and turnkey projects. Licensing grants rights to use technology in exchange for royalties; it offers low control and low risk, as in franchise agreements. A joint venture shares ownership and risk; the Maruti-Suzuki joint venture transferred Japanese auto manufacturing technology to India, giving India manufacturing capability and Suzuki market access, and is the landmark Indian example. FDI involves setting up wholly owned operations with high control and high risk, as with Samsung India. Turnkey projects deliver complete facilities, as in power plant projects, with high cost and medium risk. The choice of mode depends on the trade-off between control, cost, and risk. Absorptive capacity, from Cohen and Levinthal, is the firm's ability to recognise the value of new external information, assimilate it, and apply it; without it, transferred technology fails to create capability. Barriers to transfer include tacit knowledge (difficult to articulate), cultural differences, and IP concerns. In India, technology absorption follows five stages: acquisition, assimilation, adaptation, improvement, and innovation (AAAII), as Tata Motors absorbed Jaguar Land Rover technology after its 2008 acquisition and Maruti adapted Japanese designs into the Alto and WagonR.

Key points:

  • Technology transfer is the movement of technology, knowledge, and skills from a source organisation to a recipient.
  • A joint venture shares ownership and risk; the Maruti-Suzuki joint venture transferred Japanese auto manufacturing technology to India, giving India manufacturing capability and Suzuki market access, and is the landmark Indian example.
  • The main modes are licensing, joint ventures, FDI, and turnkey projects.
