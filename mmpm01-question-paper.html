Q1. (a) Define consumer behaviour and explain its three components with suitable examples. (10 marks)

Answer: Consumer behaviour is how individuals and organisations select, purchase, use and dispose of products to satisfy needs, driven by three components - cognitive (beliefs), affective (feelings) and behavioural (action).

Consumer behaviour (CB) is the study of the processes that individuals and organisations use to select, purchase, use, and dispose of products, services, ideas, or experiences to satisfy their needs and desires. It combines psychology, sociology, anthropology, and economics, and it helps marketers segment markets, position brands, and design effective strategies. The three components are: (1) the cognitive component - the beliefs and knowledge a consumer holds, such as believing Bournvita contains DHA for brain development; (2) the affective component - the feelings and emotions attached to the brand, such as the nostalgia created by Amul's "Utterly Butterly" advertisements; and (3) the behavioural component - the tendency to act, such as actually buying Maggi because it is linked to childhood comfort. A good Indian illustration is Flipkart's Big Billion Days: the cognitive component is comparing prices and offers, the affective component is the excitement of flash sales, and the behavioural component is stocking up during the sale. Marketers must understand all three because a favourable belief without positive emotion rarely converts into a purchase.

(b) Studying consumer behaviour is important because it helps marketers segment markets, position brands, design persuasive messages, choose the right channels and price, and predict how consumers will respond to new products. It reduces the risk of marketing failures by putting the consumer at the centre of every decision.

Key points to remember:

  • CB = select, purchase, use, dispose; built on psychology, sociology, anthropology, economics.
  • Three components: cognitive (beliefs), affective (feelings), behavioural (action).
  • All three components must work together for a purchase to happen.
  • Example: Flipkart Big Billion Days uses all three components.

Takeaway: All three components - think, feel, act - must align for a purchase; Flipkart's Big Billion Days uses all three.

Q2. (a) Explain Maslow's hierarchy of needs with Indian examples. (10 marks)

Answer: Maslow's hierarchy arranges needs in five levels - physiological, safety, belonging, esteem and self-actualisation - with lower needs satisfied first, illustrated by Indian brands from Amul and LIC to Royal Enfield.

Maslow (1943) proposed that human needs are arranged in five levels, and lower-order needs must be reasonably satisfied before higher-order needs motivate behaviour. (1) Physiological needs - food, water, shelter; Amul milk, Tata Salt, and Britannia bread serve these. (2) Safety needs - security, health, financial stability; LIC insurance, Godrej locks, and Himalaya personal care address these. (3) Belongingness needs - love, family, social acceptance; Tanishq jewellery and Cadbury celebrations tap into family bonding. (4) Esteem needs - status, recognition, self-respect; Royal Enfield, the Apple iPhone, and luxury brands appeal here. (5) Self-actualisation - reaching one's full potential; Patanjali's spiritual branding and organic food brands resonate at this level. Application: the marketer must identify which need level the product serves and design the message accordingly. A two-wheeler advertisement may sell safety (ABS, helmet) to a family buyer but freedom and adventure to a young professional. The hierarchy is not rigid - in Indian markets, festival buying often triggers several levels at once, so brands like Cadbury combine family (belonging) with celebration (esteem).

(b) Herzberg distinguishes hygiene factors from motivators. Price, basic quality, and availability are hygiene factors - their absence stops the purchase, but their presence does not excite. Brand experience, recognition, and emotional connection are motivators that create delight and loyalty. Marketers must first fix hygiene factors, then add motivators.

Key points to remember:

  • Five levels: physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualisation.
  • Indian examples: Amul (physiological), LIC (safety), Tanishq (belonging), Royal Enfield (esteem), Patanjali (self-actualisation).
  • Herzberg: hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction; motivators create satisfaction.
  • Festival buying often triggers several need levels at once.

Takeaway: Match the product to the need level: Amul feeds, LIC protects, Tanishq bonds, Royal Enfield elevates, Patanjali fulfils.

Q3. (a) Explain perception and its processes of selection, organisation and interpretation. (10 marks)

Answer: Perception is selecting, organising and interpreting stimuli into a meaningful picture - through selective attention, distortion and retention, grouping principles, and interpretation shaped by experience and motives.

Perception is the process by which an individual selects, organises, and interprets stimuli into a meaningful picture of the world. It explains why two consumers can see the same advertisement and react completely differently. The process has three stages. Selection means paying attention to only some stimuli and ignoring others - consumers notice ads that match their needs and screen out the rest through selective attention, selective distortion, and selective retention. Organisation means grouping the selected stimuli into a pattern, using principles such as closure, grouping, and figure-ground. Interpretation means giving meaning to the organised pattern, based on the consumer's past experience, expectations, and motives. Marketers influence perception through packaging, colour, price, and positioning. A striking Indian example is Maggi: for decades it was perceived as a "2-minute comfort food", but when the lead controversy hit in 2015, consumer perception shifted overnight and sales collapsed - until rebranding, relaunch, and campaigns gradually rebuilt trust. Perception is thus subjective: the same product can be seen as premium or overpriced by different consumers.

(b) Learning is a change in behaviour arising from experience. Classical conditioning links a brand to pleasant stimuli (a jingle paired with happy moments), operant conditioning rewards purchases with offers and loyalty points, and cognitive learning builds understanding through information and reasoning. Together they turn repeat purchases into brand habits.

Key points to remember:

  • Perception = select, organise, interpret stimuli into a meaningful picture.
  • Selection works through selective attention, distortion, retention.
  • Maggi 2015 example: perception changed behaviour almost overnight.
  • Learning (classical, operant, cognitive) builds brand habits.

Takeaway: Perception is subjective: the same ad is seen differently by different consumers - as the Maggi 2015 episode showed.

Q4. (a) Explain the ABC model of attitude with a suitable Indian example. (10 marks)

Answer: The ABC model splits attitude into Affective (feelings), Behavioural (action) and Cognitive (beliefs) components, as in Patanjali's combination of herbal beliefs, swadeshi pride and actual purchase.

An attitude is a learned predisposition to respond consistently favourably or unfavourably towards an object, idea, or person. The ABC model says every attitude has three components. A is the affective component - feelings and emotions towards the object. B is the behavioural component - the tendency to act, such as buying or recommending. C is the cognitive component - beliefs and knowledge about the object. A classic Indian example is Patanjali. The cognitive component is the belief that Ayurveda and natural ingredients are safe and healthy. The affective component is the trust and pride generated by Baba Ramdev's presence and the "swadeshi" appeal. The behavioural component is actually buying Patanjali products, often replacing established FMCG brands. Marketers use the ABC model to diagnose weak attitudes: if consumers believe the product is good (cognition) but do not feel drawn to it (affect), the advertising should build emotion; if they feel positive but know little (cognition), the campaign should supply facts and demonstrations. Attitude change may begin at any component - sometimes a trial offer (behaviour) leads to liking (affect), and sometimes information (cognition) leads to belief change.

(b) Strategies to change attitudes include: changing the consumer's beliefs about the brand, changing the importance attached to a belief (e.g., making "natural" more important than "cheap"), adding a new belief, and changing feelings through emotional advertising, celebrity endorsement, or improved brand experience.

Key points to remember:

  • Attitude = learned, consistent favourable or unfavourable response.
  • ABC = Affective (feelings), Behavioural (action), Cognitive (beliefs).
  • Patanjali example: herbal beliefs + trust + swadeshi pride = purchase.
  • Change strategies: change beliefs, importance, add beliefs, change feelings.

Takeaway: Attitude change can start at any component - beliefs, feelings or a trial action - and marketers attack the weakest one.

Q5. (a) How do culture and subculture influence consumer behaviour? (10 marks)

Answer: Culture - the learned values, perceptions and behaviours of society - and subcultures of religion, region, age and profession shape what people eat, wear, celebrate and buy.

Culture is the set of basic values, perceptions, wants, and behaviours that a person learns from family and other institutions of society. It is the broadest and most pervasive influence on consumer behaviour, shaping what we eat, wear, celebrate, and buy. Culture transmits values such as respect for elders, family bonds, and religiosity, which appear in consumption - gift-giving at festivals, gold for weddings, and sweets for celebrations. Subcultures are groups within a culture that share distinct values and traditions based on common experiences. In India, subcultures form on the basis of religion (Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, Christian), region (Punjabi, Bengali, South Indian), age (Gen Z, elderly), and even profession. Each subculture has its own rituals, symbols, and consumption patterns, so marketers must segment by subculture and adapt products, festivals, and media accordingly. Culture also sets taboos - for example, beef and pork are avoided in many Indian communities. Because culture is learned and shared, it is stable but slowly evolving; successful brands study cultural meanings and align their positioning with them, while brands that ignore culture risk rejection or boycotts.

(b) McDonald's India is the best-known example of cultural adaptation. It removed beef and pork entirely, introduced the McAloo Tikki, McVeggie, and Maharaja Mac, set up separate vegetarian kitchens, and adjusted pricing for the Indian market, proving that global brands must localise to survive culturally diverse markets.

Key points to remember:

  • Culture = learned values, perceptions, wants, behaviours; the broadest influence.
  • Subcultures: religion, region, age, profession - each with distinct consumption.
  • Culture sets rituals, symbols, and taboos (e.g., beef avoidance).
  • McDonald's India: McAloo Tikki, no beef/pork, separate veg kitchens.

Takeaway: Respect cultural values and taboos (no beef and pork at McDonald's India), and segment by subculture.

Q6. (a) Explain the five stages of the consumer decision process with an Indian example. (10 marks)

Answer: The consumer decision process has five stages - problem recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision and post-purchase behaviour - as in an iPhone purchase in India.

The consumer decision process (CDP) has five stages. (1) Problem recognition - a gap between the actual and desired state, triggered by internal stimuli such as hunger or external stimuli such as an advertisement. (2) Information search - internal search from memory and external search through friends, YouTube reviews, and stores. (3) Evaluation of alternatives - comparing brands on price, quality, and features, forming an evoked set. (4) Purchase decision - choosing the brand, store, timing, and payment method, often using EMI and festival offers in India. (5) Post-purchase behaviour - satisfaction leads to loyalty and positive word-of-mouth, while dissatisfaction causes complaints and switching. Example: buying an iPhone in India. A slowing old phone creates problem recognition; the buyer watches YouTube reviews and asks friends' opinions; the iPhone is compared with the Samsung S24 and OnePlus 12; the purchase happens on Flipkart during a sale; and afterwards the customer shares an unboxing on Instagram but may feel doubt about the price, which Apple counters through ecosystem lock-in. Habitual purchases skip stages, while complex purchases use all five.

(b) Cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957) is the tension a consumer feels after purchase when they doubt their choice. It is strongest for expensive, infrequent purchases. Marketers reduce it with warranties, easy returns (Amazon's policy), follow-up calls, satisfaction surveys, and loyalty rewards - Tanishq's exchange scheme and personal follow-ups push its retention above 70 per cent.

Key points to remember:

  • Five stages: problem recognition, information search, evaluation, purchase, post-purchase.
  • Example: iPhone purchase in India - all five stages.
  • Habitual buying skips stages; complex buying uses all five.
  • Dissonance is reduced by warranties, returns, follow-ups, loyalty rewards.

Takeaway: Complex purchases use all five stages; habitual ones skip ahead; marketers reduce post-purchase doubt with warranties, returns and follow-ups.
