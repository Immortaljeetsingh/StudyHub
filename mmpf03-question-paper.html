  1. (a) Define a management control system. Explain its elements and how it differs from strategic control and task (operational) control. (10)
    (b) What is goal congruence? Why is it central to the design of a good management control system? (5)
  2. (a) Explain responsibility centres: cost centre, revenue centre, profit centre and investment centre, with suitable examples. (10)
    (b) What is responsibility accounting? List the essentials of an effective responsibility accounting system. (5)
  3. (a) What is transfer pricing? Discuss the market-based, cost-based and negotiated methods of setting transfer prices. (10)

Division B buys 10,000 units, processes them and sells for Rs. 130 each. Show the division profits when the transfer price is (i) market price Rs. 100 and (ii) full cost Rs. 80, and comment on the total company profit. (5)
  4. (a) Explain the budgeting process in an organization and the role of budgets in management control. (10)

  5. (a) Discuss the measures used to evaluate the performance of an investment centre: ROI, Residual Income (RI) and Economic Value Added (EVA). (10)

Q1. Management control system: elements and levels of control

Answer: An MCS is the detector-assessor-effector-communications process set that keeps resource use aligned with objectives, sits between strategic and task control, and is designed around goal congruence.

A management control system (MCS) is the set of processes managers use to ensure that resources are obtained and used effectively and efficiently in achieving the organization's objectives. Its main elements are: a detector (measures what is happening), an assessor (compares actual with standards), an effector (takes corrective action) and a communications network (carries information between them). In practice the MCS works through planning, budgeting, responsibility centres, performance measurement, reporting and reward systems.

MCS sits between strategic control and task control. Strategic control is set by top management, is long-run, unstructured and deals with the overall direction of the firm (e.g., entering a new market). Task (operational) control is at the shop floor, highly structured, rule-based and verifiable (e.g., a quality check on each batch). Management control is the middle layer — managers of responsibility centres coordinating day to day within the strategy, using budgets and reports; it is less structured than task control but far more structured than strategy.

Goal congruence means aligning the goals of individual managers and divisions with the goals of the whole organization, so that when each manager pursues their own targets, the organization's objectives are automatically served.

Key points to remember: MCS = detector, assessor, effector, communications; three levels = strategic, management, task control; MCS is the bridge between strategy and operations; goal congruence aligns personal goals with company goals.

Takeaway: MCS is the middle control layer whose detector-assessor-effector loop enforces standards, and goal congruence is what makes the loop serve company goals.

Q2. Responsibility centres and responsibility accounting

Answer: A responsibility centre is a unit whose manager is accountable for specified inputs and outputs - cost, revenue, profit or investment - and responsibility accounting reports to the manager actually in control.

A responsibility centre is a unit whose manager is accountable for specified inputs and/or outputs. Four types:

  • Cost centre: manager controls costs only — e.g., a production shop or maintenance department.
  • Revenue centre: manager controls sales revenue only — e.g., a regional sales office.
  • Profit centre: manager controls both revenue and costs (profit) — e.g., a product division.
  • Investment centre: manager controls profit and the capital invested — e.g., a business unit with its own assets, measured by ROI/RI/EVA.

The choice depends on how much authority the manager genuinely has: give profit responsibility only where the manager can influence both revenue and cost, otherwise holding them accountable for profit creates unfair outcomes and dysfunctional behaviour.

Responsibility accounting is a system that reports revenues and costs to the manager responsible for them, matching accountability with authority. Its essentials: clear delegation of authority; a chart of accounts aligned to responsibility centres; controllable vs non-controllable items separated; timely reports comparing actuals with budgets; and performance evaluation only on controllable items.

Key points to remember: cost, revenue, profit, investment centres; accountability must match authority; responsibility accounting reports to the person in control; separate controllable from non-controllable costs.

Takeaway: Give managers accountability only for what they can influence - authority and accountability must match.

Q3. Transfer pricing + numerical

Answer: A transfer price is the internal price one division charges another, set by market, cost or negotiated methods, and the numerical shows total company profit is identical (Rs. 5,00,000) whether the price is Rs. 100 or Rs. 80.

Transfer price is the price one division charges another division of the same company for goods or services. It should motivate managers to make decisions that maximize company profit, while keeping divisions fairly measured. Main methods: market-based (use the external market price — best when a competitive market exists), cost-based (full cost or variable cost plus a margin; simple but may not reflect market value), and negotiated (divisions bargain between cost and market price; realistic but time-consuming).

Numerical (part b):

  • Transfer at market price Rs. 100: Division A profit = (100 − 80) × 10,000 = Rs. 2,00,000; Division B profit = (130 − 100) × 10,000 = Rs. 3,00,000; total = Rs. 5,00,000.
  • Transfer at full cost Rs. 80: Division A profit = 0; Division B profit = (130 − 80) × 10,000 = Rs. 5,00,000; total = Rs. 5,00,000.

Comment: total company profit is identical (Rs. 5,00,000) in both cases — transfer price only shifts profit between divisions, it does not change overall profit. But it changes incentives: at cost, Division A shows no profit and its manager may refuse to supply, so a market-based price is usually preferred.

Key points to remember: transfer price = internal price between divisions; market price preferred; transfer price shifts profit between divisions, total stays same; wrong transfer prices can mislead decisions (e.g., sourcing from outside).

Takeaway: Transfer prices only shift profit between divisions, so choose the method - market-based usually - that keeps both divisions' incentives aligned with company goals.

Q4. Budgeting and variance analysis + numerical

Answer: Budgeting runs from top-management guidelines through unit proposals, consolidation, approval and communication to variance analysis, and the material variances work out to price Rs. 5,280 (F), usage Rs. 9,600 (A) and net Rs. 4,320 (A).

Budgeting is the process of preparing quantified plans for a future period. The process runs: top management gives guidelines; divisions prepare budget proposals; finance consolidates them; a budget committee negotiates and approves; the budget is communicated and used as a plan and control standard; and during the year, actuals are compared with the budget and variances investigated. Budgets motivate achievement, coordinate departments, and are the main control instrument of the MCS.

Numerical (part b): Standard quantity for actual output = 4,800 × 5 = 24,000 kg.
Material cost variance = (SQ × SP) − (AQ × AP) = (24,000 × 4) − (26,400 × 3.80) = 96,000 − 1,00,320 = Rs. 4,320 (Adverse).
Material price variance = (SP − AP) × AQ = (4 − 3.80) × 26,400 = Rs. 5,280 (Favourable).
Material usage variance = (SQ − AQ) × SP = (24,000 − 26,400) × 4 = Rs. 9,600 (Adverse).
Check: 5,280 (F) − 9,600 (A) = 4,320 (A). The favourable price was more than wiped out by using extra material.

Key points to remember: MCV = SP×SQ − AP×AQ; price variance = (SP−AP)×AQ; usage variance = (SQ−AQ)×SP; variance = actual cost − standard cost; adverse (A) means worse than budget.

Takeaway: Budgets are the MCS plan-and-control instrument, and variances such as the Rs. 4,320 adverse material cost variance flag where corrective action is needed.

Q5. Investment centre performance: ROI, RI, EVA + numerical

Answer: Investment centres are judged on ROI, RI and EVA, and the division's numbers are ROI 15%, RI Rs. 3,60,000 and EVA Rs. 3,60,000, all measured against the 12% cost of capital.

ROI (Return on Investment) = Operating profit ÷ Invested capital. It is widely used and easy to compare, but it can make managers reject profitable projects that would lower their division's average ROI, and push short-termism. Residual Income (RI) = Operating profit − (Invested capital × cost of capital). It overcomes the ROI rejection problem because any project earning above the cost of capital raises RI. EVA (Economic Value Added) = NOPAT − (Capital × WACC); it is RI adjusted for accounting distortions (R&D, goodwill, etc.), and is favoured by shareholders because positive EVA means wealth is being created.

Numerical (part b): ROI = 18,00,000 ÷ 1,20,00,000 = 15%.
RI = 18,00,000 − (12% × 1,20,00,000) = 18,00,000 − 14,40,000 = Rs. 3,60,000.
EVA = NOPAT − (Capital × WACC) = 18,00,000 − 14,40,000 = Rs. 3,60,000 (equal to RI here because no accounting adjustments are given).

Since ROI (15%) exceeds the cost of capital (12%) and RI/EVA are positive, the division is creating value for the company.

Key points to remember: ROI = profit/capital; RI = profit − capital × cost of capital; EVA = NOPAT − capital × WACC; positive RI/EVA and ROI > cost of capital mean value creation; ROI alone can cause bad project rejections.

Takeaway: ROI above the cost of capital plus positive RI/EVA proves the division creates value, not just profit.

Q6. MCS in service organizations and MNCs

Answer: Services control intangible, simultaneous, people-based output with activity, quality and customer measures, while MNCs face currency, transfer-pricing, political, autonomy and distance problems - each with a concrete control tool.

Service organizations (banks, hospitals, consultancies, telecoms) differ from manufacturers in ways that change their control systems: output is intangible and hard to measure (no physical units), production and consumption happen simultaneously (no inventory buffer), quality depends on people rather than machines, and cost drivers are activities and time, not materials. So MCS in services emphasizes: output/activity measures (e.g., patient days, accounts handled, call volumes), quality and customer-satisfaction measures, budgets built on activity-based costing, professional-standards control, and performance metrics like revenue per employee or turnaround time — rather than pure production-variance control.

Special control problems of MNCs (Unit 14): differing currencies and inflation — measure using budgets in local currency with exchange-rate variance analysis; transfer pricing across tax jurisdictions — use arm's-length (market) prices to comply with tax laws; environmental and political differences — build flexible budgets and local contingencies; autonomy vs centralization — decentralize operations but keep centralized reporting of key indicators; and distance and communication delays — use standardized MIS and periodic reviews.

Key points to remember: services = intangible output, no inventory, people-intensive; control via activity and quality measures, not production variances; MNC problems = currency, transfer pricing, politics, autonomy, distance — answer each with a concrete control tool.

Takeaway: Each MNC control problem has a concrete answer - local-currency budgets, arm's-length prices, flexible budgets, decentralized operations and standardized MIS.
