Q1. Explain the components of compensation with reference to the Indian context.

Answer: Compensation consists of all forms of financial returns and tangible services and benefits that employees receive as part of the employment relationship. It has two broad components: direct (financial) compensation and indirect compensation or fringe benefits. Direct compensation includes basic pay, dearness allowance, overtime, incentives, bonus, and commissions. Indirect compensation includes provident fund, pension, gratuity, ESI, medical insurance, sick leave, and housing. In the Indian context, a typical CTC breakdown for a Rs 10 lakh package would be: basic wage (Rs 4 lakh, usually 40-50% of CTC), HRA (Rs 2 lakh), special allowance (Rs 2.5 lakh), employer PF (Rs 48,000), gratuity (Rs 19,230), medical allowance (Rs 15,000), and LTA (Rs 20,000). Dearness allowance compensates for inflation and is linked to the All-India Consumer Price Index, revised twice a year. Fringe benefits in India have grown from 15-20% of total compensation in the 1990s to 25-35% today, driven by tax advantages, talent competition, and statutory requirements. Compensation serves four roles: attracting talent, retaining employees, motivating performance, and fostering the desired culture.

Key points:

  • Compensation consists of all forms of financial returns and tangible services and benefits that employees receive as part of the employment relationship.
  • In the Indian context, a typical CTC breakdown for a Rs 10 lakh package would be: basic wage (Rs 4 lakh, usually 40-50% of CTC), HRA (Rs 2 lakh), special allowance (Rs 2.5 lakh), employer PF (Rs 48,000), gratuity (Rs 19,230), medical allowance (Rs 15,000), and LTA (Rs 20,000).
  • It has two broad components: direct (financial) compensation and indirect compensation or fringe benefits.

Takeaway: Compensation serves four roles: attracting talent, retaining employees, motivating performance, and fostering the desired culture.

Q2. Compare Equity Theory and Expectancy Theory in the context of compensation design.

Answer: Adams' Equity Theory (1963) states that employees compare their input-outcome ratio with that of others; perceived inequity leads to motivation problems. There are three types of equity: internal equity (fair pay within the organisation), external equity (competitive pay relative to the market), and individual equity (fair person-to-person comparisons). When employees perceive inequity, they may reduce effort, seek a pay raise, rationalise, change their comparison target, or quit. Vroom's Expectancy Theory states that Motivation = Expectancy x Instrumentality x Valence. Expectancy is the belief that effort leads to performance, instrumentality is the belief that performance leads to reward, and valence is the value the employee places on the reward. All three must be high for motivation to occur; if any one is zero, motivation is zero. Equity Theory focuses on fairness of comparisons, while Expectancy Theory focuses on the effort-reward linkage. In India, internal equity means a software engineer at TCS should earn comparably to a similar role at Infosys; if the market rate for Java developers is Rs 8 lakh and a firm pays Rs 6 lakh, external inequity will cause attrition to competitors. Pay strategies follow the LLM framework: lead, lag, or match the market.

Key points:

  • Adams' Equity Theory (1963) states that employees compare their input-outcome ratio with that of others; perceived inequity leads to motivation problems.
  • In India, internal equity means a software engineer at TCS should earn comparably to a similar role at Infosys; if the market rate for Java developers is Rs 8 lakh and a firm pays Rs 6 lakh, external inequity will cause attrition to competitors.
  • There are three types of equity: internal equity (fair pay within the organisation), external equity (competitive pay relative to the market), and individual equity (fair person-to-person comparisons).

Takeaway: Pay strategies follow the LLM framework: lead, lag, or match the market.

Q3. Describe the four methods of job evaluation with their merits and limitations.

Answer: Job evaluation is a systematic method of determining the relative worth of jobs within an organisation to establish rational pay differentials. It evaluates the job, not the person performing it. There are four methods, remembered as RCPF. Ranking is the simplest qualitative method: jobs are sorted by overall complexity. It is simple and quick but highly subjective. Classification matches jobs to predefined grades; it is easy to understand but rigid. Point rating is the most popular quantitative method: points are assigned to five to eight factors (skill, effort, responsibility, working conditions) with defined degrees, and total points determine the pay rate. In the paper-mill example, the turner job scored 316 points versus 150 for the sweeper, justifying a higher rate. Factor comparison compares jobs factor by factor and assigns monetary values; it is detailed but time-consuming. Point rating is the most widely used method in medium-to-large organisations because it produces systematic, defensible pay differentials; Indian PSUs like BHEL and SAIL use point rating with 8-12 factors across thousands of positions. Job evaluation flows from job analysis to job description, job specification, job grading, and finally job assessment. Merit rating, by contrast, evaluates the person's performance, not the job.

Key points:

  • Job evaluation is a systematic method of determining the relative worth of jobs within an organisation to establish rational pay differentials.
  • In the paper-mill example, the turner job scored 316 points versus 150 for the sweeper, justifying a higher rate.
  • It evaluates the job, not the person performing it.

Takeaway: There are four methods, remembered as RCPF.

Q4. Explain ESOPs with reference to Indian IT companies.

Answer: An Employee Stock Option Plan gives employees the right to buy company shares at a predetermined exercise price after a vesting period. ESOPs are regulated in India by SEBI's Share Based Employee Benefits Regulations, 2014. The vesting period is a minimum of one year and typically three to four years with graded vesting, and the exercise window is usually three to seven years after vesting. Taxation works in two stages: at exercise, the difference between the fair market value and the exercise price is taxed as a perquisite (salary income), and at sale, the difference between the sale price and the fair market value at exercise attracts capital gains tax. Companies use ESOPs to retain talent in competitive markets, align employee interests with shareholders, conserve cash, and reward long-term commitment. Indian IT history is full of ESOP success stories: Infosys employees became crorepatis as the stock rose 100 times in the 2000s; when Walmart acquired Flipkart in 2018, ESOP holders received payouts worth over Rs 6,000 crore; Zomato's 2021 IPO made many employee ESOP holders wealthy. Because they lock talent into the company, ESOPs are often called the "golden handcuffs" of Indian IT. Cash-poor startups also use ESOPs as a compensation substitute.

Key points:

  • An Employee Stock Option Plan gives employees the right to buy company shares at a predetermined exercise price after a vesting period.
  • ESOPs are regulated in India by SEBI's Share Based Employee Benefits Regulations, 2014.
  • The vesting period is a minimum of one year and typically three to four years with graded vesting, and the exercise window is usually three to seven years after vesting.

Takeaway: Cash-poor startups also use ESOPs as a compensation substitute.
