Q1. Explain the concept of Time Value of Money and its importance in financial management.

Answer: The time value of money (TVM) states that a rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow because of three factors: earning power, inflation and uncertainty. Money received today can be invested to earn a return, prices rise over time, and future receipts carry risk. Two techniques operationalise TVM. Compounding finds the future value of a present sum: FV = PV x (1 + r)^n, where r is the rate of interest and n the number of periods. Discounting finds the present value of a future sum: PV = FV / (1 + r)^n. TVM is the foundation of all financial decisions: capital budgeting compares project cash flows occurring at different points of time by discounting them to their present value; security valuation prices bonds and shares on the basis of discounted future cash flows; loan amortisation and retirement planning also rest on compounding and discounting. The higher the discount rate, the lower the present value of a future cash flow, and the longer the time period, the greater the future value of money invested today. Without TVM, cash flows occurring at different dates cannot be compared meaningfully, which is why every investment appraisal method that respects value uses discounted cash flows.

Key points:

  • The time value of money (TVM) states that a rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow because of three factors: earning power, inflation and uncertainty.
  • Money received today can be invested to earn a return, prices rise over time, and future receipts carry risk.
  • Without TVM, cash flows occurring at different dates cannot be compared meaningfully, which is why every investment appraisal method that respects value uses discounted cash flows.

Takeaway: Without TVM, cash flows occurring at different dates cannot be compared meaningfully, which is why every investment appraisal method that respects value uses discounted cash flows.

Q2. Explain the various methods of investment appraisal used in capital budgeting. Why is NPV considered the best method?

Answer: Capital budgeting is the process of evaluating long-term investment decisions in which cash flows occur over multiple years. The five appraisal methods are: (1) Payback Period — the time required to recover the initial investment, computed as the year before full recovery plus unrecovered cost divided by the cash flow of the recovery year; it is simple and focuses on liquidity, but ignores the time value of money and cash flows beyond the payback period; (2) Accounting Rate of Return — average profit divided by average investment; it uses accounting data and ignores TVM; (3) Net Present Value — the sum of discounted cash flows minus the initial investment, accepted when NPV is greater than zero; (4) Internal Rate of Return — the discount rate at which NPV equals zero, accepted when IRR exceeds the cost of capital (WACC); and (5) Profitability Index — the ratio of present value of inflows to present value of outflows, accepted when PI is greater than one, and useful under capital rationing. NPV is considered the best method because it directly measures the increase in shareholder wealth, handles mutually exclusive projects and projects of different scale correctly, and assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital, which is more realistic than the IRR's reinvestment assumption. For example, the Mumbai Metro's Rs. 33,000 crore investment was approved on the strength of a positive NPV of Rs. 8,500 crore.

Q3. What is capital structure? Discuss the features of an appropriate capital structure.

Answer: Capital structure is the mix of long-term debt and equity financing used by a company. An optimal capital structure is the combination that minimises the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) and thereby maximises the value of the firm. The features of an appropriate capital structure are: (1) Profitability — it should minimise the cost of capital; (2) Solvency — the company must be able to meet its fixed obligations of interest and principal as they fall due; and (3) Flexibility — it should allow the company to raise additional funds when required. Other determinants include leverage, since debt boosts EPS when ROI exceeds the cost of debt (trading on equity); cost of capital, because debt is cheaper than equity on account of the tax shield on interest; control, since equity financing dilutes control while debt does not; and size, because larger firms can access debt markets more easily. Infrastructure companies such as Adani and L&T use high leverage because their ROI consistently exceeds the cost of debt, while IT companies such as TCS and Infosys prefer equity to retain flexibility. The EBIT-EPS analysis and the indifference point help a finance manager choose between alternative financing plans.

Key points:

  • Capital structure is the mix of long-term debt and equity financing used by a company.
  • Infrastructure companies such as Adani and L&T use high leverage because their ROI consistently exceeds the cost of debt, while IT companies such as TCS and Infosys prefer equity to retain flexibility.
  • An optimal capital structure is the combination that minimises the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) and thereby maximises the value of the firm.

Takeaway: The EBIT-EPS analysis and the indifference point help a finance manager choose between alternative financing plans.

Q4. Explain the relevance and irrelevance theories of dividend policy.

Answer: Dividend theories debate whether the dividend decision affects the value of the firm. The irrelevance school, led by Modigliani and Miller (MM), holds that in perfect markets — no taxes, no transaction costs, rational investors and no information asymmetry — dividend policy does not affect firm value. Value depends on the earning power of the firm's assets, not on how profits are distributed; investors can create home-made dividends by selling part of their holdings, so a firm that retains earnings and reinvests them at a return above the cost of capital creates equal value. The relevance school argues the opposite. Walter's model states that value depends on the relationship between the return on investment (r) and the cost of capital (k): growth firms (r > k) should retain profits and pay low dividends, while declining firms (r < k) should distribute more. Gordon's bird-in-hand argument holds that investors value certain current dividends more highly than uncertain future capital gains, so a higher payout raises value. In practice, firms follow policies such as stable rupee dividend, constant payout ratio, low-plus-extra dividend, or residual dividend, and distribute through cash dividends, bonus shares, stock splits or buybacks.

  • Dividend theories debate whether the dividend decision affects the value of the firm.
