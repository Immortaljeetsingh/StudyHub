Unit 1: Basics of Communication

📚 Definition

Communication is the process of exchanging information, ideas, thoughts, feelings, and emotions between two or more persons through verbal, written, or non-verbal means to achieve mutual understanding.

Point 1: Nature and Importance of Communication

Communication is the lifeblood of any organization. It serves as the foundation for all managerial functions — planning, organizing, staffing, directing, and controlling cannot exist without effective communication.

  • Coordination: Aligns efforts of individuals toward common organizational goals
  • Decision-Making: Provides the information base for sound decisions
  • Motivation: Clarifies roles, expectations, and feedback to employees
  • Relationship Building: Fosters trust between management and workforce
  • Change Management: Facilitates smooth implementation of organizational changes

Point 4: General vs Business Communication

🌱 General Communication
  • Personal and informal in nature
  • Flexible format and structure
  • No specific rules or conventions
  • Emotional expression is common
  • Example: Chatting with friends about weekend plans
💼 Business Communication
  • Professional and formal tone
  • Goal-oriented and task-focused
  • Follows specific conventions and protocols
  • Concise, clear, and documented
  • Example: Sending a quarterly sales report to management

Point 7: Case Study — Infosys Communication Transformation

📚 Case Study

Problem: In 2015, Infosys faced communication challenges with 200,000+ employees across 46 countries. Email overload, cultural miscommunication, and delayed decision-making were costing projects.

Solution: They implemented a unified communication platform (collaboration tools + video conferencing), introduced "Communication Champions" in each unit, and trained managers in cross-cultural communication.

Result: 35% reduction in email volume, 40% faster decision-making, and employee satisfaction scores improved by 22%. This case demonstrates how organizational communication directly impacts business outcomes.

⚠️ Exam Important

Noise enters at ANY point in the process. It is the single biggest reason for communication failure. The model emphasizes that communication is NOT complete until feedback is received.

Point 2: Encoding and Decoding

🔑 Encoding
  • Converting thoughts into symbols (words, images, gestures)
  • Sender's vocabulary, education, and culture influence encoding
  • Poor encoding = misunderstood message
  • Example: A manager using technical jargon with non-technical staff
🔒 Decoding
  • Interpreting symbols back into meaningful ideas
  • Receiver's background affects interpretation
  • Requires active listening and attention
  • Example: Employee misunderstanding "ASAP" as "by end of day" vs "immediately"

Point 3: Types of Noise (Barriers)

CulturalDifferent norms and valuesIndian nodding head (meaning "I hear you") vs Western "I agree"

Point 4: Feedback — The Critical Loop

Feedback transforms one-way transmission into two-way communication. Without feedback, the sender cannot confirm understanding.

  • Positive Feedback: Confirmation of understanding ("Got it, will proceed")
  • Negative Feedback: Signals misunderstanding ("Can you clarify what you mean?")
  • Delayed Feedback: Response after time gap (email reply after 2 days)
  • Immediate Feedback: Real-time response (nodding during face-to-face meeting)
💡 Indian Example

Infosys implemented a "Feedback Friday" system where every team member gives structured feedback to peers weekly. This improved communication effectiveness by 40% and reduced project rework by 25%.

Point 5: Overcoming Communication Barriers

  1. Simplify Language: Avoid jargon; use words the receiver understands
  2. Active Listening: Focus completely on the speaker; don't plan your response while they talk
  3. Choose the Right Channel: Sensitive news in person, not over email
  4. Encourage Feedback: Ask "Do you have any questions?" genuinely
  5. Be Aware of Cultural Context: Adapt style to audience background
  6. Use Multiple Channels: Follow up verbal instructions with written summary
🧠 Mnemonic

"SCALe" for overcoming barriers — Simplify language, Choose right channel, Active listening, Loop feedback.

✅ Unit 2 Summary

  • Shannon-Weaver: linear model with noise concept; Sender → Transmitter → Channel → Receiver → Destination
  • Encoding = converting thoughts to symbols; Decoding = interpreting symbols back to meaning
  • 6 types of noise: Physical, Psychological, Semantic, Technical, Organizational, Cultural
  • Feedback transforms one-way transmission into two-way communication
  • Barriers: SCALe — Simplify language, Choose right channel, Active listening, Loop feedback

📚 Definition

Communication can be classified along multiple dimensions: verbal vs non-verbal, oral vs written, formal vs informal, and internal vs external. Each type serves different purposes and has distinct advantages.

Point 1: Verbal vs Non-Verbal Communication

💬 Verbal Communication
  • Uses spoken or written words
  • Direct and explicit meaning
  • Can be recorded and referenced later
  • Accounts for only 7% of emotional impact (Mehrabian's rule)
  • Examples: speeches, letters, emails, reports, phone calls
💭 Non-Verbal Communication
  • Uses body language, facial expressions, gestures, tone
  • Subtle and often unconscious
  • Accounts for 55% body language + 38% tone of voice
  • Culture-specific interpretations
  • Examples: eye contact, posture, handshake, dress, silence
⚠️ Exam Important

Mehrabian's 7-38-55 Rule: In face-to-face communication of feelings and attitudes, 7% is verbal, 38% is vocal (tone), and 55% is visual (body language). This means 93% of emotional communication is non-verbal.

Point 3: Oral vs Written Communication

🎤 Oral Communication
  • Immediate feedback possible
  • Personal touch and emotional expression
  • Tone and body language add meaning
  • Difficult to retract once spoken
  • Suitable for: discussions, negotiations, interviews
📝 Written Communication
  • Creates permanent record
  • Precise, detailed, and legal evidence
  • Time to think, revise, and perfect
  • No immediate feedback
  • Suitable for: contracts, policies, formal reports

Point 4: Internal vs External Communication

  • Internal: Within organization — memos, notices, team meetings, intranet, circulars
  • External: With outside parties — press releases, advertisements, annual reports, proposals
💡 Indian Example

Reliance Jio uses internal communication via their employee app "MyJio" for 50,000+ employees, while external communication includes massive advertising campaigns and investor presentations.

✅ Unit 3 Summary

  • Verbal (7%) vs Non-Verbal (93%): Mehrabian’s 7-38-55 rule for emotional communication
  • Non-verbal dimensions: Kinesics, Proxemics, Haptics, Chronemics, Paralanguage, Artifacts
  • Formal (official hierarchy, documented) vs Informal/Grapevine (personal networks, faster)
  • Oral (immediate feedback, tone) vs Written (permanent record, precise, legal evidence)
  • Internal (memos, intranet) vs External (press releases, advertisements, annual reports)

Point 2: Grapevine (Informal Communication)

Keith Davis identified 4 types of grapevine:

  • Single Strand: A tells B, B tells C, C tells D (serial chain)
  • Gossip Chain: One person tells everyone (A → B, C, D, E)
  • Probability Chain: Random sharing (each tells random others)
  • Cluster Chain: Most common — A tells selected friends, who tell their friends
⚠️ Exam Important

Grapevine is NOT always inaccurate. Research shows it is 75-90% accurate. Managers should use it strategically to gauge employee sentiment and disseminate information that doesn't need formal documentation.

Point 1: The Listening Gap

  • We spend 45% of communication time listening (more than any other activity)
  • Immediately after hearing, we retain only 50% of the message
  • After 48 hours, retention drops to 25% — and half of that is wrong
  • Most managers listen at only 25% efficiency

Point 1: Johari Window Model

👁 Open Area

Known to self AND others. Builds through disclosure and feedback. The larger this area, the better the communication.

👀 Blind Spot

Unknown to self but known to others. Reduced through seeking feedback. Example: You don't realize you interrupt others frequently.

🔒 Hidden Area

Known to self but unknown to others. Reduced through voluntary disclosure. Example: You know you're job-hunting but haven't told anyone.

❓ Unknown

Unknown to both. Represents untapped potential or deep unconscious factors. Reduced through self-discovery and observation.

⚠️ Exam Important

The Johari Window (Joseph Luft & Harry Ingham) emphasizes that effective interpersonal communication requires BOTH self-disclosure (reducing Hidden Area) AND seeking feedback (reducing Blind Spot).

Point 4: Building Rapport

  • Matching and Mirroring: Subtly adopting the other person's posture and speech patterns
  • Finding Common Ground: Shared interests, experiences, or goals
  • Active Engagement: Nodding, paraphrasing, asking follow-up questions
  • Genuine Interest: Remembering details from previous conversations

Point 5: Handling Difficult Interactions

  1. Stay Calm: Deep breath before responding to provocations
  2. Listen First: Understand the other person's perspective fully
  3. Acknowledge Feelings: "I understand you're frustrated" without necessarily agreeing
  4. Focus on Interests, Not Positions: "What outcome works for both of us?"
  5. Seek Win-Win: Collaborative problem-solving over adversarial negotiation
💡 Indian Example

Ratan Tata's handling of the Jaguar-Land Rover acquisition negotiation exemplifies excellent interpersonal communication — maintaining respectful relationships with Ford executives who had earlier humiliated Tata, and closing the deal with dignity.

✅ Unit 6 Summary

  • Emotional Intelligence (Goleman): Self-Awareness, Self-Regulation, Motivation, Empathy, Social Skills
  • Non-verbal dimensions: Kinesics, Proxemics, Haptics, Chronemics, Paralanguage, Artifacts
  • Building rapport: Matching/mirroring, common ground, active engagement, genuine interest
  • Difficult interactions: Stay calm, listen first, acknowledge feelings, focus on interests, seek win-win

Point 2: Pre-Meeting Planning

  • Agenda: List topics with time allocations, prioritize, distribute 24-48 hours in advance
  • Participants: Invite only essential attendees (Amazon's "two-pizza rule")
  • Preparation: Share background materials, specify required pre-reading
  • Logistics: Book room, test technology, arrange seating

Point 3: Conducting Effective Meetings

✅ Chairperson's Dos
  • Start and end on time
  • Keep discussion focused on agenda
  • Encourage quiet members to speak
  • Manage conflicts diplomatically
  • Summarize decisions and action items
❌ Common Mistakes
  • No agenda (meeting drifts)
  • Too many participants (decision paralysis)
  • Allowing side conversations
  • No follow-up on action items
  • Running over scheduled time

Point 4: Meeting Etiquette

  • Arrive 2-3 minutes early
  • Silence mobile devices
  • Listen actively — don't prepare your response while others speak
  • Speak concisely — respect everyone's time
  • Don't interrupt — raise hand or use chat function in virtual meetings
  • Stay for the entire meeting — leaving early signals disrespect

Point 5: Minutes of Meeting (MoM)

Essential components:

  • Date, time, venue
  • Attendees (present, absent, apologies)
  • Agenda items discussed
  • Decisions made (with voting results if applicable)
  • Action items with responsible person and deadline
  • Date of next meeting
🧠 Mnemonic

"ADAP" for effective meetings — Agenda (prepare), Decisions (make), Action items (assign), Progress (follow up).

💡 Indian Example

Amazon India follows the "6-page memo" format — no PowerPoint slides. Participants read a narrative memo in silence for the first 20 minutes, then discuss. This ensures deep thinking and eliminates superficial presentations.

✅ Unit 7 Summary

  • Types: Board, Committee, Team, Brainstorming, One-on-One, AGM
  • Pre-meeting: Agenda (24-48h advance), essential participants (two-pizza rule), background materials
  • Chairperson dos: Start/end on time, focus on agenda, encourage quiet members, summarize decisions
  • Meeting etiquette: Arrive early, silence phones, listen actively, speak concisely, don’t interrupt
  • MoM: Date, attendees, agenda items, decisions, action items (person + deadline), next meeting date
  • Mnemonic: ADAP — Agenda, Decisions, Action items, Progress

Point 2: Presentation Structure (Tell-Show-Tell)

🎯 Presentation Flow Structure
🆕 Introduction (10%)
  • Hook the audience (question, story, statistic)
  • State the purpose clearly
  • Preview main points
📦 Body (80%)
  • 3-5 main points maximum
  • Support each with evidence
  • Use transitions between points
🆕 Conclusion (10%)

Summarize key points, state call to action, end with memorable closing statement

Point 3: Visual Aids Best Practices

  • 6x6 Rule: Maximum 6 bullet points per slide, 6 words per bullet
  • Font Size: Minimum 24pt for body text, 36pt for headings
  • Contrast: Dark text on light background or vice versa
  • Images: Use high-quality, relevant images (not clip art)
  • Charts: Use bar charts for comparison, pie charts for proportions, line charts for trends
  • Consistency: Same font, color scheme, and layout throughout

Point 4: Delivery Skills

🎤 Voice
  • Volume: Loud enough for back row
  • Pace: 130-150 words per minute
  • Pause: 3-5 seconds for emphasis
  • Variety: Change tone to maintain interest
💪 Body Language
  • Posture: Stand straight, shoulders back
  • Gestures: Open palms, purposeful hand movements
  • Eye Contact: 3-5 seconds per person/section
  • Movement: Walk purposefully, don't pace

Point 5: Handling Q&A and Nervousness

  • Before Q&A: Anticipate likely questions, prepare answers
  • During Q&A: Listen completely, pause before answering, be honest if you don't know
  • Managing Nerves: Deep breathing, positive visualization, arrive early to test equipment
  • Power Pose: Stand in a confident pose for 2 minutes before presenting (Amy Cuddy research)
🧠 Mnemonic

"SOAP" for presentation delivery — Smile, Open posture, Audience eye contact, Pause for effect.

✅ Unit 8 Summary

  • Visual aids: 6x6 rule, min 24pt font, high contrast, relevant images, consistent design
  • Delivery: Voice (volume, pace 130-150 wpm, pause, variety) + Body (posture, gestures, eye contact)
  • Mnemonic: SOAP — Smile, Open posture, Audience eye contact, Pause for effect

Point 3: Principles of Good Writing

  • Use Active Voice: "We completed the project" (active) vs "The project was completed" (passive)
  • Short Paragraphs: 3-5 sentences maximum
  • Simple Words: "Use" not "utilize"; "start" not "commence"
  • Be Specific: "Submit by 5 PM Friday" not "Submit soon"
  • One Idea Per Sentence: Avoid compound-complex sentences
  • Use Bullet Points: For lists of 3+ items

Point 4: Direct vs Indirect Approach

✅ Direct Approach

Use when: Good news, routine requests, positive responses

Structure: Main point → Details → Courteous close

Example: "Your leave has been approved for Dec 20-25."

⚠ Indirect Approach

Use when: Bad news, sensitive topics, persuasive messages

Structure: Buffer → Reasons → Bad news → Positive close

Example: "We appreciate your application. Due to current requirements..."

Point 5: Editing and Proofreading Checklist

  1. Have I answered What, Why, When, How?
  2. Is the most important information first?
  3. Are sentences concise (under 20 words average)?
  4. Is the tone appropriate for the audience?
  5. Are there spelling or grammatical errors?
  6. Is the formatting consistent?
  7. Have I included a clear call to action?
🧠 Mnemonic

"WRITE" for good business writing — Who is the audience? Reason for writing? Information to include? Tone to use? Error-free?

✅ Unit 9 Summary

  • 5 C’s of business writing: Clarity, Conciseness, Completeness, Correctness, Courtesy
  • Planning: Purpose, Audience, Content, Structure (direct/indirect), Channel
  • Active voice, short paragraphs (3-5 sentences), simple words, specific, one idea per sentence
  • Direct approach: good news → Main point → Details → Close; Indirect: buffer → reasons → bad news → positive
  • Mnemonic: WRITE — Who, Reason, Information, Tone, Error-free

Point 2: High-Context vs Low-Context Cultures

🌴 High-Context
  • Meaning is in context, not just words
  • Non-verbal cues are critical
  • Indirect communication preferred
  • Relationships before business
  • Examples: Japan, China, India, Arab countries
📄 Low-Context
  • Meaning is explicit in words
  • Direct, clear communication
  • Written contracts are binding
  • Business before relationships
  • Examples: USA, Germany, Scandinavia

Point 3: Communication Style Differences

  • Direct vs Indirect: Germans say "No" directly; Japanese say "That would be difficult"
  • Formal vs Informal: Use titles in Germany/Japan; first names in Australia/USA
  • Silence: Comfortable in Japan/Finland; uncomfortable in USA/Brazil
  • Gesture: "OK" sign is positive in USA, offensive in Brazil, money in Japan
  • Time: Monochronic (one thing at a time — Germany) vs Polychronic (flexible — India, Latin America)

Point 4: Building Cross-Cultural Competence

  1. Cultural Self-Awareness: Understand your own cultural biases first
  2. Research: Learn about the other culture's norms before interacting
  3. Active Listening: Listen for meaning, not just words
  4. Flexibility: Adapt your style — don't expect others to adapt to you
  5. Patience: Allow extra time for cross-cultural understanding
  6. Avoid Stereotypes: Individuals vary within cultures

Point 5: Global Virtual Teams

  • Time Zones: Rotate meeting times to share inconvenience equally
  • Technology: Choose tools accessible to all (not everyone has Zoom)
  • Documentation: Write decisions down — verbal agreements get lost across cultures
  • Check Understanding: Ask "Can you summarize what we agreed?" instead of "Do you understand?"
  • Build Relationships: Start meetings with personal check-ins, not just business
💡 Indian Example

Indian IT companies (TCS, Infosys, Wipro) train employees in cross-cultural communication before sending them onsite. Key training: understanding American directness, Japanese formality, German punctuality, and Arab relationship-building preferences.

Point 6: Case Study — Tata-JLR Cross-Cultural Integration

📚 Case Study

Challenge: When Tata acquired Jaguar-Land Rover in 2008, they faced a massive cultural gap — Indian management style (hierarchical, relationship-driven) vs British workforce (egalitarian, task-driven).

Approach: Tata preserved JLR's British identity and management autonomy. Ratan Tata personally visited UK plants, listened to worker concerns, and respected existing communication norms. They didn't impose Indian corporate culture.

Lesson: Successful cross-cultural communication requires adaptation, not assimilation. Tata's respectful approach turned a potential cultural clash into a $3B+ success story. JLR became profitable within 3 years of acquisition.

⚠️ Exam Important

Edward T. Hall's Context Theory: High-context cultures (India, Japan, China) rely on implicit messages, relationships, and non-verbal cues. Low-context cultures (USA, Germany, Scandinavia) prefer explicit, direct, written communication. Misunderstanding this distinction is the #1 cause of cross-cultural business failures.

🧠 Mnemonic

"HALL" for cross-cultural success — High/Low context awareness, Adapt your style, Learn the culture, Listen actively. Remember: "Edward HALL opened the door to cultural understanding."

✅ Unit 14 Summary

  • Hofstede dimensions: Power Distance, Individualism, Uncertainty Avoidance, Long-Term Orientation
  • High-context (India, Japan) vs Low-context (USA, Germany): implicit vs explicit communication
  • Style differences: Direct/Indirect, Formal/Informal, Silence, Gestures, Monochronic/Polychronic time
  • Cross-cultural competence: Self-awareness, research, active listening, flexibility, patience, avoid stereotypes
  • Global virtual teams: Rotate time zones, accessible tools, written decisions, check understanding

Point 3: Privacy and Confidentiality

  • Personal Data: Protect employee and customer information (GDPR, IT Act 2000)
  • Trade Secrets: Safeguard proprietary information (NDAs, confidentiality agreements)
  • Whistleblowing: Ethical obligation to report wrongdoing (Companies Act 2013 provisions)
  • Informed Consent: Get permission before sharing someone's information

Point 4: Crisis Communication Ethics

  • Transparency: Share what you know, acknowledge what you don't
  • Timeliness: Communicate promptly — silence breeds speculation
  • Consistency: Unified message across all channels and spokespersons
  • Empathy: Acknowledge impact on affected parties
  • Accountability: Take responsibility; don't blame others
💡 Indian Example

Johnson & Johnson's Tylenol crisis (1982) is the gold standard of ethical crisis communication — immediate recall, full transparency, and putting consumer safety above profits. Contrast with Boeing's 737 MAX crisis where delayed and defensive communication worsened the situation.

Point 5: Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) in Communication

  • Stakeholder Engagement: Communicate with all affected parties, not just shareholders
  • Green Communication: Avoid "greenwashing" — environmental claims must be substantiated
  • Inclusive Language: Gender-neutral, non-discriminatory communication
  • Community Impact: Consider how communication affects local communities
⚠️ Exam Important

India's Companies Act 2013 mandates CSR spending (2% of average net profit) for qualifying companies. Ethical communication about CSR activities must be accurate, verifiable, and not misleading — violating this can attract SEBI penalties.

🧠 Mnemonic

"TRRF-T" for ethical communication principles — Truthfulness, Respect, Responsibility, Fairness, Transparency.
