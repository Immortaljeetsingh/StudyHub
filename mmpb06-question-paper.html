Q1. Define corporate governance. Explain the agency theory and the three types of agency costs with suitable examples.

Answer: Corporate governance is the system of rules, practices and processes by which a company is directed and controlled, balancing the interests of all its stakeholders — and agency theory explains the conflict that arises when owners separate from managers.

Corporate governance is the system of rules, practices, and processes by which a company is directed and controlled. It balances the interests of the company's many stakeholders — shareholders, management, customers, suppliers, financiers, government, and the community — and its principles are Transparency, Accountability, Fairness, and Responsibility (mnemonic T-A-F-R).

Agency theory is the foundation of corporate governance. It arises from the separation of ownership and control: shareholders (principals) own the company, but professional managers (agents) run it. Because agents may act in their own interest rather than the shareholders' interest, the agency problem emerges. This is why boards of directors exist — to provide oversight, strategy direction, and accountability. Agency costs are the costs of this conflict and come in three forms (mnemonic M-B-R): Monitoring costs — expenses shareholders incur to supervise management, such as audit fees, board meeting costs, and reporting requirements. Bonding costs — expenses management incurs to assure shareholders they are acting in their interest, such as voluntary disclosures and subscribing to governance codes. Residual loss — the inefficiency that remains even after monitoring and bonding, caused by information asymmetry. Example: a large bank pays for external audits and independent directors (monitoring), publishes quarterly results voluntarily (bonding), yet some managerial self-dealing may still escape detection (residual loss). Infosys is often cited as a well-governed company with a majority of independent directors and separate Chairman and CEO roles, which keeps agency costs low.

Key points: Corporate governance = rules that direct and control companies (T-A-F-R); agency theory = conflict between owners and managers; agency costs = Monitoring + Bonding + Residual loss (M-B-R), reduced by strong boards and independent directors.

Takeaway: Open with the T-A-F-R definition of corporate governance, then present agency theory as the owner–manager conflict and list the three agency costs (M-B-R) with an example.

Q2. What is the Basel framework? Explain the three pillars of Basel II and the key capital requirements introduced by Basel III.

Answer: The Basel framework is the global rulebook of bank capital issued by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision — from Basel I's 8% CAR to Basel II's three pillars and Basel III's tougher post-2008 ratios.

The Basel framework is a set of international banking regulations issued by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) to ensure banks hold enough capital to absorb losses and remain stable. It evolved in three stages: Basel I (1988) focused on credit risk and set an 8% Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR); Basel II (2004) introduced a more risk-sensitive framework; and Basel III (2010) strengthened capital and liquidity after the 2008 financial crisis. The Capital Adequacy Ratio is the ratio of a bank's capital to its Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA) — CAR = (Tier 1 + Tier 2 capital) / RWA ≥ 8%.

Basel II rests on three pillars (mnemonic M-S-M). Pillar 1: Minimum Capital Requirements — banks must hold capital against credit, market, and operational risk, with CAR of at least 8%. Pillar 2: Supervisory Review — regulators assess each bank's internal capital assessment and take action where risks are underestimated. Pillar 3: Market Discipline — banks must publicly disclose their risk profile, capital adequacy, and risk management practices so markets can discipline them. Basel III added higher quality capital: Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) of 4.5%, Tier 1 of 6%, total CAR of 8%, plus a 2.5% Capital Conservation Buffer, a countercyclical buffer of 0-2.5%, a 3% leverage ratio, and a 100% Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR). Example: if a bank has Tier 1 of Rs 80,000 crore, Tier 2 of Rs 20,000 crore, and RWA of Rs 10,00,000 crore, its CAR = 10%, comfortably above the 8% minimum. SBI maintains a CAR of about 13-14%, well above the Basel III requirement of 10.5% (8% + 2.5% buffer).

Key points: Basel framework = global bank capital rules (Basel I: 8% CAR, Basel II: three pillars, Basel III: post-2008 strengthening); pillars are Minimum capital, Supervisory review, Market discipline (M-S-M); CAR = (Tier 1 + Tier 2)/RWA ≥ 8%.

Takeaway: Memorise the three pillars (M-S-M) and the Basel III numbers — CET1 4.5%, Tier 1 6%, CAR 8% plus a 2.5% buffer — and prove the CAR formula on one worked example.

Q3. Discuss the role of the RBI in the corporate governance of Indian banks. Explain the recommendations of the Narasimham and P.J. Nayak committees.

Answer: The RBI is the licensor, supervisor, regulator, resolver and consumer-protector of Indian banks, and the Narasimham and P.J. Nayak committees reshaped the governance architecture those banks sit inside.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is the regulator and supervisor of Indian banks, and its role in corporate governance is fivefold. Licensing — it grants banking licences and approves new bank formation. Supervision — it conducts on-site inspections and off-site monitoring of banks. Regulation — it sets capital adequacy norms, exposure limits, and priority sector lending requirements. Resolution — it applies the Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework, amalgamations, and restructuring for weak banks. Consumer protection — it runs the Banking Ombudsman scheme for grievance redressal. RBI also prescribes board composition norms, risk committees, and disclosure requirements that form the governance architecture of banks.

Two committees shaped Indian banking governance. The Narasimham Committee I (1991) recommended banking reforms: NPA recognition norms, capital adequacy requirements, and phased lending reforms. Narasimham Committee II (1998) focused on risk management, prudential norms, and a stronger supervisory framework. The P.J. Nayak Committee (2014) examined bank board governance and recommended a more professional board composition for public sector banks, reduction of government stake, and governance reforms to insulate boards from political and bureaucratic interference. Indian banks also manage four major risk types (mnemonic C-M-O-L): Credit risk (borrower default, seen in NPAs), Market risk (losses from interest rate or forex changes), Operational risk (fraud and system failures), and Liquidity risk (inability to meet short-term obligations). Example: ICICI Bank's governance structure — separate Chairman and MD roles, majority independent directors, and dedicated risk and audit committees — reflects the RBI's governance framework in practice.

Key points: RBI governs banks through licensing, supervision, regulation, resolution, and consumer protection; Narasimham I & II set NPA norms and risk frameworks; P.J. Nayak (2014) pushed board professionalism; bank risks are Credit, Market, Operational, Liquidity (C-M-O-L).

Takeaway: Say "licence, supervise, regulate, resolve, protect" for the RBI, then give one line each for Narasimham I & II and Nayak (2014), and close with the C-M-O-L risk types.

Q4. What is an NPA? Explain the asset classification norms for bank loans and discuss the recovery mechanisms available to banks in India.

Answer: A Non-Performing Asset is any loan overdue by 90 days or more, and Indian banks classify such assets on the S-S-D-L ladder with rising provisioning, then recover through SARFAESI, DRT and IBC.

A Non-Performing Asset (NPA) is a loan whose interest or principal payment is overdue for 90 days or more. NPAs are a core indicator of bank asset quality and governance, and banks are required to classify and provision for them transparently. RBI's CRILC (Central Repository of Information on Large Credits) database tracks borrowers with exposure of Rs 5 crore and above to detect stress early.

Asset classification follows the mnemonic S-S-D-L. Standard assets: regular payments (1-90 days overdue), with 0-10% provisioning. Sub-standard assets: overdue for 90 days to 12 months, with 15-25% provisioning. Doubtful assets: sub-standard for 12+ months, split into D1 (25%), D2 (40%), and D3 (100%) provisioning as the default persists. Loss assets: identified as uncollectible, with 100% provisioning and write-off. Banks manage NPAs through three lines of defence: Prevention — better credit appraisal, monitoring, and early warning systems; Resolution — Corporate Debt Restructuring (CDR), Strategic Debt Restructuring (SDR), and the Scheme for Sustainable Structuring (S4A); and Recovery — using legal mechanisms: the SARFAESI Act (2002), which lets banks seize and sell collateral without court intervention; Debt Recovery Tribunals (DRTs); and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC, 2016), which provides time-bound resolution in 180+90 days. Example: Indian banks' NPAs peaked at about 11.5% in 2018, prompting RBI's Asset Quality Review and stricter provisioning norms, which cleaned up balance sheets and strengthened governance.

Key points: NPA = loan overdue 90+ days; classification is Standard, Sub-standard, Doubtful, Loss (S-S-D-L) with rising provisioning; recovery uses SARFAESI, DRT, and IBC; CRILC tracks large exposures to catch stress early.

Takeaway: Define NPA as 90+ days overdue, walk the S-S-D-L ladder with its provisioning percentages, then name SARFAESI, DRT and IBC as the recovery toolkit.

Q5. Compare the corporate governance of NBFCs with commercial banks. Discuss the governance challenges faced by NBFCs, with reference to the IL&FS crisis.

Answer: An NBFC lends and invests like a bank but without demand deposits, deposit insurance, payment-system access or CRR/SLR — and the IL&FS crisis showed how thin that safety net makes its governance.

An NBFC (Non-Banking Financial Company) is a financial company registered under the Companies Act that provides loans and other financial services, but it differs from a commercial bank in key ways (mnemonic N-B-D-P): it cannot accept demand deposits; it has no deposit insurance cover (no DICGC); it has limited access to the payment system; and it is not required to maintain CRR/SLR. NBFCs are regulated under the RBI Act and Companies Act, while banks are regulated under the Banking Regulation Act. A Systemically Important NBFC is one with asset size above Rs 500 crore, attracting stricter RBI norms, while a Core Investment Company (CIC) holds 90%+ of its assets in group investments.

Governance challenges in NBFCs include lighter regulation compared to banks, liquidity risk, related-party transactions, and concentration risk in specific sectors. The IL&FS crisis (2018) is the defining example: IL&FS, a systemically important NBFC, faced a liquidity crunch when its short-term debt became due but long-term infrastructure assets could not be sold, triggering defaults that shook the mutual fund and money market system. The crisis revealed governance failures — weak board oversight, excessive group interconnections, and mismatched assets and liabilities. The consequences included tighter RBI governance norms for NBFCs: higher capital adequacy, board reforms, exposure limits, and enhanced disclosure requirements. Comparison example: Bajaj Finance (an NBFC) can lend to consumers but cannot accept demand deposits or offer deposit insurance like HDFC Bank can. Governance quality in NBFCs is therefore critical precisely because the regulatory safety net is thinner than for banks.

Key points: NBFCs differ from banks: no demand deposits, no DICGC, no payment system access, partial regulation (N-B-D-P); the IL&FS crisis (2018) exposed liquidity and governance failures, leading to stricter RBI norms for systemically important NBFCs (asset size > Rs 500 crore).

Takeaway: Open with the N-B-D-P differences, then narrate the IL&FS 2018 liquidity and governance failures and the stricter RBI norms that followed for systemically important NBFCs.

Q6. Explain the structure of mutual funds in India (Sponsor - Trust - AMC - Custodian) and the role of the trustee in protecting unit-holder interests.

Answer: An Indian mutual fund runs as a Sponsor – Trust – Trustee – AMC – Custodian chain under SEBI, with trustees supervising the AMC to protect unit holders.

A mutual fund is a pool of money collected from many investors and managed by an Asset Management Company (AMC) to invest in stocks, bonds, and other securities. The governance structure in India is a four-part chain (mnemonic T-A-C): Sponsor — the entity that establishes the mutual fund, sets up the trust, and appoints trustees; Trust — the legal owner of the fund's assets, holding them for the benefit of unit holders; Trustee — the oversight body that ensures the AMC acts in the investors' interest; AMC — the company that makes investment decisions and runs operations; plus a Custodian — who holds the securities in safe custody and settles transactions. This structure is regulated under the SEBI (Mutual Fund) Regulations, 1996.

The trustee's role is the heart of mutual fund governance. Trustees do not manage money; they supervise the AMC. Their responsibilities include monitoring that the AMC complies with the scheme objectives and SEBI regulations, reviewing related-party transactions, safeguarding that fund assets are not misused, and protecting unit-holder interests, including ensuring accurate Net Asset Value calculation. NAV (Net Asset Value) is the price of one unit, computed as NAV = (Total Assets − Total Liabilities) / Total Outstanding Units. Example: if a fund has Rs 1,000 crore in assets, Rs 50 crore in liabilities, and 50 crore units, NAV = (1000 − 50)/50 = Rs 19 per unit. SBI Mutual Fund's trustee company oversees its AMC exactly this way — an independent layer that keeps the fund manager honest. SEBI's classification of funds (equity, debt, hybrid, index, and ELSS) further protects investors by making risk levels transparent, so trustees can verify that the fund stays within its declared mandate.

Key points: Mutual fund structure = Sponsor → Trust → Trustee → AMC → Custodian (T-A-C), regulated by SEBI (1996); trustees protect unit holders by supervising the AMC; NAV = (Assets − Liabilities)/Units.

Takeaway: Draw the Sponsor → Trust → Trustee → AMC → Custodian chain, make the trustee the hero who supervises the AMC, and finish with the NAV formula.
