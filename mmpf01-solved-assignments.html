Q1. Distinguish between gross working capital and net working capital. Explain permanent and temporary working capital with examples. (10 Marks)

Answer: Gross Working Capital is the total investment in current assets - cash, inventory, receivables and marketable securities. It is an asset-side concept used in the modern corporate finance perspective and indicates the total funds tied up in short-term operations. Net Working Capital is the excess of current assets over current liabilities: NWC = Current Assets - Current Liabilities. It is an accounting-based liquidity measure: positive NWC means the firm can meet its short-term obligations and provides a cushion to creditors. For example, a firm with ₹50 lakh current assets and ₹30 lakh current liabilities has gross working capital of ₹50 lakh but net working capital of only ₹20 lakh. Working capital is also divided by behaviour into permanent and temporary components. Permanent working capital is the minimum level of current assets needed to run the business throughout the year regardless of fluctuations - for example, a garment manufacturer must always hold raw material stock. Temporary working capital is the additional requirement during seasonal or peak periods, such as the festival-season surge in demand for Amul's dairy products. This distinction drives the financing decision: permanent WC should be financed from long-term sources, while temporary WC can be met with short-term borrowing such as cash credit or overdraft.

Key points:

  • Gross Working Capital is the total investment in current assets - cash, inventory, receivables and marketable securities.
  • For example, a firm with ₹50 lakh current assets and ₹30 lakh current liabilities has gross working capital of ₹50 lakh but net working capital of only ₹20 lakh.
  • It is an asset-side concept used in the modern corporate finance perspective and indicates the total funds tied up in short-term operations.

Takeaway: This distinction drives the financing decision: permanent WC should be financed from long-term sources, while temporary WC can be met with short-term borrowing such as cash credit or overdraft.

Q2. Define the cash conversion cycle. Explain its components and the ways in which a firm can shorten it. (10 Marks)

Answer: The Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) is the net time period during which a firm's cash remains tied up in operating activities, from payment for raw materials to collection from customers. The operating cycle runs Cash - Raw Materials - Work-in-Progress - Finished Goods - Receivables - Cash and equals Inventory Period plus Receivables Period. The CCC adjusts for supplier credit: CCC = Inventory Days + Receivable Days - Payable Days, where inventory days = 365/Inventory Turnover, receivable days = 365/Receivables Turnover and payable days = 365/Payables Turnover. A shorter cycle means faster cash recovery and a lower working capital requirement; a longer cycle locks up funds. A firm can shorten its CCC by adopting just-in-time inventory and faster production, accelerating collections through digital payments such as UPI, tightening credit terms, offering discounts for early payment and negotiating longer supplier payment terms. Amazon India collects instantly from customers through digital payments but pays suppliers in 60-90 days, creating a negative CCC - effectively using supplier money to fund operations; Flipkart, Zomato and Swiggy follow similar models. Reliance Industries reduced its CCC from 45 days to 30 days through JIT retail inventory, digital collections and negotiated 60-90 day payables, saving thousands of crores annually. Industry benchmarks vary: FMCG 20-30 days, manufacturing 40-60 days, infrastructure 90-120 days.

Key points:

  • The Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) is the net time period during which a firm's cash remains tied up in operating activities, from payment for raw materials to collection from customers.
  • A firm can shorten its CCC by adopting just-in-time inventory and faster production, accelerating collections through digital payments such as UPI, tightening credit terms, offering discounts for early payment and negotiating longer supplier payment terms.
  • The operating cycle runs Cash - Raw Materials - Work-in-Progress - Finished Goods - Receivables - Cash and equals Inventory Period plus Receivables Period.

Q3. Discuss the factors that determine the working capital requirements of a firm. (10 Marks)

Answer: Working capital requirements are determined by a combination of internal and external factors. The nature of business is foremost: manufacturing firms need more working capital than service firms, while infrastructure firms need the most because clients such as government agencies delay payments for 90-120 days. HUL needs little working capital because consumers pay immediately, whereas L&T needs high working capital for the same reason. Production policy matters too: level production gives steady needs, seasonal production gives fluctuating needs, and JIT production lowers requirements. The credit policy is decisive - a liberal credit policy increases receivables and hence working capital, while strict credit terms reduce it. Growth rate matters: expanding businesses need proportionately more working capital to finance rising stocks and debtors. Operating efficiency reduces requirements, as faster turnover of inventory and receivables releases cash. Among external factors, inflation increases working capital needs as raw material and other costs rise; interest rates determine the cost of borrowing; competition forces liberal credit; and technology, such as UPI and digital collections, reduces requirements. Estimation methods include the percentage-of-sales method, the operating cycle method (daily expenses x operating cycle days) and the balance sheet method. Remember the mnemonic NPC GOI - Nature, Production, Credit, Growth, Operating efficiency, Inflation - and always classify factors as internal or external.

Key points:

  • Working capital requirements are determined by a combination of internal and external factors.
  • The nature of business is foremost: manufacturing firms need more working capital than service firms, while infrastructure firms need the most because clients such as government agencies delay payments for 90-120 days.
  • HUL needs little working capital because consumers pay immediately, whereas L&T needs high working capital for the same reason.

Takeaway: Remember the mnemonic NPC GOI - Nature, Production, Credit, Growth, Operating efficiency, Inflation - and always classify factors as internal or external.

Q4. What is cash credit? Discuss the methods of bank finance for working capital and the Tandon Committee norms. (10 Marks)

Answer: Cash credit is the most common working capital facility in India: a bank sanctions a limit against the security of current assets such as inventory and debtors, and the borrower draws, repays and re-borrows up to that limit as needed, paying interest only on the amount used. Other methods of bank finance include: overdraft, which allows drawing beyond the account balance up to a sanctioned limit, usually unsecured and used for short-term gaps; term loans with fixed repayment schedules for specific needs; packing credit, which is pre-shipment finance for exporters; and bill discounting, where trade bills are converted into immediate cash. The Working Capital Gap is Current Assets minus Current Liabilities other than bank borrowings. The Tandon Committee (1975) recommended that banks finance up to 40% of this gap, ensuring the borrower contributes its own funds. Under RBI norms, working capital finance should not exceed 40% of the borrower's projected turnover; typically the first 25% of the gap comes from long-term sources and the remaining 75% from bank finance. Cash credit is preferred for its high flexibility, while overdraft limits are usually lower than cash credit limits. Remember the mnemonic COTPB - Cash credit, Overdraft, Term loan, Packing credit, Bill discounting.

Key points:

  • Cash credit is the most common working capital facility in India: a bank sanctions a limit against the security of current assets such as inventory and debtors, and the borrower draws, repays and re-borrows up to that limit as needed, paying interest only on the amount used.
  • Other methods of bank finance include: overdraft, which allows drawing beyond the account balance up to a sanctioned limit, usually unsecured and used for short-term gaps; term loans with fixed repayment schedules for specific needs; packing credit, which is pre-shipment finance for exporters; and bill discounting, where trade bills are converted into immediate cash.
