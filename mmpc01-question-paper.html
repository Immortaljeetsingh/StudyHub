During this period the bank recorded continuous business growth and by 1997, the bank's total business stood at 12,000 crores. Arvind Jain was a fiery young man who essentially believed in turnaround performance. His style of leadership was autocratic and he believed that people around him should be committed to executing his orders rather than wasting time on debates and discussions. He formed a core group of top executives to strategize and monitor the implementation of action plans. Being a traditional bank where hierarchy and authority were respected, it was not long before everyone adjusted to the new style of functioning. Everybody from the branch offices, regional offices, and the head office, religiously followed the orders of the top management. The result was a stupendous success. The bank became a force to reckon with among the private sector banks in southern India.

Arvind Jain emphasized the following aspects:

• Recruiting top-notch professionals and re-engineering the corporate brand of the bank emphasizing marketing and business development.
• A top-down approach in the decision-making process.
• Adoption of technology for modernizing business operations.

Along with the positive developments were a few negative aspects:

• Centralization of the bank's functioning.
• Formation of a coterie which wielded power in the bank.
• Emphasis on performance at any cost rather than on means.
• Frustration and disillusionment of the employees at large.

Parallel with these developments, there were other developments too in the bank. Differences arose between the promoter Sudhakar Gupta and the MD Arvind Jain, which eventually led to the resignation and exit of the latter from the bank. A few of his faithful followers too exited from the bank. • Emphasis on streamlining systems and procedures.
• Nurturing employees to strictly adhere to laid-down norms/systems.
• Training of existing employees in core areas such as credit, audit etc., recruitment of young professionals, i.e., MBA, M.Com., etc. as management trainees and their induction into the bank to bring in fresh blood and enthusiasm.

• Posting of successful line personnel as faculty in Staff Training Colleges to drive home the importance of training to the employees of the bank.

Q1. Different roles a manager performs and their dynamics in the present day context (20 Marks)

Answer: Mintzberg watched real managers at work and concluded they perform 10 roles in 3 categories — interpersonal, informational and decisional — switching between them all day.

Key points:
  • Interpersonal roles (dealing with people): Figurehead (representing the organisation at ceremonies), Leader (motivating and guiding subordinates), Liaison (connecting with people outside the unit).
  • Informational roles (dealing with information): Monitor (scanning for information), Disseminator (sharing information inside), Spokesperson (speaking for the organisation outside).
  • Decisional roles (dealing with decisions): Entrepreneur (initiating change), Disturbance handler (handling crises), Resource allocator (dividing money, time, people), Negotiator (settling agreements).
  • Role dynamics: roles are not separate boxes — authority and status give the manager information, and information makes decision-making possible, so the three groups are interlinked; managers switch roles many times a day.
  • Level dynamics: the role mix changes with level — a front-line supervisor plays the leader role most, while a CEO plays figurehead, spokesperson and negotiator more often.
  • Present-day dynamics: virtual and hybrid teams, email and video-call communication, data-driven decisions, digital change, plus new duties — employee wellbeing, work-life balance and ESG reporting.
  • Example: a bank branch manager uses the leader role to guide tellers, the monitor role to read daily reports, the spokesperson role to answer customers, and the disturbance handler role during a technical outage of the banking app.

Takeaway: memorise 3 groups and 10 roles, then show the authority-information-decision link and a digital-age example.

Q2. Approaches to organizing and analyzing work and their relevance (20 Marks)

Answer: Organizing groups activities, assigns duties and creates authority relationships — and five approaches, from Taylor to contingency, show how work can be organised and analysed.

Key points:
  • Scientific management (F.W. Taylor): analyse each job into small tasks with time-and-motion studies to find the "one best way"; relevance — process standardisation and assembly lines; example — Maruti Suzuki's factory floor follows standard operating procedures for every task.
  • Administrative theory (Henri Fayol): organise the whole organisation using principles — division of work, unity of command, scalar chain, span of control, authority equal to responsibility; relevance — formal hierarchy and clear reporting lines in every company.
  • Bureaucratic approach (Max Weber): organise through rules, hierarchy, impersonal decisions and written records; relevance — government departments and banks where consistency and fairness matter.
  • Systems approach: organisation as an open system of interrelated parts (inputs, processes, outputs, feedback) interacting with the environment; relevance — managers see how one department's change affects the whole firm.
  • Contingency approach: no one best structure — the right way depends on strategy, size, technology and environment; relevance — a startup stays flat and flexible, a large bank needs formal controls and many levels.
  • Work analysis tools: job analysis (breaking a job into tasks, duties and responsibilities), work simplification, and value chain analysis (identifying activities that add value to customers).
  • Example: Zomato organises delivery, dining and hyperpure supply into separate divisions (departmentalization) but keeps a common technology platform — division of work and coordination working together.

Takeaway: sequence the five approaches, attach one thinker and one relevance point to each, and close with a departmentalization example.

Q3. Staffing: meaning and importance + sources of recruitment (20 Marks)

Answer: Staffing fills and keeps filled the positions in the organisation — the right person in the right job at the right time — through recruitment, selection and development.

Key points:
  • Scope: staffing covers manpower planning, job analysis, recruitment, selection, placement, induction, training, performance appraisal, promotion and career development.
  • Importance — productivity: right people raise productivity and quality of work.
  • Importance — cost: effective staffing reduces recruitment and training costs, and lowers turnover.
  • Importance — advantage: skilled people give a competitive advantage that machines cannot copy; fair selection builds satisfaction; succession planning ensures continuity when leaders leave.
  • Internal sources: promotion, transfer, internal job postings, employee referrals — advantages: cheap, fast, morale booster, the person is already known; disadvantages: limited talent pool, inbreeding (no new ideas), vacancy chains, jealousy among colleagues.
  • External sources: advertisements, campus placements, employment exchanges, consultants and head-hunters, job portals like Naukri and LinkedIn, walk-in interviews — advantages: wider choice, fresh ideas and experience, reduces internal rivalry; disadvantages: costly and slow, higher risk of wrong choice, longer induction, may demotivate internal candidates.
  • Example: a software company promotes team leads from within (internal) but hires freshers through campus placements and senior experts through consultants (external).

Takeaway: split the answer into staffing scope, its four importance points, and the internal-external sources trade-off with one example.

Q4. Role of communication in the organization, barriers, and how to minimize them (20 Marks)

Answer: Communication is the lifeline of the organisation — it carries decisions down, feedback up and coordination sideways — and its barriers can be minimised systematically.

Key points:
  • Downward: carries decisions and instructions from top to bottom.
  • Upward: carries feedback, reports and suggestions from workers to management.
  • Lateral and diagonal: coordinates work between departments; the informal grapevine spreads information quickly, though not always accurately.
  • Culture and trust: good communication builds trust, motivation and a strong organisational culture.
  • Semantic barriers: jargon, technical words and unclear language — example: a manager telling a worker to "optimise the KPI" instead of explaining in simple words.
  • Psychological barriers: perception, emotions, status differences, filtering (people pass on only what suits them) and lack of attention.
  • Organisational and physical barriers: too many hierarchy levels, information overload, unclear authority, noise, distance and poor technology.
  • Minimizing: simple clear language; active listening and asking for feedback; the right channel (email for records, face-to-face for sensitive matters); fewer hierarchy levels and an open-door policy; empathy and awareness of receiver perceptions.
  • Example: a salary-freeze circular spread rumours; CEO town-hall meetings with question-answer sessions brought calm acceptance.

Takeaway: cover the four directions, the four barrier families, five remedies, and land the town-hall example.

Q5. Short notes (write any three) (20 Marks)

Answer (a) Organizational culture: Culture is the set of shared values, beliefs, assumptions and behavioural patterns that distinguish one organisation from another.

Key points:
  • Three levels: visible artifacts (dress code, offices, rituals), values (what people say they believe) and basic assumptions (deeply held beliefs).
  • Functions: identity, commitment and stability; helps new employees understand "how things are done here".
  • Formation: shaped by founders, kept alive by stories, symbols and rituals, and taught to newcomers through socialisation.
  • Example: Tata's culture of ethics and trust and Infosys's culture of learning and integrity are quoted as competitive advantages.

Answer (b) Turnaround management: Turnaround is the process of reviving an organisation that is sick, loss-making or declining.

Key points:
  • Diagnose: identify causes — bad strategy, high costs, weak leadership or obsolete products.
  • Retrench: cut costs, sell unused assets, close weak divisions and downsize.
  • Reposition: launch new products, enter new markets, rebuild the brand; then stabilise with control systems.
  • Success factors: a strong change leader, quick decisions and honest communication; example — the revival of Air India after privatisation, and Maruti's turnaround in the 1980s.

Answer (c) Line and staff: Line authority commands along the chain of command; staff authority advises and supports.

Key points:
  • Line managers: production, sales and operations — they decide and command.
  • Staff managers: HR, legal, finance-advising specialists help line managers but cannot directly command them.
  • Conflict: the plant manager (line) decides production targets while HR (staff) advises on hiring policy — staff recommend, line carries accountability.
  • Balance: clear authority definitions and mutual respect prevent friction.

Answer (d) Brainstorming: Brainstorming is Alex Osborn's group technique for generating creative ideas without premature criticism.

Key points:
  • Rules: no criticism during generation, freewheeling (wild ideas allowed), quantity over quality, and building on others' ideas (piggybacking).
  • Process: define the problem, gather a small group, generate ideas rapidly, record all ideas, then evaluate and shortlist.
  • Example: an advertising agency brainstorms taglines for a new cola brand and picks the best from a long list.
  • Limitations: one dominant member may control the group; some members may sit back (social loafing).

Answer (e) Managerial grid theory: Blake and Mouton's grid measures leadership on concern for people and concern for production, each on a 1–9 scale.

Key points:
  • Five main styles: 1,1 impoverished (minimum effort); 9,1 task/authority-compliance; 1,9 country club; 5,5 middle-of-the-road; and 9,9 team management (high on both — considered the best).
  • Example: leaders who involve teams in goal-setting while demanding high performance, like Narayana Murthy in early Infosys, reflect the 9,9 style.
  • Use: leadership training and self-improvement — the grid makes style visible and changeable.

Takeaway: each note is definition + 3–4 distinguishing points + an example — the grid, culture and turnaround notes often recur across years.

Q6. Case study: Modern Bank Limited (40 Marks)

Answer (a): The core issues are autocratic leadership and centralization, failed leadership transition, poor change management, culture clash and erosion of employee trust.

Key points:
  • Autocratic leadership and centralization: Arvind Jain ran the bank top-down, a small coterie wielded power, and decision-making was fully centralised; employees were expected to obey, not discuss.
  • Performance at any cost: the bank emphasised results over means, creating frustration and disillusionment among employees at large.
  • Failed leadership transition: when Manoj Pillai replaced the autocratic style with systems-based, freedom-giving leadership, employees used to central command perceived him as weak; product innovation stopped and the bank recorded negative growth during 1997–2000.
  • Poor change management: the multinational takeover brought higher pay for new recruits, angering old employees; many left through VRS and the remaining staff was confused about direction.
  • Culture clash: the bank swung between extreme styles — autocratic, then system-driven, then transformation-driven — without a consistent culture, hurting morale and performance.

Answer (b): As the manager I would balance leadership styles, communicate the vision, and rebuild trust through training, fair rewards and innovation.

Key points:
  • Balance leadership styles: keep clear, decisive direction but involve top and middle managers through cross-functional teams and suggestion schemes.
  • Communicate the vision: town halls and personal meetings (like Vikrant Advani's) so everyone understands the transformation and their role in it.
  • Build knowledge management: intranet, e-learning, discussion forums and case studies from critical experience spread learning across the bank.
  • Invest in training and development: strengthen staff training colleges, induct young management trainees, post successful line personnel as faculty.
  • Fair reward management: align pay and incentives (CTC, performance-linked pay) transparently so old employees don't feel cheated versus new recruits.
  • Manage change with empathy: counselling and communication before VRS and restructuring; the remaining staff needs clarity, not confusion.
  • Restart product innovation: a core team of young professionals for re-branding and product re-engineering, powered by core banking, internet banking and call-centre technology.

Takeaway: both extreme styles failed — the model answer balances direction with participation and links every measure to the case facts (Jain, Pillai, Advani).

Read the full case: Rising absenteeism at a fast-food chain

A national fast-food chain introduced a new shift-rotation system across its outlets to match 24x7 delivery demand. Three months later, absenteeism across outlets rose from 4% to 14%, service scores fell sharply, and several crew members requested transfers. The store managers blamed "lazy" staff, while crew members complained that the new rosters ignored family commitments, the reason for the change was never explained, and suggestions were brushed aside. The HR head now wants to understand what went wrong and how to fix it.

Questions: (a) Diagnose the problem in the case. Which theories of motivation and change explain the employees' behaviour? (b) As the HR head, what measures would you adopt to resolve the situation?

Q1. Management: definition and functions (20 Marks)

Answer: Management is getting things done through and with people by performing five continuous, interlocking functions — planning, organizing, staffing, directing and controlling.

Key points:
  • Definition: management is the process of utilising human and material resources to achieve organisational objectives efficiently and effectively.
  • Planning: deciding in advance what to do, how to do it and who will do it — the base on which every other function rests.
  • Organizing: grouping activities, assigning duties, delegating authority and creating coordination between departments.
  • Staffing: putting the right person in the right job through recruitment, selection, training and development.
  • Directing: leading, motivating and communicating so that subordinates willingly perform their tasks.
  • Controlling: comparing actual performance with standards and taking corrective action when deviations are found.
  • Interrelated cycle: the functions are not watertight boxes — a revised plan triggers fresh organizing and staffing, and control feeds back into planning.

Takeaway: master the five functions as one interlocking cycle, because every management act combines them.

Q2. Evolution of management thought (20 Marks)

Answer: Management thought evolved in three broad waves — classical, behavioural and modern — each adding a new insight about work, people and the environment.

Key points:
  • Scientific management (Taylor): time-and-motion study, "one best way" of doing each task, scientific selection and differential piece-rate wages — efficiency on the shop floor.
  • Administrative theory (Fayol): 14 principles (division of work, unity of command, scalar chain, span of control) and the five functions — efficiency at the whole-organisation level.
  • Bureaucracy (Weber): rules, hierarchy, impersonality and written records — fair and consistent administration.
  • Behavioural wave: the Hawthorne studies (Mayo) proved that social factors and recognition affect productivity; human relations theory followed, then Maslow, Herzberg and McGregor.
  • Management science: operations research, decision models and MIS brought quantitative tools to decision-making.
  • Systems and contingency: the organisation is an open system of interdependent parts, and there is no one best way — the right approach depends on the situation.
  • Present day: digital-era management blends all waves — data-driven efficiency (classical) with purpose, wellbeing and agility (behavioural-contingency).

Takeaway: quote the sequence classical → behavioural → modern with one thinker per school and you have a full answer.

Q3. Decision-making process and types (20 Marks)

Answer: Decision-making is choosing the best course of action from alternatives, and it is the heart of every managerial function.

Key points:
  • Steps: define the problem → gather information → identify alternatives → evaluate alternatives → select the best → implement → review and learn.
  • Programmed decisions: routine and repetitive, solved by rules and standard procedures — e.g. sanctioning a standard loan.
  • Non-programmed decisions: unique and unstructured, needing judgement — e.g. entering a new market or launching a product.
  • Levels: strategic (top), tactical (middle) and operational (lower) decisions, each with a different horizon and risk.
  • Group vs individual: groups give more information and acceptance but are slower; individuals are faster but riskier.
  • Bounded rationality (Simon): managers rarely have perfect information, so they "satisfice" — choose the first acceptable option.
  • Barriers: information overload, personal bias, time pressure and fear of responsibility distort the process.

Takeaway: a structured process with a review loop turns guesswork into defensible decisions.

Q4. Communication process and the role of feedback (20 Marks)

Answer: Communication is the transfer of a message with shared meaning, and the process is complete only when the sender receives feedback confirming understanding.

Key points:
  • Elements: sender, encoding, message, channel, decoding, receiver, feedback and noise.
  • Steps: the sender converts an idea into words/symbols (encoding), transmits it through a channel, the receiver decodes it, and understanding is confirmed by feedback.
  • Channels: oral (meetings, phone), written (letters, circulars, email), and digital (chat, video calls); formal channels flow downward, upward and laterally.
  • Feedback: closes the loop — it lets the sender check meaning, correct misunderstandings and improve future messages; two-way communication is always safer.
  • Noise: physical (distance, poor network) and psychological (prejudice, emotions) interference that distorts the message.
  • Grapevine: the informal channel spreads news fast but unreliably — organisations must manage it with timely, official communication.
  • Example: a company announcing a pay policy by a single circular creates rumour; a town-hall with a question-answer session confirms understanding through immediate feedback.

Takeaway: always build feedback into communication — without it you have transmission, not communication.

Q5. Case study: Rising absenteeism at a fast-food chain (40 Marks)

Answer (a): The real problem is an imposed change — the new shift-rotation system — that broke work-life balance and motivation; the "lazy staff" label is only the visible symptom.

Key points:
  • Diagnosis: rosters ignored family commitments (work-life imbalance), the reason for the change was never explained, and suggestions were brushed aside — classic signs of poorly managed change.
  • Motivation theories: Herzberg — working conditions and security are hygiene factors whose loss creates dissatisfaction; Maslow — safety and belonging needs were threatened; absenteeism is withdrawal behaviour.
  • Change theories: Lewin — the change was imposed without unfreezing (creating readiness), so resistance surfaced as absenteeism; participation was missing at every stage.
  • Management failure: store managers blamed employees instead of diagnosing the system; one-way communication let the grapevine replace facts.

Answer (b): As HR head I would repair the change process with participation, communication and flexible design rather than more discipline.

Key points:
  • Participation: involve crew representatives in redesigning rosters — shifts balanced with family commitments and fair rotation.
  • Education and communication: explain why 24x7 operation demands rotation and how the new system protects fairness; hold outlet-level Q&A sessions.
  • Flexibility: offer choice-based rostering (self-scheduling windows), swap systems and predictable weekly patterns.
  • Support: counselling for stressed staff, an employee suggestion scheme with quick responses, and recognition for reliable attendance (Herzberg's motivators).
  • Supervision: retrain store managers in change management and people skills; add an attendance pulse dashboard for early warning.
  • Pilot and review: test the new roster in two outlets for three months, compare absenteeism and service scores, then roll out nationally with a review cycle.

Takeaway: participation and communication convert an imposed change into an accepted one — fix the system, not the people.

Read the full case: The open-office announcement

A software company decided to move to an open-office layout to encourage collaboration. The decision was announced through a single email, and the move was scheduled within six weeks. Within days, anxiety spread through the grapevine: employees feared loss of privacy, noise, and forced relocation of desks. Productivity dipped, two senior developers resigned, and a petition demanding a rethink gathered signatures. The CEO is surprised — the plan seemed good to him on paper.

Questions: (a) Identify the core issues in the case. (b) As the HR head, suggest measures to resolve the situation.

Q1. Organizing: concept, process and span of control (20 Marks)

Answer: Organizing is the function of creating the structure of tasks, authority and coordination through which plans are executed.

Key points:
  • Concept: grouping activities, assigning duties, delegating authority and creating relationships between positions so people work together for common goals.
  • Process: identify the activities needed to reach objectives → group them into departments (departmentalization) → assign duties → delegate authority → coordinate → review the structure periodically.
  • Departmentalization bases: function, product, territory and customer — e.g. a bank has branches by territory and divisions by product.
  • Span of control: the number of subordinates a manager can supervise effectively; narrow spans give tall structures, wide spans give flat structures.
  • Span determinants: task complexity, competence of subordinates, manager's capacity, and closeness of supervision needed.
  • Authority types: line (direct command), staff (advisory) and functional authority, balanced by delegation and accountability.

Takeaway: structure follows strategy — choose departments and spans to match the work, people and environment.

Q2. Staffing: meaning, steps and importance (20 Marks)

Answer: Staffing fills and keeps filled every position with competent people through a continuous pipeline of manpower planning, recruitment, selection and development.

Key points:
  • Steps: manpower planning → recruitment (attracting candidates) → selection (choosing the best) → placement and induction → training and development → performance appraisal → promotion and career planning.
  • Recruitment vs selection: recruitment is a positive process that widens the applicant pool; selection is a negative process that rejects unsuitable candidates.
  • Importance: competent people raise productivity and quality; staffing creates a competitive advantage that machines cannot copy.
  • Cost and continuity: good staffing reduces recruitment and training costs, lowers turnover, and builds succession pipelines for leadership continuity.
  • Satisfaction: fair selection and visible career paths motivate employees and strengthen employer branding (Tata, Infosys attract talent on this reputation).
  • Modern tools: psychometric tests, AI-based screening, structured interviews and digital onboarding platforms make the process faster and fairer.

Takeaway: staffing is a cycle, not an event — appraisal data must feed back into manpower planning.

Q3. Controlling: importance and process (20 Marks)

Answer: Controlling ensures that actual results match planned standards and converts deviations into corrective action.

Key points:
  • Importance: it completes the management cycle, detects deviations early, protects resources, and sets standards for the future.
  • Process: establish standards → measure actual performance → compare with standards → analyse deviations → take corrective action.
  • Standards: must be clear, measurable and linked to objectives — e.g. sales targets, quality levels, cost budgets, delivery times.
  • Key principle: management by exception — focus attention only on significant deviations instead of checking everything.
  • Essentials of a good system: timely, accurate, flexible, economical, understandable and focused on critical points.
  • Techniques: budgetary control, break-even analysis, PERT/CPM, standard costing, MIS dashboards and internal audit.
  • Example: an e-commerce firm's dashboard flags a 20% rise in delivery delays by region, and the logistics team is redirected the same day.

Takeaway: control without corrective action is only measurement — always close the loop.

Q4. Forces for change and the change management process (20 Marks)

Answer: Change is triggered by external and internal forces, and it succeeds only when managed as a structured, people-centred process.

Key points:
  • External forces: technology disruption, competition, regulatory changes, customer expectations and economic shifts (e.g. digital banking forced branch transformation).
  • Internal forces: new leadership, strategy change, obsolete processes, culture gaps, employee expectations and performance problems.
  • Process: recognise the need → diagnose the gap → plan the change → communicate and involve people → implement → reinforce and refreeze → evaluate.
  • Lewin's model: unfreezing (create readiness) → moving (implement) → refreezing (stabilise through rewards and habits).
  • Resistance: habit, fear of the unknown, loss of status, economic insecurity and group norms — individual and organisational.
  • Overcoming resistance: education and communication, participation, facilitation and support, negotiation, and only as a last resort coercion.
  • Example: banks moved employees from teller counters to digital desks by retraining (support) and involving them in rollout plans (participation).

Takeaway: change management is people management — involvement and communication decide whether change lands or fails.

Q5. Case study: The open-office announcement (40 Marks)

Answer (a): The core issues are one-way communication, absence of participation, and a change imposed without unfreezing — so anxiety and rumour filled the vacuum.

Key points:
  • Communication failure: a single email carried the decision; no forum for questions or feedback existed, so the grapevine replaced official facts.
  • Participation gap: employees were not consulted on layout, seating or timelines — the change felt imposed, triggering resistance (petition, resignations).
  • Perceived losses: privacy, control over workspace, status and comfort — individual reasons for resistance (fear of the unknown).
  • Process failure: no diagnosis, no pilot, no support plan — Lewin's unfreezing step was skipped entirely.

Answer (b): I would stop the rollout, rebuild trust with two-way communication, and redesign the change with employees as partners.

Key points:
  • Pause and explain: hold town halls and Q&A sessions stating why open offices were chosen and what will change for each team.
  • Participate: form a staff design committee (seating zones, quiet booths, noise rules); pilot in one floor and publicise results.
  • Support: offer a transition period, adjustable desks, hybrid seating options and relocation counselling for affected teams.
  • Manage the grapevine: publish a weekly update with facts and decisions; assign floor champions as official information points.
  • Retention: speak personally to the resigning developers and address their specific concerns before accepting resignations.
  • Review: measure productivity, satisfaction and turnover after 90 days and adjust the design — change is a loop, not a launch event.

Takeaway: communicate before, during and after the change — employees resist imposed decisions, not good ideas.

Q1. Models of change + resistance to change and its management (20 Marks)

Answer: Change models map how organisations move from the current state to a desired state, and every model succeeds only when resistance is understood and managed.

Key points:
  • Lewin's three-step model: Unfreezing (create readiness and reduce resistance) → Moving/Changing (implement new behaviours) → Refreezing (stabilise the new state through reinforcement and rewards).
  • Kotter's eight-step model: create urgency → build a guiding coalition → form a strategic vision → enlist volunteers → enable action by removing barriers → generate short-term wins → sustain acceleration → institute change into culture.
  • Reasons for resistance (individual): habit, fear of the unknown, loss of status or job security, economic insecurity, misunderstanding and low tolerance for change.
  • Reasons for resistance (organisational): structural inertia, group norms, resource constraints, threat to expertise or power relationships, and sunk costs.
  • Management of resistance: education and communication, participation and involvement, facilitation and support, negotiation and agreement; manipulation and coercion only as last resort.
  • Example: demonetisation (2016) required unfreezing old cash habits, moving to digital payments, and refreezing cashless behaviour through rewards and convenience.

Takeaway: name two models (Lewin + Kotter), then link each reason for resistance to its matching strategy — education, participation, support, negotiation.

Q2. Barriers to effective communication (20 Marks)

Answer: Barriers are the forces that distort a message between sender and receiver, and they can be classified into four broad groups before being removed.

Key points:
  • Semantic barriers: jargon, technical words, ambiguous language and different meanings of the same word — e.g. "optimise the KPI" says nothing to a shop-floor worker.
  • Psychological barriers: perception, emotions, status differences, filtering (passing on only what suits the sender), selective attention and poor listening.
  • Organisational barriers: too many hierarchy levels, information overload, unclear authority and absence of feedback channels.
  • Physical barriers: noise, distance, poor technology and inconvenient timing of messages.
  • Interpersonal barriers: low trust and credibility between parties — a message from a manager seen as untrustworthy is doubted even when accurate.
  • Remedies: simple language, active listening, feedback and confirmation, the right channel, fewer layers, open-door policy and empathy.

Takeaway: identify the barrier's family first — the cure (language, feedback, structure, technology) always matches the cause.

Q3. Factors influencing choice of structure + degree of decentralization (20 Marks)

Answer: Structure choice depends on strategy, size, technology and environment, while the degree of decentralization decides how far down decisions are pushed.

Key points:
  • Strategy: cost leadership suits a centralised, tight structure; differentiation and innovation suit flexible, decentralised designs.
  • Size: small organisations stay simple and flat; growth adds departments, levels and formal rules.
  • Technology: routine mass-production technology suits mechanistic structures; non-routine, knowledge work suits organic structures.
  • Environment: stable environments favour mechanistic (bureaucratic) structures; turbulent environments favour organic, adaptable ones.
  • Nature of work and people: complex tasks and skilled, committed subordinates allow wider spans and more decentralisation.
  • Degree of decentralization: the continuum between centralization (decisions at the top) and decentralization (decisions pushed to lower levels); influenced by size, geographical spread, history, willingness to delegate, subordinate competence and urgency of decisions.
  • Balance: routine, fast-moving operational decisions are decentralised; strategic, high-risk decisions stay centralised — e.g. branch managers sanction small loans; head office fixes rates and limits.

Takeaway: structure and decentralization are both contingency decisions — match them to strategy, size, technology and environment.

Q4. Any two theories of motivation + present-day relevance (20 Marks)

Answer: Content theories (Maslow, Herzberg) explain what motivates people, process theories (Expectancy, Equity) explain how — and all remain relevant with adaptation to today's workplace.

Key points:
  • Maslow's need hierarchy: physiological → safety → social/belonging → esteem → self-actualization; a satisfied need stops motivating, so managers must know which level each employee is at.
  • Herzberg's two-factor theory: hygiene factors (pay, conditions, security, policy) prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate; motivators (achievement, recognition, growth, responsibility) create satisfaction.
  • McClelland's needs: achievement, power and affiliation — achievement-driven people thrive in challenging roles with feedback.
  • Expectancy theory (Vroom): motivation = expectancy (effort → performance) x instrumentality (performance → reward) x valence (value of reward); all three must be positive.
  • Equity theory: people compare their input-output ratio with others; perceived unfairness reduces effort — pay transparency and fair appraisals matter.
  • Present-day relevance: salary and benefits are hygiene basics even in IT and startups; motivators today are autonomy, purpose, learning and work-life balance — especially for remote and gig employees.

Takeaway: motivation is individual — keep hygiene levels fair (Herzberg) while feeding higher needs (Maslow) through challenge, purpose and growth.

Q5. Short notes (write any three) (20 Marks)

Answer (a) PERT and CPM: Network planning techniques that schedule project activities, find the critical path and control time and cost.

Key points:
  • Network elements: activities (tasks), events (start/end points), and the critical path — the longest chain of dependent activities that fixes project duration.
  • PERT: probabilistic, uses three time estimates (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) — ideal for R&D and new projects where times are uncertain.
  • CPM: deterministic, assumes known activity times — ideal for construction, maintenance and repetitive projects; enables cost-time trade-off (crashing).
  • Slack/float: the delay an activity can absorb without delaying the project; only critical activities have zero slack.
  • Uses: project monitoring, resource levelling and crash-cost analysis — e.g. building a metro line or launching a product on a fixed date.

Answer (b) Characteristics of directing: Directing is the function of guiding, supervising and motivating subordinates so they work willingly.

Key points:
  • Continuous process: directing operates throughout the life of the organisation, at every moment of supervision.
  • Top-down flow: it flows from superiors to subordinates along the chain of command.
  • Human and psychological: it deals with people's behaviour, emotions and attitudes, not just tasks.
  • Pervasive: performed at every level — every manager directs, from the CEO to the supervisor.
  • Elements: supervision, leadership, motivation and communication work together to make directing effective.

Answer (c) Impact of IT on organizing work: Information technology is reshaping how work is organised, coordinated and controlled.

Key points:
  • Flatter structures: IT replaces middle-management information relays, removing layers and widening spans of control.
  • Virtual and remote teams: work is organised around projects and networks rather than fixed departments and desks.
  • Real-time coordination: ERP, dashboards and instant messaging coordinate functions across locations, e.g. a bank's core-banking network links every branch live.
  • Knowledge sharing: intranets, e-learning and knowledge portals (like Vikrant Advani's KM initiatives in the June 2024 case) spread learning organisation-wide.
  • Empowerment and control: IT enables self-service and data-driven control — employees get information directly, while managers monitor through MIS.

Answer (d) Scientific management: F.W. Taylor's approach to analysing work scientifically to maximise efficiency.

Key points:
  • Principles: science not rule of thumb; harmony not discord; cooperation not individualism; maximum output; equal division of work between management and workers.
  • Techniques: time-and-motion study, standardisation of tools and tasks, functional foremanship, and differential piece-rate wages.
  • Impact: assembly-line efficiency and standard operating procedures (e.g. Maruti Suzuki's factory floor) trace their origin to Taylor.
  • Criticism: treated workers as machines, ignored social needs — which the Hawthorne studies later corrected.

Answer (e) Single use plan: A one-time plan framed for a specific purpose or project, used once and then dissolved.

Key points:
  • Forms: budgets (money plans for a period), programmes (sequences of activities for a purpose), and projects (one-off undertakings).
  • Contrast with standing plans: standing plans (policies, procedures, rules) are repeated again and again; single-use plans expire with their objective.
  • Example: a festival-sale campaign budget, an office-move project plan, or a product-launch programme.
  • Management role: each single-use plan needs clear goals, resources and a review point, and its results feed the next planning cycle.

Takeaway: every short note needs one line of definition, 2–3 distinguishing features and one example — that structure scores marks reliably.

Key points:
  • Personal causes: Renu's recent divorce and financial pressure forced her into a job below her abilities; her professional identity (foreign languages) was never used.
  • Career frustration: every application for a professional job was rejected for lack of related education/experience, so her growth needs (Maslow, Herzberg) stayed unmet.
  • Warning signs: poor dressing, late arrival, two-hour lunches, absenteeism on Mondays/Fridays, slurred speech and unsteadiness — the classic pattern of growing dependence.
  • Job-behaviour link: boredom and lack of challenge accelerated withdrawal; the job was "temporary" in her mind, weakening commitment.

Answer (ii): Renu's behaviour shows stress-withdrawal and possible dependence; Khan acted with empathy and good intent but intervened too late and without a structured plan.

Key points:
  • Renu's behaviour: unmet growth needs → frustration → neglect of self and work → absenteeism → dependence; each stage reinforced the next (withdrawal loop).
  • Khan's positives: she cared (wanted to help, not punish), gathered observations, granted leave, and consulted the Personnel Manager — correct instincts.
  • Khan's failures: she waited months before acting ("job still accomplished on time"), avoided confrontation at the first signs, granted the vacation without conditions, and had no discussion of the "IQ above 50" remark — a cry for help that went unaddressed.

  • Job-redesign route: use her language skills — international correspondence, translation work, liaison with overseas offices — restoring challenge and growth (Herzberg's motivators).
  • Performance route: a clear performance-improvement plan with observable standards, regular feedback and a review date.
  • Leave and wellness route: structured leave combined with a return-to-work plan and wellness support, instead of an unplanned two-week break.
  • Transfer route: temporary transfer to another department with fresh tasks and supervision, breaking the boredom loop.
  • Disciplinary route: written warnings only after supportive options fail and performance continues to decline — always the last resort.

Key points:
  • Treats the root cause: counselling addresses the dependence and stress; job redesign addresses the boredom and unmet growth needs — together they break the withdrawal loop.
  • Protects a valuable employee: Renu was the top typist who performed excellently for nine months; replacing her costs recruitment, training and lost performance.
  • Aligns with HR policy: modern HR favours rehabilitation over punishment; the company avoids legal and ethical risk of dismissing an employee with a health problem.
  • Measurable plan: a 3–6 month probation with attendance, performance and wellness reviews, then evaluate — if the plan fails, discipline becomes justified.

Takeaway: diagnose the person behind the poor performance — rehabilitate and redesign first, discipline only after support has genuinely failed.

Q1. Challenges management encounters for effectiveness (20 Marks)

Answer: Effective management is a continuous struggle against internal and external challenges that change with every shift in people, technology and environment.

Key points:
  • Internal challenges: resistance to change, interpersonal conflict, resource scarcity, motivation gaps, skill obsolescence, information overload and coordination failures.
  • External challenges: a VUCA world — volatility, uncertainty, complexity and ambiguity; globalisation, technology disruption, regulation, competition and shifting customer expectations.
  • People challenges: attracting and retaining talent, managing a multigenerational and diverse workforce, remote/hybrid coordination, and employee wellbeing.
  • Ethical challenges: balancing profit with ethics, ESG responsibilities, and pressure to compromise on values in competitive markets.
  • Measurement challenge: effectiveness (doing the right things) is hard to measure against efficiency (doing things right); managers must balance both.
  • Indian context: high attrition in IT, regulatory complexity, informal-sector competition, and rapid digital adoption make these challenges sharper.
  • Response: agility, continuous learning, participative leadership and strong culture convert challenges into capability.

Takeaway: effectiveness is dynamic — managers must continuously adapt rather than rely on static routines.

Q2. Necessity of planning + process and steps (20 Marks)

Answer: Planning decides today what must be done tomorrow; it gives direction, reduces uncertainty and provides the standards that all other functions use.

Key points:
  • Necessity: provides direction and focus; reduces risk and uncertainty; coordinates departments; sets control standards; improves resource efficiency; motivates through clear goals (MBO).
  • Step 1 – objectives: define what the organisation wants to achieve, in clear, measurable terms.
  • Step 2 – premises: identify assumptions about the future — market, technology, policy, resources — as the foundation of plans.
  • Steps 3–4 – alternatives: generate and evaluate alternative courses of action against cost, risk, resources and fit with objectives.
  • Step 5 – selection: choose the best course and build derivative plans (budgets, schedules, policies) around it.
  • Steps 6–7 – implementation and review: execute through organising and directing, then compare outcomes with plans (controlling) and revise premises.

Takeaway: present planning as a loop — objectives → premises → alternatives → selection → implementation → review — and mention control feeding back.

Q3. Features of controlling + essentials of a good control system (20 Marks)

Answer: Controlling keeps actual performance aligned with standards, and a good control system is timely, accurate, flexible and economical.

Key points:
  • Features: controlling is a continuous and pervasive function, forward-looking, based on standards, goal-oriented, and allows management by exception.
  • Process: establish standards → measure actual performance → compare with standards → analyse deviations → take corrective action.
  • Essentials of a good system: tailored to the organisation; timely (reports when action is still possible); accurate; flexible; economical (cost less than the losses they prevent); understandable; focused on critical points; and objective.
  • Example — timely: a bank tracks NPA levels monthly, not yearly, so branches can act before accounts turn bad.
  • Example — flexible: a sales dashboard adjusts targets when a new competitor enters, instead of rigidly enforcing old numbers.
  • Example — critical points: an airline watches on-time departures and safety drills, not the colour of the lounge furniture.

Takeaway: control is a cycle ending in corrective action — and every essential (timely, flexible, economical) can be illustrated with a one-line example.

Q4. Any two theories of motivation + present-day relevance (20 Marks)

Answer: Maslow's hierarchy tells managers what to offer; Herzberg's two-factor theory tells them which of those offerings actually motivate — and both apply directly to today's workforce.

Key points:
  • Maslow: five levels — physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualization; a satisfied need no longer motivates, so motivation must be tailored to the employee's current level.
  • Herzberg: hygiene factors (pay, conditions, company policy, security) remove dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth) create satisfaction — the opposite of "more pay = more motivation".
  • Relevance — pay: competitive salary is now hygiene; even generous pay only prevents dissatisfaction (e.g. churn in Indian IT despite high salaries).
  • Relevance — motivators: autonomy, purpose, learning and work-life balance drive engagement for remote, hybrid and gig workers.
  • Relevance — diversity: needs differ across generations and roles; a flexible-benefits menu lets employees pick what motivates them (esteem vs security vs growth).
  • Practical use: job enrichment, recognition programmes, career paths and transparent rewards operationalise both theories.

Takeaway: keep hygiene fair so people don't leave, then add motivators so people give their best — both theories together explain modern engagement.

  • Same word, different meanings: "profit", "target" and "bonus" can mean different things to an accountant, a salesman and a worker.
  • Jargon and technical language: specialists use terms outsiders don't understand, creating a semantic barrier.
  • Ambiguity and abstraction: vague words like "soon", "reasonable" or "improve quality" leave room for misunderstanding.
  • Remedy: use simple, concrete words; define terms; ask for feedback to confirm shared meaning.

Answer (d) Hawthorne studies: The Western Electric experiments (1924–32) led by Elton Mayo that founded the human relations movement.

Key points:
  • Illumination experiment: productivity rose even when light was reduced — attention and social factors mattered more than physical conditions.
  • Relay assembly test room: output rose with breaks and incentives, and kept rising — group spirit and recognition were at work.
  • Interviewing programme: 21,000 interviews showed emotional factors and attitudes shape performance.
  • Bank wiring room: informal groups set their own output norms, controlling members' productivity.
  • Impact: proved employees are social beings, not just rational-economic machines — gave birth to human relations and later behavioural management.

Answer (e) External recruitment: External recruitment draws candidates from outside the organisation through various sourcing channels.

Key points:
  • Sources: advertisements (newspapers, online), campus placements, employment exchanges, consultants and head-hunters, job portals (Naukri, LinkedIn), walk-ins and professional bodies.
  • Advantages: wider talent pool, fresh ideas and experience from outside, no internal rivalry, and new skill-sets enter the firm.
  • Disadvantages: costly and slow, higher risk of wrong selection, longer induction time, and it can demotivate internal candidates.
  • Balance: best practice mixes sources — promote from within where possible, recruit externally for new capabilities and leadership depth.

Takeaway: each note earns marks with definition + distinguishing points + one example — keep the structure uniform across all five options.

Q1. Roles of a manager and their present-day dynamics (20 Marks)

Answer: Mintzberg's ten managerial roles in three groups — interpersonal, informational and decisional — describe everything a manager actually does, with new twists in today's digital workplace.

Key points:
  • Interpersonal: figurehead (ceremonial duties), leader (motivating subordinates), liaison (external contacts).
  • Informational: monitor (scanning information), disseminator (sharing inside), spokesperson (speaking outside).
  • Decisional: entrepreneur (initiating change), disturbance handler (crises), resource allocator (time, money, people), negotiator.
  • Dynamics: roles are interlinked through authority → information → decisions, and managers switch roles many times a day; mix changes with level (supervisor plays leader; CEO plays figurehead and negotiator).
  • Present day: virtual and hybrid teams, video-call communication, data-driven decisions, digital transformation, and new duties like wellbeing and ESG reporting.
  • Example: a bank branch manager leads tellers (leader), reads daily reports (monitor), answers customers (spokesperson) and manages an app outage (disturbance handler).

Q2. Approaches to organizing and analyzing work (20 Marks)

Answer: Work can be organized through scientific, administrative, bureaucratic, systems or contingency approaches — each with its own logic, tools and limits.

Key points:
  • Scientific (Taylor): time-and-motion study finds the "one best way" — standardisation and assembly-line efficiency (Maruti Suzuki's SOPs).
  • Administrative (Fayol): principles — division of work, unity of command, scalar chain, span of control — design the whole hierarchy.
  • Bureaucratic (Weber): rules, hierarchy, impersonality and written records — consistency and fairness in government departments and banks.
  • Systems: organisation as an open system of inputs → processes → outputs → feedback, interacting with the environment.
  • Contingency: no one best structure — depends on strategy, size, technology and environment; startups stay flat, large banks stay formal.
  • Work analysis tools: job analysis, work simplification and value-chain analysis identify what each position must do and what adds customer value.

Takeaway: sequence the approaches historically and pair each with an example to show relevance and limitation.

Q3. Staffing: importance + sources of recruitment (20 Marks)

  • Importance: right people raise productivity; effective staffing cuts recruitment and training cost; skilled people are a competitive advantage; fair processes build satisfaction and reduce turnover; succession planning ensures continuity.
  • Internal sources: promotion, transfer, internal postings, employee referrals — cheap, fast, motivating, the person is known; but limited pool, inbreeding, vacancy chains and jealousy.
  • External sources: advertisements, campus placements, employment exchanges, consultants, job portals (Naukri, LinkedIn), walk-ins — wider choice, fresh ideas, no internal rivalry; but costly, slow, risky, longer induction, demotivating for internal candidates.
  • Balance: most firms promote from within for motivation and recruit externally for new capabilities — a software firm promotes team leads but hires freshers from campus and seniors through consultants.

Takeaway: weigh sources against cost, speed and talent needs — the best policy mixes internal promotion with targeted external hiring.

Q1. Features, purpose and process of planning (20 Marks)

Answer: Planning decides in advance what to do and how, giving the organisation direction, coordination and control standards through a step-by-step process.

Key points:
  • Features: planning is goal-oriented, primary (first among functions), pervasive (all levels), forward-looking, continuous, intellectual (thinking before doing), and chooses from alternatives.
  • Purpose: direction and focus, reduced risk and uncertainty, coordination of departments, standards for control, efficient resource use, and motivation through clear goals.
  • Steps: set objectives → develop planning premises (assumptions) → identify alternatives → evaluate alternatives → select the course → formulate derivative plans → implement → review and follow-up.
  • Types: strategic vs operational; long-term vs short-term; standing vs single-use plans.
  • MBO: management by objectives converts planning into individual commitments that are later used for appraisal and control.
  • Example: a retailer's festive-sale plan sets revenue targets, assumes discount economics, evaluates channels, then implements with budgets and dashboards.

Takeaway: features (goal-oriented, primary, pervasive, continuous) plus the 8-step process plus MBO covers every mark on this question.

Q2. Organizing and analyzing work: concept, approaches, limitations (20 Marks)

Answer: Organizing groups activities, duties and authority into a structure, and the approaches — scientific, administrative, bureaucratic, systems and contingency — each carry their own limitations.

Key points:
  • Concept: identifying activities, grouping them into departments, assigning duties, delegating authority and creating coordination; analyzing work means breaking jobs into tasks, duties and responsibilities (job analysis).
  • Scientific approach: time-and-motion study for the one best way — limitation: treats workers as machines, ignores social needs.
  • Administrative approach: Fayol's principles for the whole hierarchy — limitation: rigid, assumes a stable organisation, principles can conflict (e.g. unity of command vs specialist advice).
  • Bureaucratic approach: rules and hierarchy for consistency — limitation: red tape, rigidity, slow decisions, dehumanising.
  • Systems approach: organisation as an open system of inputs, processes, outputs and feedback — limitation: abstract, hard to operationalise at the shop-floor level.
  • Contingency approach: the best structure depends on strategy, size, technology and environment — limitation: offers no universal prescription, demands situational judgement.
  • Relevance: hybrid designs in practice — e.g. Zomato groups delivery/dining/supply as divisions (departmentalization) on a common technology platform.

Takeaway: every approach's limitation is its fix — rigidity of classical schools is answered by systems and contingency thinking.

Q3. Directing: concept, characteristics + difference from staffing (20 Marks)

Answer: Directing guides, supervises and motivates people to work willingly; staffing supplies the people — directing activates them.

Key points:
  • Concept: the managerial function of leading, communicating, supervising and motivating subordinates to achieve organisational objectives.
  • Characteristics: continuous process; flows top-down along the chain of command; pervades all levels; deals with human and psychological factors; aims at willing cooperation; uses supervision, leadership, communication and motivation as its tools.
  • Difference — purpose: staffing builds the workforce (right person, right job); directing activates it (getting the work done).
  • Difference — nature: staffing is about recruitment, selection, placement, training and appraisal; directing is about guidance, supervision, motivation and communication on the job.
  • Difference — flow: staffing involves both upward planning and selection decisions; directing always flows from superior to subordinate.
  • Sequence: first staff the positions, then direct the people — a manager cannot direct someone who has not been selected, placed and trained.

Takeaway: one sentence separates them — staffing builds the team, directing makes the team work.

Q4. Leadership styles and their present-day relevance (20 Marks)

Answer: Leadership styles range from autocratic to democratic to laissez-faire — and today's context demands a situational blend rather than one fixed style.

Key points:
  • Autocratic: the leader decides alone and commands — fast, clear, works in crises (e.g. Arvind Jain's turnaround era in the Modern Bank case); limitation: kills initiative and morale.
  • Democratic / participative: decisions are shared with the team — builds commitment, better ideas, higher satisfaction; slower and costly in time.
  • Laissez-faire: the leader gives full freedom to experts — works with highly skilled, self-driven teams; risks drift and coordination loss otherwise.
  • Blake-Mouton grid: concern for people vs concern for production — 9,9 team management is the ideal balance.
  • Situational view: the right style depends on the task, the team's maturity and the situation (contingency) — a crisis needs quick autocratic calls; innovation needs participation.
  • Present-day relevance: remote and hybrid teams demand transparent, participative and coaching styles; young knowledge workers respond to influence, purpose and autonomy, not commands.

Takeaway: no style is always best — leaders today must read the situation and flex between command, participation and delegation.

Q5. Group vs individual decision-making + overcoming barriers (20 Marks)

Answer: Groups decide with more information and acceptance but at higher time and conformity cost; individuals are faster but narrower — and known barriers can be systematically overcome.

Key points:
  • Group advantages: more information and perspectives, higher-quality alternatives, better acceptance and commitment, clear division of responsibility, and development of members.
  • Group disadvantages: slower and costlier, groupthink (conformity), domination by one member, social loafing, and the risk of compromise that pleases no one.
  • Individual advantages: speed, clear accountability, and no conformity pressure; ideal for routine and time-critical decisions.
  • Individual disadvantages: limited information, personal bias, and decisions that lack team buy-in.
  • Barriers to effective decisions: information overload, bounded rationality, personal bias and stereotyping, emotions, time pressure, and fear of responsibility.
  • Overcoming barriers: define the problem clearly, gather reliable data, use brainstorming and devil's advocacy to counter groupthink, set decision deadlines, use decision criteria, and build a feedback/review step.
  • Choice rule: match the mode to the decision — routine operational decisions individually; strategic, complex, high-stakes decisions in groups.

Takeaway: decide by fit — complexity and stakes call for groups; speed and accountability call for individuals — and always add a review step.

Key points:

Q7. Theories of business & CSR + benefits and drivers (20 Marks)

Answer: Business-society theories range from the classical shareholder view to stakeholder and CSR theories — and CSR today is both a moral duty and a business driver.

Key points:
  • Classical/neoclassical view: the only responsibility of business is profit within the law (Friedman) — social spending is the government's job.
  • Stakeholder theory: business must balance customers, employees, suppliers, community, environment and shareholders — not just owners.
  • Carroll's CSR pyramid: economic (be profitable) → legal (obey the law) → ethical (do what is right) → philanthropic (give back) responsibilities stacked in order.
  • Triple bottom line: measure success on people, planet and profit (social, environmental, financial).
  • Benefits of CSR: stronger brand and customer loyalty, easier talent attraction and retention, better risk and reputation management, investor preference, and long-term cost savings (energy, waste).
  • Drivers: regulation and compliance (Companies Act 2013 mandates 2% profit CSR in India), customer and investor pressure, media scrutiny, ethical leadership, and competitive differentiation.
  • Indian examples: Tata Group's trusts and community work, ITC's e-choupal and afforestation, and statutory CSR boards in listed companies.

Takeaway: answer the theory side with Carroll's pyramid + stakeholder view, and the practice side with benefits and Indian drivers.

Q8. Factors contributing to motivation + two theories (20 Marks)

Answer: Motivation comes from the interaction of individual needs, job design and organisational rewards — best explained by combining content theories (what) with process theories (how).

Key points:
  • Individual factors: needs (Maslow's five levels), personality, attitudes, self-esteem, career aspirations and personal values.
  • Job factors: challenge, variety, autonomy, feedback, and clarity of role — job enrichment raises motivation.
  • Organisational factors: pay and benefits, recognition, promotion prospects, leadership style, organisational culture and fairness of policies.
  • Environmental factors: work-life balance, peer support, job security and the broader economic climate.
  • Herzberg's two-factor theory: hygiene (pay, conditions, security) prevents dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, growth) create satisfaction — relevance: pay is hygiene, so retention needs more than salary.
  • Vroom's expectancy theory: motivation = expectancy (effort→performance) x instrumentality (performance→reward) x valence (reward value) — relevance: managers must link effort, results and rewards credibly for hybrid and sales teams.
  • Present-day relevance: purpose, learning, flexibility and fair transparency motivate modern workers — one-size-fits-all rewards fail.

Takeaway: list the factor families briefly, then go deep on two theories — one content (Herzberg) and one process (Vroom) — with a modern application each.

Q1. Impact of the Human Relations approach on management thought (20 Marks)

Answer: The Human Relations approach, born from the Hawthorne studies, shifted management thought from treating workers as machines to recognising them as social beings — and its influence continues today.

Key points:
  • Origin: Elton Mayo's Hawthorne experiments at Western Electric (1924–32) — illumination, relay-assembly, interviewing and bank-wiring studies.
  • Key findings: productivity rose with attention and group spirit, informal groups set their own output norms, and emotions and attitudes shape performance — not just pay and physical conditions.
  • Shift in thought: from Taylor's economic-man assumption to social man — workers respond to recognition, belonging and participation.
  • Impact on management functions: directing became leadership and motivation; communication gained feedback and listening; supervision shifted from command to facilitation.
  • Behavioural follow-ons: Maslow, Herzberg, McGregor (Theory X/Y) and Likert built on it, creating the behavioural school.
  • Critique: lacked scientific rigour, oversimplified as the "happy worker" myth — but it permanently humanised management thought.
  • Present-day echo: employee engagement, psychological safety, culture and wellbeing programmes are all grandchildren of Hawthorne.

Takeaway: anchor the answer in Hawthorne's findings, then trace their legacy through the behavioural school into today's engagement practices.

Q2. Scientific management approach to organizing and analyzing work (20 Marks)

Answer: Taylor's scientific management analyses each job into its smallest tasks, finds the "one best way", and organises work around standardised, measured methods.

Key points:
  • Core idea: replace rule-of-thumb practices with science — every job can be studied, standardised and taught.
  • Analyzing work: time-and-motion study breaks work into basic motions, eliminating waste and fixing standard times (e.g. brick-laying experiments).
  • Organizing work: tasks are simplified and standardised; functional foremanship divides supervision into specialised roles; tools and methods are standardised.
  • Scientific selection and training: workers are matched to jobs by ability and trained in the one best method, not left to learn by trial.
  • Motivation through wages: differential piece-rate — higher pay per unit to those meeting standards — ties reward directly to output.
  • Principles: science not rule of thumb; harmony not discord; cooperation not individualism; maximum output; equal division of work between management and workers.
  • Impact and limits: gave us assembly lines and SOPs (Maruti Suzuki), but ignored social needs — corrected by the Human Relations approach.

Takeaway: present the four-step cycle, name Taylor's five principles, and close with the classical critique to show balance.

Q3. "A manager has to inspire and influence his people to willingly work" — Elaborate (20 Marks)

Answer: The statement is the essence of leading — management gets results not by command but by inspiring and influencing people so they work willingly towards organisational objectives.

Key points:
  • Leading vs managing: management orders tasks; leadership inspires commitment — willing effort comes from the head and heart, not just compliance.
  • Inspiration: a compelling vision, personal example, purpose and enthusiasm make people identify with goals — "people buy into the leader before the plan".
  • Influence tools: motivation (needs and rewards), communication (shared meaning), and trust — influence, not authority, produces willing cooperation.
  • Delegation and empowerment: giving responsibility and support makes people own their tasks; participation converts targets into personal goals.
  • Recognition and growth: Herzberg's motivators — achievement, recognition, responsibility — keep effort willing and sustained.
  • Example: Ratan Tata's personal integrity inspired employee loyalty across the group; a branch manager who listens and coaches gets far more than one who only issues orders.

Takeaway: command buys compliance, influence buys commitment — quote the levers (vision, motivation, communication, trust) and an example.

Q4. Importance of organisation structure and chart + factors influencing choice (20 Marks)

Answer: Structure and chart give the organisation form — clear roles, authority and coordination — and the right design depends on strategy, size, technology and environment.

Key points:
  • Importance of structure: divides work into roles, creates authority relationships and reporting lines, coordinates departments, defines accountability, and enables growth.
  • Organisation chart: the visual map of positions, hierarchy, spans of control and lines of authority — tells employees who reports to whom and where they fit.
  • Chart benefits: quick orientation of new staff, reveals overlaps and gaps, communicates lines of command, and supports planning of changes.
  • Chart limits: shows formal relations only — not informal influence, actual workload or quality of relationships.
  • Choice factors: strategy (cost vs differentiation), size and growth stage, technology (routine vs knowledge work), environment (stable vs turbulent), nature of tasks and skills, and managerial philosophy.
  • Example: a startup stays flat with informal coordination; a growing bank formalises departments, levels and spans as scale, regulation and risk demand.

Takeaway: structure + chart = the skeleton; choice factors = the contingency logic — answer both halves explicitly.

Q5. Channels of communication and their effectiveness (20 Marks)

Answer: Organisations communicate through formal channels — downward, upward, horizontal and diagonal — plus the informal grapevine, each effective for different purposes.

Key points:
  • Downward: orders, policies, targets from top to bottom — effective for instruction and coordination; example: a circular announcing a new leave policy.
  • Upward: reports, feedback, grievances from bottom to top — effective for control and participation; example: monthly sales reports, suggestion schemes.
  • Horizontal/lateral: between peers and departments — effective for coordination and problem-solving; example: production and logistics teams synchronising dispatch.
  • Diagonal: communication across levels and departments (e.g. a finance analyst with the marketing head) — effective for speed on cross-functional projects.
  • Grapevine: the informal network — fast and human but unreliable; effective as an early-warning radar and morale gauge; example: office chatter about promotions.
  • Choosing channels: match richness to the message — face-to-face for sensitive or ambiguous matters, written/digital for records and scale (email, intranet, meetings, video calls).

Takeaway: each channel serves a purpose — instructions down, feedback up, coordination sideways — and the grapevine needs managing, not killing.

Q6. Importance of organisational culture + traits of great culture + building factors (20 Marks)

Answer: Culture is the shared values and assumptions that shape behaviour; great cultures are built deliberately on values, leadership, people practices and stories.

Key points:
  • Importance: culture gives identity, commitment and stability; guides decisions without rules; coordinates behaviour; and is a hard-to-copy competitive advantage.
  • Traits of a great culture: clear shared values; trust and psychological safety; open communication and transparency; recognition and fairness; learning and innovation; strong leadership example; and low politics.
  • Building factor — leadership: founders and leaders model the values; Ratan Tata's integrity and Infosys's "people first" shaped their cultures.
  • Building factor — people practices: merit-based recruitment, fair rewards, development and work-life balance embed values in daily experience.
  • Building factor — rituals and stories: onboarding, celebrations, legends and symbols transmit culture to newcomers; e.g. Infosys's "customer first" narratives.
  • Building factor — structure and communication: open-door policies, transparency and empowered teams sustain culture through change.
  • Sustainability: culture survives when it is deliberately audited, reinforced through rewards, and adapted without losing its core values.

Takeaway: culture lives below the surface — leaders build it through example, practices, stories and consistent reinforcement.

Q7. Reasons for resistance to change + strategies to overcome (20 Marks)

Answer: People resist change from habit, fear and perceived loss — both individual and organisational — and each reason has a matching management strategy.

Key points:
  • Individual reasons: habit, fear of the unknown, loss of status or job security, economic insecurity, misunderstanding of purpose, and low tolerance for change.
  • Organisational reasons: structural inertia, group norms, resource constraints, threat to expertise or power, and sunk-cost attachment to old ways.
  • Education and communication: explain the why, what and how before and during change — best when resistance comes from misinformation.
  • Participation and involvement: let those affected design the change — converts resistors into owners (best for deep change).
  • Facilitation and support: training, counselling and time to adjust — best when fear and anxiety drive resistance.
  • Negotiation and agreement: incentives and explicit packages for groups that clearly lose — best when resistance comes from a powerful minority.
  • Manipulation and coercion: selective information or threats — fast but toxic; only as last resort because trust damage is long-lasting.

Takeaway: diagnose the reason first, then choose the strategy — and always prefer participation over coercion.

Q8. Importance of controlling + process and essentials of a good control system (20 Marks)

Answer: Controlling keeps the organisation on track towards its objectives, and a good control system is a timely, accurate, flexible feedback loop ending in corrective action.

Key points:
  • Importance: completes the management cycle, detects deviations early, protects resources, provides standards for future planning, and empowers employees with clear expectations.
  • Process: establish standards → measure actual performance → compare with standards → analyse deviations → take corrective action.
  • Essentials of a good system: timely (before damage), accurate, flexible, economical, understandable, objective and focused on critical points.
  • Forward-looking: control should predict and prevent, not only report the past — leading indicators beat lagging ones.
  • Management by exception: concentrate attention on significant deviations; routine matters run on standard procedures.
  • Techniques: budgets, break-even analysis, PERT/CPM, standard costing, MIS dashboards and audits — matched to the nature of work.
  • Example: a bank's NPA dashboard flags a rising slippage trend region-wise, and branches act before the year-end report arrives.

Takeaway: control is a loop that feeds planning — every deviation must end in an action, not just a report.
