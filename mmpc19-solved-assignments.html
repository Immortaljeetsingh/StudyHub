Q1. Define quality. Compare the views of Deming, Juran and Crosby. How has quality management evolved over time?

Answer: Quality means different things to different stakeholders. According to ISO 8402, quality is the totality of features of a product or service that satisfy stated or implied needs. Deming defined quality as a predictable degree of uniformity and dependability at low cost, emphasising systems and variation. Juran viewed quality as fitness for use, focusing on customer utility. Crosby defined quality as conformance to requirements, holding that quality is free when work is done right the first time, while Feigenbaum described quality as the total composite of product and service characteristics. The evolution of quality management spans four eras: the inspection era (1920s), where defects were detected by sorting good from bad; statistical quality control (1940s), which prevented defects through control charts; quality assurance (1960s), which built systematic, documented processes; and total quality management (1980s), which made quality a company-wide continuous improvement philosophy. These shifts moved quality from a detection activity to a strategic management approach, an idea central to TQM.

Key points:

  • Quality means different things to different stakeholders.
  • According to ISO 8402, quality is the totality of features of a product or service that satisfy stated or implied needs.
  • These shifts moved quality from a detection activity to a strategic management approach, an idea central to TQM.

Takeaway: These shifts moved quality from a detection activity to a strategic management approach, an idea central to TQM.

Q2. What are the costs of quality? Explain the four categories and Deming's chain reaction.

Answer: The cost of quality is the total expenditure a firm incurs to achieve quality plus the losses caused by poor quality. It has four categories, remembered as PAIE. Prevention costs, the cheapest, are incurred before production through training, quality planning and process design. Appraisal costs arise during production through inspection, testing and audits. Internal failure costs, such as scrap, rework and re-inspection, occur before delivery, while external failure costs from warranty claims, returns and recalls occur after delivery and are the most expensive. The cost of poor quality (COPQ) is typically 15-25% of sales revenue, of which only 3-5% is visible; the rest is hidden in lost customers and reputational damage. Deming's chain reaction explains the economics of quality: improving quality lowers costs, which raises productivity, which increases market share and keeps the firm in business. As Crosby argued, every rupee spent on prevention saves about ten rupees in failure costs, so investing upfront in prevention is the most economical quality strategy.

Key points:

  • The cost of quality is the total expenditure a firm incurs to achieve quality plus the losses caused by poor quality.
  • Internal failure costs, such as scrap, rework and re-inspection, occur before delivery, while external failure costs from warranty claims, returns and recalls occur after delivery and are the most expensive.
  • Prevention costs, the cheapest, are incurred before production through training, quality planning and process design.

Takeaway: It has four categories, remembered as PAIE.

Q3. Explain the DMAIC methodology of Six Sigma. What is the role of 5S in workplace quality?

Answer: Six Sigma is a disciplined, data-driven methodology for eliminating defects, introduced by Motorola in the 1980s and famously adopted by GE. It targets 3.4 defects per million opportunities, representing a 99.9997% yield. DMAIC describes its five phases: Define the problem, scope and customer requirements; Measure current performance by collecting baseline data; Analyze the data to identify root causes; Improve by designing and piloting solutions; and Control by monitoring the process so gains are sustained. Advanced tools such as QFD, FMEA, Kaizen and Poka-Yoke support the methodology. The 5S technique creates an organised, disciplined workplace: Seiri (Sort) removes unnecessary items, Seiton (Set in Order) arranges everything for easy access, Seiso (Shine) keeps the workplace clean, Seiketsu (Standardize) fixes standards for consistency, and Shitsuke (Sustain) turns discipline into habit. Together, Six Sigma reduces process variation while 5S builds the visible order and employee ownership on which continuous improvement depends.

Key points:

  • Six Sigma is a disciplined, data-driven methodology for eliminating defects, introduced by Motorola in the 1980s and famously adopted by GE.
  • Advanced tools such as QFD, FMEA, Kaizen and Poka-Yoke support the methodology.
  • It targets 3.4 defects per million opportunities, representing a 99.9997% yield.

Takeaway: Together, Six Sigma reduces process variation while 5S builds the visible order and employee ownership on which continuous improvement depends.

Q4. Describe the ISO 9001 certification process. Differentiate between Stage 1 and Stage 2 audits.

Answer: ISO 9001 is the only certifiable standard in the ISO 9000 family and sets requirements for a quality management system. Certification follows seven steps: Application, Document Review, Stage 1 Audit, Stage 2 Audit, Certification Decision, Surveillance Audits and Re-certification. Stage 1 is a readiness assessment that reviews documentation to answer, "Are you ready?"; Stage 2 verifies on-site implementation to answer, "Are you doing what you said?" The certificate is valid for three years and maintained through annual surveillance audits, after which a full re-certification audit is conducted. Organisations may also pursue ISO 14001 for environmental management and ISO 45001 for occupational health and safety, all sharing the same ten-clause high-level structure that allows an Integrated Management System. In India, the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) provides product certification through the ISI mark and gold hallmarking. Audits themselves are first-party (internal self-assessment), second-party (customer evaluation of suppliers) or third-party (independent certification bodies), keeping the system credible.

Key points:

  • ISO 9001 is the only certifiable standard in the ISO 9000 family and sets requirements for a quality management system.
  • In India, the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) provides product certification through the ISI mark and gold hallmarking.
  • Certification follows seven steps: Application, Document Review, Stage 1 Audit, Stage 2 Audit, Certification Decision, Surveillance Audits and Re-certification.

Takeaway: Audits themselves are first-party (internal self-assessment), second-party (customer evaluation of suppliers) or third-party (independent certification bodies), keeping the system credible.
