Q1. Explain Kotler's five product levels with examples. (10 marks)

Answer: Theodore Levitt observed that products are almost always a combination of the tangible and the intangible - a product is a complex cluster of value satisfactions. Kotler's five-level model explains this: (1) Core benefit - the fundamental need the product satisfies; a hotel customer buys "rest and sleep," not a room. (2) Basic product - the tangible offering that delivers the benefit; a hotel room with a bed, bathroom, and towels. (3) Expected product - attributes consumers normally expect, such as clean sheets, running water, and a working television. (4) Augmented product - features that exceed expectations, such as complimentary breakfast, free Wi-Fi, or a gym in premium Indian hotels like Taj and ITC. (5) Potential product - everything the product could become in the future, including new services such as wellness retreats or rooftop dining. In the FMCG context, Parle-G's core benefit is affordable nourishment, the basic product is the biscuit packet, the expected product is consistent taste, augmentation includes new variants and packaging, and the potential product may include fortified nutrition. Marketers use the model to differentiate: in mature categories, competition shifts from the core and basic levels to the augmented level, where brands like Amul and Tata add service and experience.

Key points:

  • Theodore Levitt observed that products are almost always a combination of the tangible and the intangible - a product is a complex cluster of value satisfactions.
  • (3) Expected product - attributes consumers normally expect, such as clean sheets, running water, and a working television.
  • Kotler's five-level model explains this: (1) Core benefit - the fundamental need the product satisfies; a hotel customer buys "rest and sleep," not a room.

Takeaway: Marketers use the model to differentiate: in mature categories, competition shifts from the core and basic levels to the augmented level, where brands like Amul and Tata add service and experience.

Q2. Explain the product life cycle (PLC) and the marketing strategies appropriate at each stage. (10 marks)

Answer: The product life cycle describes the stages a product passes through: introduction, growth, maturity, and decline. In the introduction stage, sales grow slowly, costs are high, and competition is limited; the strategy is to create awareness through informative promotion and either skimming or penetration pricing. In the growth stage, sales rise rapidly, competitors enter, and profits peak; the strategy is to improve quality, add features and new segments, and build distribution. In the maturity stage, sales growth slows, competition intensifies, and profits stabilize or fall; the strategy is to modify the market, the product, or the marketing mix - Parle-G and Colgate use line extensions, promotions, and repackaging to defend share. In the decline stage, sales and profits fall; the strategy is to harvest, divest, or reposition, such as HUL pruning weak brands to focus on power brands. Rogers' adopter categories - innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards - help marketers time their promotion. The PLC is a useful planning tool, but it has limitations: not all products follow the curve, and the stage boundaries are difficult to identify until after the fact. Each stage therefore requires different product, price, promotion, and distribution strategies.

Key points:

  • The product life cycle describes the stages a product passes through: introduction, growth, maturity, and decline.
  • In the decline stage, sales and profits fall; the strategy is to harvest, divest, or reposition, such as HUL pruning weak brands to focus on power brands.
  • In the introduction stage, sales grow slowly, costs are high, and competition is limited; the strategy is to create awareness through informative promotion and either skimming or penetration pricing.

Takeaway: Each stage therefore requires different product, price, promotion, and distribution strategies.

Q3. Define brand equity and explain Aaker's model and Keller's CBBE model. (10 marks)

Answer: Brand equity is the added value a brand name gives a product over its generic equivalent - the differential consumer response and financial worth attached to the brand. David Aaker's model identifies five components: brand awareness (recall and recognition), brand associations (perceptions and imagery), perceived quality, brand loyalty, and proprietary assets such as patents. For example, Tata's equity rests on the trust built over decades. Kevin Keller's Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE) model builds a pyramid in four stages: brand salience (who are you?), performance and imagery (what are you?), judgments and feelings (what do I think and feel about you?), and resonance (how much connection do I have with you?). Resonance is the highest stage, where consumers identify deeply with the brand, as with Apple or Royal Enfield owners. Measurement follows three approaches: financial (Interbrand valuation), consumer-based (Aaker's Ten and Keller's CBBE), and combined methods. In India, brands such as Maggi illustrate consumer-based equity: despite the 2015 controversy, the strong emotional associations built over decades enabled a successful relaunch within months. Brand equity directly influences price premiums, distribution power, and resilience to competition.

Key points:

  • Brand equity is the added value a brand name gives a product over its generic equivalent - the differential consumer response and financial worth attached to the brand.
  • David Aaker's model identifies five components: brand awareness (recall and recognition), brand associations (perceptions and imagery), perceived quality, brand loyalty, and proprietary assets such as patents.
  • For example, Tata's equity rests on the trust built over decades.

Takeaway: Brand equity directly influences price premiums, distribution power, and resilience to competition.

Q4. Distinguish between line extension and brand extension. Discuss the success factors and risks of brand extension. (10 marks)

Answer: A line extension introduces a new variant within an existing category under the existing brand - for example, Amul adding a new ice-cream flavour or Colgate launching a new toothpaste variant. A brand extension (category extension) uses an established brand name to enter a completely new product category - for example, Maggi entering masala, or Patanjali moving from Ayurvedic products into food, personal care, and even clothing. Success factors include a strong fit between the parent brand and the new category, a positive brand image, transferable associations, quality assurance by the parent, and adequate marketing support. In India, brands like Tata have extended from steel and tea into cars, IT services, and hotels successfully because of shared values of trust and quality. Risks include brand dilution - the original image becomes blurred; cannibalization - the extension steals sales from the parent; failure spillover - a failed extension damages the parent brand; and consumer confusion. Line extensions also carry disadvantages such as weaker line logic, lower brand loyalty, and redistribution of market share rather than growth of category demand. Architecture choices - branded house, house of brands, endorsed brands, and sub-brands - help companies manage extension decisions systematically.

Key points:

  • A line extension introduces a new variant within an existing category under the existing brand - for example, Amul adding a new ice-cream flavour or Colgate launching a new toothpaste variant.
  • A brand extension (category extension) uses an established brand name to enter a completely new product category - for example, Maggi entering masala, or Patanjali moving from Ayurvedic products into food, personal care, and even clothing.
  • Success factors include a strong fit between the parent brand and the new category, a positive brand image, transferable associations, quality assurance by the parent, and adequate marketing support.
