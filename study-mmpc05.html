Block-01 Descriptive Statistics

Unit 1: Collection of Data

📖 Definition

Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing, and interpreting numerical data to aid decision-making. Data collection is the foundational first step — without reliable data, all subsequent analysis is meaningless.

▶
Data Collection Methods
▶
Primary vs Secondary Data

1. Primary Data vs Secondary Data

📱 Primary Data
  • Collected firsthand by the investigator for a specific purpose
  • Fresh, original, and tailored to the research problem
  • Costly and time-consuming to collect
  • Examples: Census of India enumerators visiting households; Nielsen conducting consumer surveys for Hindustan Unilever
📚 Secondary Data
  • Already existing data collected by someone else for a different purpose
  • Cheaper and faster to obtain
  • May not perfectly fit the current research need
  • Examples: RBI Bulletin, CMIE data, Company annual reports, Economic Survey of India

3. Observation Method

The investigator observes the phenomenon without direct questioning. Used when respondents cannot articulate their behaviour (e.g., traffic flow patterns at Delhi Metro stations, shelf-browsing behaviour in Reliance Retail stores). Can employ mechanical/electronic devices like CCTV, RFID tags, or website cookies.

4. Questionnaire Design Principles

⚠️ Exam Favourite

A well-designed questionnaire is the backbone of data collection. Follow these rules:

5. Sources of Secondary Data

GovernmentCensusRBICMIEWHOAnnual Reports
  • Government Sources: Census of India, Economic Survey, RBI Bulletin, Labour Bureau, Ministry of Statistics (MOSPI)
  • International Sources: World Bank, IMF, UNDP Human Development Report
  • Private Sources: CMIE (Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy), ICRA, CRISIL, Industry associations like NASSCOM, CII
💡 Indian Example

Flipkart's Big Billion Days: Flipkart collects primary data through real-time clickstream analysis, cart abandonment tracking, and post-purchase surveys. It also uses secondary data from CMIE Consumer Pyramids to understand spending patterns across income groups in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities.

🧠 Memory Aid

"PQRST" for data collection steps: Problem definition → Questionnaire design → Respondent selection → Survey execution → Tabulation of results.

Key Takeaways

Primary data is original but costly; secondary data is cheap but may not fit perfectly. Questionnaire design is a skill — keep it short, simple, and logical. Always pre-test before final deployment. In India, MOSPI and CMIE are gold-standard secondary data sources.

4. Ogives and Their Uses

Less-than Ogive: Plot upper class boundaries vs cumulative frequencies (rising curve). Greater-than Ogive: Plot lower class boundaries vs cumulative frequencies (falling curve). The point of intersection of the two ogives gives the median value.

💡 Indian Example

🧠 Memory Aid

5. Solved Example: Frequency Distribution

📝 Worked Problem

Q: Construct a frequency distribution for marks of 30 students: 45, 52, 67, 73, 81, 56, 62, 78, 85, 91, 48, 59, 71, 76, 83, 54, 65, 74, 79, 88, 42, 57, 68, 75, 82, 50, 63, 72, 77, 86. Use 5 classes.

Solution: Range = 91−42 = 49. Class width = 49/5 ≈ 10. Classes: 40-49, 50-59, 60-69, 70-79, 80-89, 90-99.

Frequencies: 40-49 → 3, 50-59 → 5, 60-69 → 5, 70-79 → 9, 80-89 → 6, 90-99 → 2. Total = 30. ✓

Key Takeaways

Proper classification (geographical, chronological, qualitative, quantitative) is the foundation. Histograms show frequency shape; ogives help find medians and percentiles. Always include title, axis labels, source, and footnotes in tables.

Unit 3: Measures of Central Tendency

📖 Definition

A measure of central tendency is a single value that represents the entire dataset — the "centre" or "typical" value. It condenses thousands of data points into one summary number for quick comparison.

▶
Mean Median Mode
▶
Measures of Central Tendency

1. Arithmetic Mean (AM)

Formula: X̄ = ΣX / n (individual series)  |  X̄ = ΣfX / Σf (frequency series)

Assumed Mean Method: X̄ = A + Σfd / Σf  |  Step Deviation: X̄ = A + h × Σfd' / Σf

✅ Merits
  • Based on all observations
  • Simple to calculate and understand
  • Best for further algebraic treatment
  • Most widely used in business
❌ Limitations
  • Heavily affected by extreme values
  • Cannot be used for open-ended series
  • Cannot be determined graphically
  • May not correspond to any actual data point

2. Median

The middle value when data is arranged in order. For grouped data: Median = L + [(n/2 − CF) / f] × h

⚠️ Exam Tip

Median is the positional average — it doesn't use the actual values, only their position. This makes it robust against outliers. Use median for income data (skewed by billionaires) and property prices.

3. Mode

The most frequently occurring value. For grouped data: Mode = L + [(f₁ − f₀) / (2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)] × h

Useful in business for identifying the most popular product size, best-selling price point, or peak demand hour.

4. Geometric Mean (GM) and Harmonic Mean (HM)

GM = ∛(x₁ × x₂ × ... × xₙ) — Used for average growth rates (CAGR of stock returns, GDP growth)

HM = n / Σ(1/xᵢ) — Used for average rates (speed, price-earnings ratios)

Relationship: AM ≥ GM ≥ HM (equality only when all values are identical)

MeasureFormula FocusBest Used ForAffected by Outliers?
Arithmetic MeanSum of valuesSymmetric data, further calculationsYes, highly
MedianMiddle positionSkewed data, income/price dataNo
ModeMost frequentIdentifying popular itemsNo
Geometric MeanProduct of valuesGrowth rates, ratiosModerately
Harmonic MeanReciprocal of valuesRates, speedsModerately
🧠 Memory Aid

"AM ≥ GM ≥ HM" — Think: "Always Greater, Humble." The arithmetic mean is always the largest, harmonic the smallest. For mean = median = mode, the distribution is perfectly symmetrical (normal).

Mean, Median & Mode — Position in Different Distributions
💡 Indian Example

Infosys Revenue Growth: If Infosys revenue grew 12% in Year 1, 8% in Year 2, and 15% in Year 3, the arithmetic average growth is (12+8+15)/3 = 11.67%. But the geometric mean = ∛(1.12×1.08×1.15) − 1 = 11.63% — more accurate for compounding growth rates.

Key Takeaways

Mean is the most popular but sensitive to outliers. Median is best for skewed data. Mode is best for categorical data. GM is the correct average for growth rates. The choice depends on data type and analysis purpose.

Unit 4: Measures of Variation and Skewness

📖 Definition

Dispersion measures how spread out data is from the central value. Two datasets can have the same mean but vastly different variability — dispersion captures this difference. Skewness measures the asymmetry of the distribution.

▶
Standard Deviation Variance
▶
Skewness and Kurtosis

1. Measures of Dispersion

MeasureFormulaMeritsLimitations
RangeMax − MinSimplest, quick calculationBased on only 2 values, affected by outliers
Quartile Deviation(Q₃ − Q₁) / 2Not affected by extreme valuesIgnores 50% of data
Mean DeviationΣ|Xᵢ − X̄| / nBased on all observationsNot suitable for further algebraic treatment
Standard Deviation√[Σ(Xᵢ − X̄)²/n]Most important, algebraically tractableAffected by outliers
CV (Coefficient of Variation)(σ/X̄) × 100Compares variability across different units/meansMeaningless if X̄ ≈ 0
⚠️ Exam Favourite

Standard Deviation is the most frequently tested topic. Key formula: σ = √[ΣfX²/n − (ΣfX/n)²]. Variance = σ². Remember: SD is in the same units as the original data; variance is in squared units.

2. Skewness

✅ Symmetrical

Mean = Median = Mode. Bell-shaped curve. Example: Heights of adult Indian males (approximately).

➡ Positive Skew

Mean > Median > Mode. Long tail to the right. Example: Income distribution in India (few billionaires pull the mean right).

⬅ Negative Skew

Mean < Median < Mode. Long tail to the left. Example: Exam scores in an easy test (most students score high, few fail).

📏 Measures

Karl Pearson: SK = 3(Mean − Median)/σ. Bowley: (Q₃ + Q₁ − 2×Med)/(Q₃ − Q₁). Kurtosis: Measures peakedness (leptokurtic, platykurtic, mesokurtic).

🧠 Memory Aid

For skewness direction: "Positive = Pull Right" — think of Ambani's income pulling the mean to the right. "SK = 3(Mean−Median)/σ" — the "3" comes from the empirical relationship Mean − Mode ≈ 3(Mean − Median).

💡 Indian Example

Comparing Stock Volatility: An investor comparing HDFC Bank (CV = 18%) vs Yes Bank (CV = 45%) can conclude that Yes Bank is far more volatile. The CV allows comparison even though their mean prices are vastly different (₹1,600 vs ₹18).

4. Solved Example: Standard Deviation

📝 Worked Problem

Q: Find SD of: 5, 8, 12, 15, 20.

Solution: Mean = (5+8+12+15+20)/5 = 60/5 = 12.

Deviations: −7, −4, 0, 3, 8. Squared: 49, 16, 0, 9, 64. Σ = 138.

Variance = 138/5 = 27.6. SD = √27.6 = 5.25. CV = (5.25/12)×100 = 43.75%.

Key Takeaways

Standard deviation is the gold standard for dispersion. CV allows comparison across different scales. Skewness tells you if the distribution is symmetric — critical for choosing the right statistical test. Positive skew is more common in economic data (income, prices).

2. Fundamental Rules

3. Bayes' Theorem

⚠️ Exam Favourite

P(Aᵢ|B) = [P(B|Aᵢ) × P(Aᵢ)] / ΣP(B|Aⱼ) × P(Aⱼ) — Bayes' theorem revises prior probabilities using new evidence (posterior probability). It is the foundation of Bayesian statistics and machine learning.

💡 Indian Example

Medical Diagnosis (Bayes' Theorem): A diagnostic test for dengue has 95% sensitivity (true positive rate) and 2% false positive rate. If dengue prevalence in Delhi during monsoon is 5%, and a patient tests positive, what's the probability they actually have dengue? Using Bayes: P(Dengue|+) = (0.95×0.05) / [(0.95×0.05) + (0.02×0.95)] = 0.0475 / 0.0665 = 71.4%. This is why doctors confirm with secondary tests!

🧠 Memory Aid

"Bayes = Backwards" — Bayes' theorem works backwards from effect to cause. Normal probability: Cause → Effect. Bayes: Effect → Cause. Think: "Given that the test is positive, what's the chance of having the disease?"

4. Solved Example: Bayes' Theorem

📝 Worked Problem

Q: A factory has 3 machines A, B, C producing 40%, 35%, 25% of items. Defect rates: A = 3%, B = 4%, C = 5%. An item is found defective — what is the probability it came from Machine B?

Solution: P(B|D) = P(D|B)×P(B) / [P(D|A)×P(A) + P(D|B)×P(B) + P(D|C)×P(C)]

= (0.04×0.35) / [(0.03×0.40) + (0.04×0.35) + (0.05×0.25)]

= 0.014 / (0.012 + 0.014 + 0.0125) = 0.014 / 0.0385 = 0.3636 (36.36%)

Probability Tree — Disease Testing Example

Key Takeaways

Classical probability assumes equally likely outcomes. Conditional probability captures dependency between events. Bayes' theorem is the most powerful tool — it updates beliefs with new evidence. Independence means P(A∩B) = P(A) × P(B).

Unit 6: Discrete Probability Distributions

📖 Definition

A discrete probability distribution assigns probabilities to each possible value of a discrete random variable. The two most important are the Binomial and Poisson distributions.

▶
Binomial Distribution
▶

1. Key Concepts

Random Variable: A function that assigns numerical values to outcomes of a random experiment. Expected Value: E(X) = Σx × p(x) — the long-run average. Variance: Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]² — measures spread.

2. Binomial Distribution

PropertyDetail

Binomial Applications

  • Number of customer arrivals at a SBI branch per hour
  • Number of defective chips per wafer at a semiconductor plant

🧠 Memory Aid

"Binomial = Batches, Poisson = Points" — Binomial counts successes in a fixed number of trials (batch of items). Poisson counts events at random points in time/space (arrivals, defects). When n is large and p is small, Binomial ≈ Poisson (with λ = np).

4. Solved Example: Binomial Distribution

📝 Worked Problem

Q: Defective rate at Tata Steel is 5%. In a sample of 10 items, find P(X = 2) defective items.

Solution: n = 10, p = 0.05, x = 2.

P(X=2) = C(10,2) × (0.05)² × (0.95)⁸ = 45 × 0.0025 × 0.6634 = 0.0746 (7.46%).

Mean = np = 10×0.05 = 0.5. Variance = np(1−p) = 0.475.

Key Takeaways

Binomial: fixed n trials, two outcomes, constant p. Poisson: rare events, mean = variance. Both are discrete. When n→∞ and p→0, Binomial converges to Poisson. Expected value is the weighted average of all possible outcomes.

Unit 7: Continuous Probability Distributions

📖 Definition

For continuous random variables, probability is defined over intervals (not specific points). The total area under the probability density function (PDF) curve equals 1. The three key distributions are Uniform, Normal, and Exponential.

▶
Normal Distribution
▶
Z-Score Standard Normal

1. Uniform Distribution

All values equally likely between a and b. f(x) = 1/(b−a). Mean = (a+b)/2. Variance = (b−a)²/12. Example: Random arrival time of a train between 10:00 and 10:30.

2. Normal Distribution

⚠️ Most Important Distribution

The Normal (Gaussian) Distribution is the most important distribution in statistics. Its bell-shaped curve appears in heights, weights, IQ scores, stock returns, measurement errors, and countless natural phenomena. Central Limit Theorem ensures that sample means are approximately normal for large n.

Properties: Symmetrical, Mean = Median = Mode, defined by μ and σ. 68-95-99.7 Rule: 68% within 1σ, 95% within 2σ, 99.7% within 3σ.

Standard Normal (Z): Z = (X − μ) / σ. Converts any normal distribution to the standard normal with μ=0, σ=1.

3. Exponential Distribution

Models time between events in a Poisson process. f(x) = λe^(−λx). Mean = 1/λ. Memoryless property: P(X > s+t | X > s) = P(X > t).

DistributionShapeParametersKey Use
UniformRectanglea, bRandom number generation, equal probability scenarios
NormalBell curveμ, σHeights, test scores, measurement errors, stock returns
ExponentialDecaying curveλWaiting times, equipment lifespan, inter-arrival times
💡 Indian Example

Z-Score in Practice: If the average package at IIM Ahmedabad is ₹30 lakh with SD ₹8 lakh, and a student gets ₹46 lakh, the Z-score = (46−30)/8 = 2.0. This student is in the top 2.5% of the batch (since P(Z>2) = 0.0228).

🧠 Memory Aid

"68-95-99.7" — the three-sigma rule. Think: "68% of students score near average, 95% within two deviations, and 99.7% within three — almost everyone." The Z-table is just a lookup for areas under the normal curve.

4. Solved Example: Z-Score Calculation

📝 Worked Problem

Q: TCS salaries are normally distributed with μ = ₹8 lakh, σ = ₹2 lakh. What % of employees earn between ₹6 lakh and ₹12 lakh?

Solution: Z₁ = (6−8)/2 = −1.0   Z₂ = (12−8)/2 = 2.0

P(−1 < Z < 2) = P(Z < 2) − P(Z < −1) = 0.9772 − 0.1587 = 0.8185 (81.85%)

Normal Distribution — 68-95-99.7 Rule

Key Takeaways

Normal distribution is the king of statistics — most tests assume normality. Z = (X−μ)/σ standardizes any normal variable. Exponential is for time-between-events. Uniform is for equal-likelihood scenarios.

Q: A startup can Launch (60% success → ₹50L profit, 40% fail → ₹20L loss) or Wait (guaranteed ₹10L). Calculate EMV for each.

Solution: EMV(Launch) = 0.60×50 + 0.40×(−20) = 30 − 8 = ₹22 lakh. EMV(Wait) = ₹10 lakh. Decision: Launch (higher EMV).

Decision Tree — Launch vs Wait

Key Takeaways

Block-03 Statistical Inference

Unit 9: Sampling Methods

📖 Definition

Sampling is the process of selecting a subset (sample) from a larger group (population) to make inferences about the population. A well-designed sample can represent millions of people with manageable cost and time.

▶
Sampling Methods
▶
Random Stratified Cluster Sampling

1. Census vs Sample

Census (Complete Enumeration)
  • Studies every unit in the population
  • Accurate but extremely expensive and time-consuming
  • India's Census: conducted every 10 years, costs ₹8,000+ crore
  • Impractical for destructive testing (testing every lightbulb)
Sample Survey
  • Studies a subset representing the population
  • Economical, quick, and manageable
  • NSSO surveys: 100,000+ households represent 1.4 billion Indians
  • Sampling error is inevitable but can be controlled

2. Sampling Methods

⚠️ Exam Favourite

Distinguish between probability sampling (every unit has a known, non-zero chance) and non-probability sampling (selection is subjective or convenient). Probability sampling is always preferred for statistical inference.

MethodHow It WorksBest ForIndian Example
Simple Random (SRS)Every unit has equal chance; lottery or random numbersHomogeneous populationsSelecting 50 employees randomly from TCS's 600,000 using employee ID numbers
SystematicSelect every kth unit after random start (k = N/n)Ordered listsEvery 10th customer exiting a Big Bazaar store for feedback
StratifiedDivide into strata, sample from each proportionallyHeterogeneous population with clear groupsNSSO dividing India into states, then sampling proportionally from urban/rural

3. Sample Size Determination

n = (Z²σ²) / e² where Z = confidence level (1.96 for 95%), σ = population SD, e = margin of error. Larger samples give more precision but cost more. The trade-off between accuracy and cost is a key managerial decision.

🧠 Memory Aid

"SSCC" for probability sampling: Simple random → Systematic → Cluster → Stratified. Remember: Stratified is "like a layer cake" (sample from each layer), Cluster is "like a bunch of grapes" (pick whole bunches).

Sampling Methods — Visual Comparison

Key Takeaways

Probability sampling is essential for valid inference. Stratified sampling reduces error when population has distinct subgroups. Cluster sampling is cost-effective for scattered populations. Sample size formula: n = Z²σ²/e².

Unit 10: Sampling Distributions

📖 Definition

A sampling distribution is the probability distribution of a statistic (like the sample mean X̄) computed from all possible samples of size n from a population. It is the bridge between descriptive and inferential statistics.

▶
Sampling Distribution
▶
Central Limit Theorem

1. Standard Error

The standard deviation of the sampling distribution — measures how much the sample statistic varies from sample to sample.

Standard Error of Mean

σx̄ = σ / √n

As n increases, SE decreases — more precise estimates.

Standard Error of Proportion

σp = √[p(1−p)/n]

Used for proportions (e.g., share of voters supporting a party).

2. Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

⚠️ Most Important Theorem

Regardless of the population distribution, the sampling distribution of the mean approaches a normal distribution as n increases (n ≥ 30 is sufficient). This is why the normal distribution is everywhere — it emerges from averaging any data!

Implications: Enables hypothesis testing, confidence interval construction, and Z-score calculations even when the population is not normally distributed.

3. Confidence Intervals

CI = X̄ ± Zα/2 × (σ/√n)

  • 90% CI: Z = 1.645 — "I'm 90% confident the true mean lies in this range"
  • 95% CI: Z = 1.96 — most commonly used
  • 99% CI: Z = 2.576 — wider interval, more confidence

When σ is unknown and n < 30, use t-distribution with (n−1) degrees of freedom. The t-distribution has heavier tails than normal, accounting for extra uncertainty.

💡 Indian Example

Exit Polls: During elections, TV channels survey ~50,000 voters (out of 900 million). Using CLT, they construct 95% confidence intervals: "Party A: 45% ± 2%" means the true vote share is between 43% and 47% with 95% confidence. This is why different channels show slightly different numbers.

🧠 Memory Aid

"CLT = Any Shape becomes Normal when Averaged" — No matter how skewed the original data (income, house prices), the distribution of sample means becomes normal. This is the "magic" that makes all of inferential statistics work.

4. Solved Example: Confidence Interval

📝 Worked Problem

Q: A sample of 100 Infosys employees has mean salary ₹12 lakh, σ = ₹3 lakh. Construct 95% CI for the population mean.

Solution: CI = X̄ ± Z × (σ/√n) = 12 ± 1.96 × (3/10) = 12 ± 1.96 × 0.3 = 12 ± 0.588.

95% CI = (₹11.412 lakh, ₹12.588 lakh). We are 95% confident the true mean lies in this range.

Key Takeaways

CLT is the foundation of inferential statistics — it justifies using normal-based tests. Standard error decreases with √n (diminishing returns to larger samples). Confidence intervals quantify uncertainty in estimates. Use t-distribution when σ is unknown and n is small.

3. Z-Test vs T-Test

Z-Test
  • σ is known OR n ≥ 30
  • Z = (X̄ − μ0) / (σ/√n)
  • Uses standard normal table
T-Test
  • σ is unknown AND n < 30
  • t = (X̄ − μ0) / (s/√n)
  • Uses t-table with (n−1) df

4. Types of Tests

Two-tailed: H₁: μ ≠ μ₀Right-tailed: H₁: μ > μ₀Left-tailed: H₁: μ < μ₀
💡 Indian Example

Testing Claim: Amul claims its ice cream tubs contain 500ml. A consumer group tests 36 tubs, finds X̄ = 495ml, s = 12ml. H₀: μ = 500, H₁: μ ≠ 500. Z = (495−500)/(12/6) = −2.5. At α = 0.05, critical Z = ±1.96. Since |−2.5| > 1.96, reject H₀ — the tubs are significantly underfilled!

🧠 Memory Aid

"H₀ is the status quo — you need strong evidence to reject it" — like "innocent until proven guilty." If p-value < α, reject H₀. Think: "If p is low, H₀ must go!"

5. Solved Example: Two-Tailed Z-Test

📝 Worked Problem

Q: A company claims its bulbs last 1000 hours. σ = 50. Sample of 36 bulbs: X̄ = 985. Test at α = 0.05.

Solution: H₀: μ = 1000, H₁: μ ≠ 1000. Z = (985−1000)/(50/6) = −15/8.33 = −1.80. Critical Z = ±1.96.

Since |−1.80| = 1.80 < 1.96, fail to reject H₀. There is insufficient evidence to reject the claim.

Hypothesis Testing — Two-Tailed Test (α = 0.05)

Key Takeaways

Always start with H₀ (null). Type I error (α) is set by the researcher (typically 0.05). Z-test for known σ or large n; t-test for unknown σ and small n. Two-tailed tests check for difference; one-tailed tests check for direction.

Unit 12: Chi-Square Tests

📖 Definition

The Chi-Square (χ²) test is a non-parametric test used for categorical data. It compares observed frequencies with expected frequencies to determine if there are significant differences or associations.

▶
Chi-Square Test
▶
Goodness of Fit Test

1. Chi-Square Statistic

χ² = Σ[(O − E)² / E] where O = Observed frequency, E = Expected frequency

Properties: Always positive, right-skewed, approaches normal as df increases. Mean = df, Variance = 2×df.

2. Three Types of Chi-Square Tests

TestPurposedf FormulaExample
Goodness of FitDoes observed data match a theoretical distribution?k − 1Are dice fair? (6 categories)
Test of IndependenceAre two categorical variables related?(r−1)(c−1)Is brand preference independent of gender?
Test of HomogeneityDo different populations have same proportions?(r−1)(c−1)Do different states have same preference pattern?

3. Conditions for Valid Chi-Square Test

  • All expected frequencies must be ≥ 5 (some texts say ≥ 1 with adjusted df)
  • Observations must be independent
  • Total observed = Total expected
  • Data must be in frequencies, not percentages
💡 Indian Example

Market Research (Independence Test): Marico wants to know if preference for Parachute vs parachute brand is independent of region (North/South/East/West). They survey 1,000 consumers. Using a 4×2 contingency table, if χ² calculated > χ² critical at (3)(1) = 3 df, they conclude that brand preference varies significantly by region — and should customize marketing accordingly.

Chi-Square Distribution — Right-Tailed Test
🧠 Memory Aid

"χ² = Sum of (What happened − What should happen)² / What should happen" — It measures the total squared deviation from expectation. Large χ² = big discrepancy = reject the null hypothesis of "no difference."

4. Solved Example: Goodness of Fit

📝 Worked Problem

Q: A die is rolled 120 times. Observed: {1:18, 2:22, 3:20, 4:25, 5:15, 6:20}. Is the die fair? (χ² critical at 5 df, α=0.05 = 11.07)

Solution: Expected = 120/6 = 20 for each face. χ² = (18−20)²/20 + (22−20)²/20 + (20−20)²/20 + (25−20)²/20 + (15−20)²/20 + (20−20)²/20

= 4/20 + 4/20 + 0/20 + 25/20 + 25/20 + 0/20 = 0.2+0.2+0+1.25+1.25+0 = 2.90.

Since 2.90 < 11.07, fail to reject H₀ — the die appears fair.

Key Takeaways

Chi-square works with frequencies (not means). Goodness of fit tests distribution shape. Independence tests relationship between two categorical variables. Always check that expected frequencies ≥ 5.

2. Karl Pearson's Correlation Coefficient (r)

r = Σ[(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)] / √[Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² × Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²]

|r| RangeStrengthExample
0.00 – 0.30WeakShoe size and IQ
0.30 – 0.70ModerateYears of education and income

3. Spearman's Rank Correlation (rₛ)

rₛ = 1 − [6Σd²] / [n(n² − 1)] — Used for qualitative data (rankings, ratings) or when data doesn't meet Pearson's assumptions.

4. Key Properties of r

  • Unitless: Ranges from −1 to +1
  • Symmetric: rxy = ryx
  • Independent of origin and scale: Adding a constant or multiplying by a constant doesn't change r
  • Measures only linear relationship: r = 0 doesn't mean no relationship (could be curvilinear)
  • Correlation ≠ Causation: Ice cream sales and drowning deaths are correlated (both increase in summer) but one doesn't cause the other
⚠️ Exam Favourite

"Correlation does not imply causation" — this is the most commonly tested concept. Two variables may be correlated due to: (1) Direct causation, (2) Reverse causation, (3) Common cause (confounding), or (4) Pure coincidence.

💡 Indian Example

Stock Market Correlation: The correlation between Nifty 50 and S&P 500 is approximately r = 0.65 — moderately positive. When US markets fall, Indian markets tend to follow (global contagion). But the correlation between Nifty and gold prices is approximately r = −0.30 — a mild negative relationship (gold is a safe haven when stocks fall).

5. Solved Example: Pearson's r

📝 Worked Problem

Q: Find r for: X = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}, Y = {2, 4, 5, 4, 5}.

Solution: X̄ = 3, Ȳ = 4. Σ(xi−x̄)(yi−ȳ) = (−2)(−2)+(−1)(0)+(0)(1)+(1)(0)+(2)(1) = 4+0+0+0+2 = 6.

Σ(xi−x̄)² = 4+1+0+1+4 = 10. Σ(yi−ȳ)² = 4+0+1+0+1 = 6.

r = 6 / √(10×6) = 6/√60 = 6/7.746 = 0.7746 — strong positive correlation.

Key Takeaways

Pearson's r for quantitative data, Spearman's rₛ for ranked data. r measures linear association only. |r| > 0.7 is strong. Correlation does not equal causation. r is unaffected by change of origin or scale.

Unit 15: Regression

📖 Definition

Regression analysis establishes a mathematical relationship between a dependent variable (Y) and one or more independent variables (X) to predict Y from X. While correlation tells you IF variables are related, regression tells you HOW they are related.

▶
Regression Analysis
▶
Least Squares Method

1. Simple Linear Regression

Y = a + bX where a = intercept (value of Y when X = 0), b = slope (change in Y per unit change in X)

Regression of Y on X

Y = a + b₁X

b₁ = r × (σy/σx)

a = ȳ − b₁x̄

Regression of X on Y

X = c + b₂Y

b₂ = r × (σx/σy)

c = x̄ − b₂ȳ

2. Key Properties

  • Both regression lines intersect at (x̄, ȳ) — the point of means
  • b₁ × b₂ = r² — product of regression coefficients equals square of correlation
  • If r = ±1, both lines coincide (perfect linear relationship)
  • Regression coefficients cannot change sign — if r is positive, both b₁ and b₂ are positive

3. Coefficient of Determination (R²)

R² = r² = Explained variation / Total variation

An R² of 0.85 means 85% of the variation in Y is explained by X. The remaining 15% is unexplained (due to other factors or random error).

4. Multiple Regression

Y = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + ... + bₖXₖ — Used when multiple factors influence the dependent variable.

Example: Predicting sales from advertising spend, price, competitor price, and seasonal index.

💡 Indian Example

Predicting House Prices: A regression model for Mumbai real estate: Price = 25 + 0.8×Area(sqft) + 15×Bedrooms − 5×Distance_from_station(km). R² = 0.82. Interpretation: Each additional sqft increases price by ₹800. Each km from station decreases price by ₹5 lakh. 82% of price variation is explained by these three factors.

🧠 Memory Aid

"b₁ × b₂ = r²" — The product of the two regression coefficients always equals r². If you know any two, you can find the third. Also: "Regression is the best linear predictor" — it minimizes the sum of squared errors (OLS = Ordinary Least Squares).

5. Solved Example: Regression Prediction

📝 Worked Problem

Q: Given: X̄ = 40, ȳ = 35, σₓ = 10, σᵧ = 12, r = 0.8. Find regression equation of Y on X and predict Y when X = 50.

Solution: b₁ = r × (σᵧ/σₓ) = 0.8 × (12/10) = 0.96. a = ȳ − b₁X̄ = 35 − 0.96×40 = 35 − 38.4 = −3.4.

Y = −3.4 + 0.96X. When X = 50: Y = −3.4 + 0.96(50) = −3.4 + 48 = 44.6.

Verify: b₁ × b₂ = r² → 0.96 × b₂ = 0.64 → b₂ = 0.667.

Regression — Scatter Plot with Best Fit Line

Key Takeaways

Regression quantifies the relationship; correlation only measures its strength. R² tells you how well the model fits (0 to 1). The regression line always passes through the means. b₁ × b₂ = r² is a key identity for exams.

📝 Worked Problem

Forecast for May = S₄ = 114.04
