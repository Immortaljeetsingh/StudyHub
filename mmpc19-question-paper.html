Solved Answers for MMPC-19 Question Paper

Q1. Which fundamental ideas underpin Total Quality Management (TQM)? Explain how TQM is different from conventional methods of quality management.

Answer: TQM is built on customer focus, total employee involvement, process-centred thinking and continual improvement, and it differs from conventional quality management by preventing defects instead of detecting them.

Quality itself has multiple definitions: Deming calls it predictable uniformity and dependability, Juran calls it "fitness for use", Crosby calls it "conformance to requirements", and Feigenbaum treats it as the total composite of product characteristics. Second, TQM rests on eight principles: customer focus, total employee involvement, process-centred thinking, an integrated system, a strategic approach, continual improvement, fact-based decisions, and open communications. Third, quality evolved in four stages: inspection (1920s, detecting defects), statistical quality control (1940s, preventing defects with control charts), quality assurance (1960s, systematic processes), and TQM (1980s, company-wide quality).

TQM differs from conventional quality management in four ways. Conventional methods inspect finished goods to sort the good from the bad; TQM builds quality into the process so defects never occur. In conventional systems, quality is the quality department's job; under TQM every employee is responsible. Conventional firms merely meet standards; TQM firms aim to exceed customer expectations. Finally, conventional management fire-fights problems, while TQM uses the PDCA cycle for never-ending improvement. Toyota's production system is the classic example: every worker can pull the andon cord to stop the line when they spot a defect, which is impossible in a conventional inspection-based system.

Key points to remember: TQM fundamentals = customer focus, total employee involvement, continual improvement, fact-based decisions. TQM prevents defects instead of detecting them, involves everyone instead of one department, and improves continuously instead of fire-fighting.
Takeaway: Answer in two halves — fundamentals first (definitions + 8 principles), then four contrasts: prevent vs detect, everyone vs one department, exceed vs meet, PDCA vs fire-fighting.

Q2. How does leadership fit into the TQM implementation process? Explain with the help of examples.

Answer: Leadership is the number one enabler of TQM — leaders supply the vision, resources and culture without which quality initiatives fail.

Leaders in TQM perform six roles: set the quality vision, nurture a quality culture, allocate resources, remove barriers, recognize achievements, and lead by example. Deming's 14 points capture this in "institute leadership" and "drive out fear" — leaders must support rather than supervise.

The leadership styles that best fit TQM are transformational leadership (inspiring people beyond expectations), servant leadership (the leader serves employees and removes obstacles), and empowering leadership (giving frontline workers authority for quality decisions). The difference between "managing quality" and "leading quality" matters: managing means controlling systems and processes, while leading means building vision and culture. At Tata Steel, the CEO personally participates in quality circle presentations, signalling that quality is a top priority. Top management also forms the steering committee that oversees the entire TQM structure — Steering Committee → Quality Council → TQM Coordinator → Quality Circles. The Ritz-Carlton example shows leadership in practice: the chain empowers every employee to spend up to $2,000 per guest to resolve quality issues immediately.

Key points to remember: Leadership commitment is the foundation of TQM. Leaders set vision, nurture culture, allocate resources and lead by example; transformational and servant styles work best; Tata Steel and Ritz-Carlton show leadership-driven TQM.
Takeaway: Open with "without top management commitment TQM fails", list the six leadership roles, then one Indian (Tata Steel) and one global (Ritz-Carlton) example.

Q3. Explain with the help of examples, how an organization will guarantee continuous TQM improvement.

Answer: Organizations guarantee continuous improvement by institutionalizing the PDCA cycle, kaizen culture, DMAIC projects and control mechanisms that make improvement a permanent habit.

The basic cycle is Deming's PDCA: Plan (identify the problem and plan a solution), Do (implement on a small scale), Check (evaluate results), Act (standardize or revise). Running PDCA repeatedly creates never-ending improvement. Kaizen adds a cultural layer — small improvements by everyone, every day, rather than occasional big breakthroughs. Six Sigma's DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control) provides a rigorous project-based route to improvement, and ISO 9001 makes improvement a formal requirement in Clause 10.

Organizations guarantee the habit through concrete mechanisms. Standard operating procedures lock in improvements so gains are not lost. Control charts (with limits of mean ± 3σ) monitor processes continuously so any special-cause variation is caught immediately. Regular internal quality audits verify that improvements are actually being followed. Employee suggestion schemes and quality circles give workers a permanent channel to propose improvements. Training and self-improvement (Deming point 13) keep skills current. GE guarantees improvement through Six Sigma: Jack Welch required all managers to be Six Sigma trained, and the company saved $12 billion over five years. Toyota's kaizen culture runs thousands of small improvements each year, and Motorola achieved 3.4 defects per million opportunities by applying the same discipline.

Key points to remember: Continuous improvement is guaranteed by PDCA cycles, Kaizen culture, DMAIC projects, ISO 9001 Clause 10, control charts, audits, suggestion schemes and training — with GE and Toyota as proof that the habit delivers billions in savings.
Takeaway: Draw the PDCA wheel first, then list the guarantee mechanisms (SOPs, control charts, audits, suggestions, training) and close with GE & Toyota numbers.

Q4. Describe the standard tools and methods which are employed in TQM.

Answer: TQM employs the seven basic quality tools, statistical quality control (control charts and capability indices), acceptance sampling and advanced methods like DMAIC, 5S, QFD, FMEA and poka-yoke.

The seven basic quality tools (mnemonic CCCHPSF) are: Cause-and-Effect (Fishbone) Diagram to identify root causes, Check Sheet for systematic data collection, Control Chart to monitor process stability over time, Histogram to show frequency distribution, Pareto Chart to prioritize problems using the 80/20 rule, Scatter Diagram to test relationships between variables, and Flowchart to map process steps.

Statistical Quality Control adds control charts — X-bar and R charts for variables (central tendency and dispersion) and p and c charts for attributes (proportion defective and number of defects) — with control limits at mean ± 3σ. Process capability is measured with Cp = (USL − LSL)/6σ and Cpk = min[(USL − μ)/3σ, (μ − LSL)/3σ]; a Cp of 1.33 means a capable process, and 2.0 means Six Sigma capability. Acceptance sampling distinguishes producer's risk α (rejecting a good lot) from consumer's risk β (accepting a bad lot).

Advanced methods include Six Sigma's DMAIC, 5S workplace organization (Sort, Set in Order, Shine, Standardize, Sustain), QFD (translating customer "whats" into technical "hows" via the House of Quality), FMEA (prioritizing potential failures by Risk Priority Number), Poka-Yoke error-proofing, and quality circles — small voluntary groups of 5-10 employees solving problems. For example, a bottle cap factory with USL = 26.4mm, LSL = 25.6mm, mean 26.0mm and σ = 0.1mm gets Cp = 1.33, meaning the process is capable.

Key points to remember: Seven basic tools (CCCHPSF), control charts, Cp/Cpk capability indices, acceptance sampling, plus DMAIC, 5S, QFD, FMEA, Poka-Yoke and quality circles form the standard TQM toolkit.
Takeaway: Structure the answer as three layers — 7 basic tools, SQC (charts + Cp/Cpk), advanced methods — and reproduce the bottle-cap Cp = 1.33 worked example.

Q5. Does TQM implementation affect the organizational culture? Justify with the help of examples.

Answer: Yes — TQM deeply changes organizational culture, and culture change is actually a precondition for TQM success.

Concretely, TQM changes culture in observable ways. Decisions move from gut feeling to data-driven decisions. Communication becomes open across departments instead of siloed. Employees move from "leave quality to the inspectors" to a zero-defect mindset where everyone owns quality. Recognition programs replace fear of punishment, which is exactly Deming's advice to "drive out fear" and "remove barriers to pride of workmanship." The Ritz-Carlton hotel chain exemplifies this: employees are trusted with $2,000 per guest to fix problems, which only works in a culture of empowerment and customer obsession. At Tata Steel, the CEO attending quality circle presentations embeds quality as a top priority in the culture. Toyota's culture of worker empowerment — any worker can stop the assembly line with the andon cord — could not exist in a conventional command-and-control culture. On the other hand, TQM fails when leadership commitment is absent, which is why culture change must start from the top.

Key points to remember: TQM transforms culture from blame to learning, from silos to teamwork, from intuition to data — evidenced by Ritz-Carlton's empowerment, Tata Steel's leadership participation and Toyota's andon cord culture.
Takeaway: Say "yes, and it is a precondition"; give the three culture shifts (blame→learning, silos→teams, intuition→data) plus three company examples.

Q6. What is the role of workers in the TQM implementation process? Illustrate with the help of examples.

Answer: Workers are the heart of TQM implementation — quality is created at the point of work, so employees must own, stop, improve and solve quality problems themselves.

They participate in quality circles — voluntary groups of 5-10 employees who meet regularly (often weekly) to identify and solve production problems, as pioneered by Ishikawa. They are empowered to stop work when they detect defects, rather than passing problems downstream. They run kaizen improvements, suggest small changes every day, and they receive continuous on-the-job training (Deming's point 6: "institute training on the job").

Examples show how powerful the worker's role is. At Toyota, any worker can pull the andon cord to stop the entire assembly line if they spot a defect — this bottom-up authority is credited with Toyota's legendary quality. At Ritz-Carlton, frontline staff can spend up to $2,000 per guest to resolve a complaint immediately, without asking a manager. Tata Steel runs quality circles where frontline workers present improvements directly to the CEO. GE made Six Sigma training mandatory for all managers and employees, and its workforce delivered $12 billion in savings. The role of workers also includes feeding data into quality tools — they complete check sheets, contribute causes to fishbone diagrams, and use control charts. In short, TQM converts workers from passive operators into active problem-solvers.

Key points to remember: Workers are quality owners in TQM: they join quality circles, stop defects at source, run kaizen, receive training, and solve problems — as shown by Toyota's andon cord, Ritz-Carlton's $2,000 empowerment and Tata Steel's quality circles.
Takeaway: One-line answer + four roles (circles, stop defects, kaizen, training), then prove each with Toyota, Ritz-Carlton, Tata Steel and GE examples.

Q7. What impact does TQM have on customer satisfaction? Elucidate with the help of suitable examples.

Answer: TQM raises customer satisfaction directly — customer focus drives quality, and quality reduces costs and wins market share, loyalty and profit (Deming's chain reaction).

Deming's chain reaction explains the logic: improve quality → costs decrease (fewer defects, less rework) → productivity improves → market share increases → the organization stays in business. Satisfied customers stay loyal, buy more, and recommend the product to others, which is far cheaper than winning new customers.

TQM also protects customers from the hidden costs of poor quality. Cost of Poor Quality (COPQ) is typically 15-25% of sales revenue, and only 3-5% of it is visible (warranty, scrap, rework); the rest is hidden — lost customers, damaged reputation, and low employee morale. By investing in prevention (the cheapest cost category, "PAIE": Prevention < Appraisal < Internal Failure < External Failure), TQM avoids external failures like recalls and warranty claims that destroy customer trust. Examples: Toyota's TQM system built a reputation for reliability that translates into among the highest customer loyalty in the auto industry. Ritz-Carlton, a two-time Baldrige winner, ties every process to guest satisfaction and empowers staff to fix problems instantly. Xerox used benchmarking in the 1980s to match Japanese competitors' quality and recover lost customers. On the reverse side, Volkswagen's emission scandal (Dieselgate) shows how one quality and ethics failure erased years of customer goodwill.

Key points to remember: TQM boosts customer satisfaction through customer focus, Deming's chain reaction (quality → market share), and prevention-driven quality costs; Toyota, Ritz-Carlton and Xerox show the payoff, Dieselgate shows the cost of failure.
Takeaway: Draw the chain reaction and the PAIE pyramid, quote COPQ 15-25%, then give one positive (Toyota) and one negative (Dieselgate) example.

Q8. Write short notes on the following: (a) Quality audit (b) Certification.

Answer: A quality audit is a systematic, independent examination that verifies the QMS conforms and works, while certification is formal recognition by an accredited body that the system meets a standard such as ISO 9001.

(a) Quality audit: There are three types based on who conducts them: first-party (internal) audits, where the organization assesses itself; second-party audits, where a customer evaluates a supplier; and third-party audits, where an independent certification body verifies compliance. The audit process follows seven steps: Planning, Opening Meeting, Document Review, On-site Audit, Closing Meeting, Audit Report, and Follow-up (mnemonic "POD-OC-F"). Key terms include nonconformity (failure to meet a requirement), corrective action (action to eliminate the cause of a detected nonconformity), and observation (a finding that could become a nonconformity). For ISO 9001 certification, audits happen in two stages: Stage 1 checks readiness (documentation review) and Stage 2 verifies implementation (on-site).

(b) Certification: Quality certification is formal recognition by an accredited body that an organization's management system meets a standard such as ISO 9001, the world's most recognized QMS standard. The certification process has seven steps: Application, Document Review, Stage 1 Audit, Stage 2 Audit, Certification Decision, Surveillance Audits, and Re-certification (mnemonic "A-D-S-S-C-S-R"). The certificate is valid for three years, with annual surveillance audits in between, followed by full re-certification. ISO 9001:2015 is organized into 10 clauses built on seven quality management principles (customer focus, leadership, engagement of people, process approach, improvement, evidence-based decisions, relationship management). In India, BIS (Bureau of Indian Standards) provides product certification — the ISI mark for products and Hallmark for gold — as a national equivalent of quality certification.

Key points to remember: Audits come in three parties (internal, customer, certifier) over seven steps (POD-OC-F); certification runs A-D-S-S-C-S-R with 3-year validity, and ISO 9001 is the most recognized QMS standard, with BIS doing this work in India.
Takeaway: Two mini-answers, each with its mnemonic: audit = 3 parties + POD-OC-F; certification = A-D-S-S-C-S-R + 3-year cycle + BIS in India.

Q1. What do you understand by quality? Discuss the different dimensions of quality.

Answer: Quality is fitness for use (Juran) or conformance to requirements (Crosby), and it has eight product and service dimensions that customers perceive.

Key points:

  • Definitions: Deming — predictable uniformity and dependability; Juran — fitness for use; Crosby — conformance to requirements; Feigenbaum — total composite of product characteristics.
  • Garvin's eight dimensions: performance, features, reliability, conformance, durability, serviceability, aesthetics and perceived quality.
  • Quality is defined by the customer, not by the producer — quality and grade are different (a budget car and a luxury car can both be high quality).
  • Quality has a time dimension too: quality of design, quality of conformance and quality of performance.
  • Example: Maruti Suzuki's small cars deliver reliability and fuel economy at their price point — high quality in their grade.
Takeaway: Start with one definition, list Garvin's eight dimensions, and add "quality is defined by the customer".

Q2. Discuss Deming's 14 points of quality management.

Answer: Deming's 14 points are management principles that transform an organization from an inspection-based, fear-driven system to a learning, quality-driven system.

Key points:

  • Constancy of purpose — improve products and services to become competitive and stay in business.
  • Adopt the new philosophy; cease dependence on mass inspection; end lowest-tender purchasing; improve constantly.
  • Institute training on the job; institute leadership (supervisors must lead, not supervise); drive out fear.
  • Break down barriers between departments; eliminate slogans, targets and quotas that punish people; remove barriers to pride of workmanship.
  • Institute education and self-improvement for everyone; take action to accomplish the transformation.
  • The 14 points rest on the PDCA cycle and the system of profound knowledge (appreciation of system, theory of variation, theory of knowledge, psychology).
Takeaway: Group the 14 points into themes — purpose/philosophy, prevention over inspection, training and leadership, drive out fear, teamwork, pride of workmanship — and connect them to PDCA.

Q3. Explain the concept of quality costs and discuss its categories.

Answer: Quality costs are the total costs incurred to achieve quality plus the costs of failing to achieve it, classified as PAIE — prevention, appraisal, internal failure and external failure.

Key points:

  • Prevention: training, quality planning, design review, supplier development — the cheapest category.
  • Appraisal: inspection, testing, calibration, auditing — costs of checking conformance.
  • Internal failure: scrap, rework, downgrading, downtime — defects found before shipment.
  • External failure: warranty claims, returns, recalls, lost customers — the costliest category, discovered by customers.
  • COPQ is typically 15-25% of sales, mostly hidden; investing in prevention shrinks the total (Crosby: "quality is free").
Takeaway: Quote the PAIE order, the 15-25% figure and the rule "prevention is cheaper than cure".

Q4. What is Statistical Quality Control? Explain control charts for variables and attributes.

Answer: SQC applies statistical methods — mainly control charts — to keep processes stable, capable and within specification limits.

Key points:

  • Control limits are set at mean ± 3σ; points beyond them signal a special cause that needs action.
  • Variables (measured) charts: X-bar chart monitors the mean, R chart monitors dispersion of sample ranges.
  • Attributes (counted) charts: p chart monitors proportion defective, c chart monitors number of defects per unit.
  • Common causes are part of the process (management's job); special causes are assignable (operator's job).
  • Process capability: Cp = (USL − LSL)/6σ; Cpk adjusts for off-centering; Cp ≥ 1.33 means capable.
Takeaway: X-bar/R for variables, p/c for attributes; draw the chart with one out-of-control point and explain mean ± 3σ.

Q5. Explain the PDCA cycle and its application in continuous improvement.

Answer: PDCA (Plan-Do-Check-Act) is Deming's four-step improvement cycle that, when repeated, produces never-ending quality improvement.

Key points:

  • Plan: define the problem, collect data, analyse causes and plan a solution.
  • Do: implement the plan on a small scale or trial basis.
  • Check: measure results against the plan and study the gap.
  • Act: standardize what worked (SOP) or revise the plan and cycle again.
  • Applications: kaizen events, ISO 9001 process approach, DMAIC projects, and every quality circle meeting.
Takeaway: "Plan, small Do, Check against plan, Act to standardize" — the wheel turns forever.

Q6. Explain the role of top management in TQM implementation.

Answer: Top management drives TQM through vision, policy, resources, structure and personal example — TQM without top commitment is a slogan, not a system.

Key points:

  • Sets the quality vision, mission and policy and translates them into measurable quality objectives.
  • Forms the steering committee (CEO + functional heads) and the quality council that oversees implementation.
  • Allocates resources — budget, training, time — and removes barriers between departments.
  • Leads by example (Deming point 1: constancy of purpose) and recognizes quality achievements.
  • Example: Tata Steel's CEO personally attends quality circle presentations; Ritz-Carlton's leadership ties every process to guest satisfaction.
Takeaway: Structure — Steering Committee → Quality Council → TQM Coordinator → Quality Circles — is the top-management backbone of TQM.

Q7. What is ISO 9000? Discuss the requirements of ISO 9000.

Answer: ISO 9000 is the family of quality management system standards; ISO 9001:2015 sets the certifiable requirements built on 10 clauses and 7 quality management principles.

Key points:

  • Family: ISO 9000 (fundamentals and vocabulary), ISO 9001 (requirements), ISO 9004 (sustained success beyond 9001).
  • ISO 9001:2015 has 10 clauses; clauses 4-10 are auditable (context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation, improvement).
  • Built on the process approach, PDCA and risk-based thinking.
  • Seven principles: customer focus, leadership, engagement of people, process approach, improvement, evidence-based decisions, relationship management.
  • Certification by accredited bodies, valid 3 years with surveillance audits; in India BIS offers equivalent product certification (ISI mark).
Takeaway: 9000 = basics, 9001 = requirements (10 clauses, 7 principles), 9004 = excellence; draw the pyramid in the exam.

Q8. Write short notes on: (a) Quality circles (b) Kaizen.

Answer: Quality circles are small voluntary worker groups that solve workplace problems, while kaizen is the habit of continuous small-step improvement by everyone.

Key points:

  • (a) Quality circles: 5-10 members from the same work area, meeting regularly (usually weekly) to identify and solve quality problems; pioneered by Ishikawa; supported by facilitators and management recognition; example: Tata Steel's circles present improvements to the CEO.
  • (b) Kaizen: continuous improvement in small increments (vs big breakthroughs); targets the 3-Mu — Muda (waste), Mura (unevenness), Muri (overburden).
  • Kaizen uses 5S, suggestion schemes and standard work; a kaizen event delivers quick wins on a focused process.
  • Both depend on PDCA and training; together they convert workers into problem-solvers.
Takeaway: Circles = structure for participation; kaizen = culture of small daily improvements; mention 3-Mu and 5S.

Q1. Define quality. Explain the evolution of quality management.

Answer: Quality is customer-defined fitness for use, and quality management evolved in four stages from inspection to company-wide TQM.

Key points:

  • Stage 1 - Inspection (1920s): sort good from bad at the end of the line; detection only.
  • Stage 2 - Statistical Quality Control (1940s): Shewhart's control charts prevent defects; Deming takes SQC to Japan.
  • Stage 3 - Quality Assurance (1960s): systematic quality planning, systems and standards; Juran's trilogy, Crosby's zero defects.
  • Stage 4 - TQM (1980s): company-wide, customer-driven, continuous improvement; Feigenbaum's total quality control.
  • India: BIS standards and the ISI mark paralleled this evolution from the 1950s.
Takeaway: Four-stage timeline with one name per stage — Shewhart, Deming/Juran, Crosby, Feigenbaum.

Q2. Discuss Juran's contribution to quality management (the Quality Trilogy).

Answer: Juran defined quality as fitness for use and structured quality management as a trilogy — quality planning, quality control and quality improvement.

Key points:

  • Quality planning: identify customers, determine needs, design product/process to meet them.
  • Quality control: feedback loop — choose control subject, measure, compare with standards, act on the difference.
  • Quality improvement: project-by-project breakthroughs, guided by Pareto (vital few vs trivial many).
  • Juran's 80/20 rule: most quality problems come from a few causes; managers own most problems, not workers.
  • Juran's spiral of progress: quality advances through a continuous sequence of activities across functions.
Takeaway: "Fitness for use" + trilogy (plan, control, improve) + 80/20 — Juran's exam fingerprint.

Q3. What are the seven quality tools? Explain the Pareto chart with an example.

Answer: The seven tools (CCCHPSF) turn data into decisions; the Pareto chart applies the 80/20 rule to focus on the vital few causes.

Key points:

  • Seven tools: Check sheet, Pareto chart, Fishbone diagram, Histogram, Scatter diagram, Control chart, Flowchart.
  • Pareto: rank causes of defects from highest to lowest frequency; the top few usually account for most losses.
  • Example: in a call centre, wrong billing (42%) and long hold time (28%) cause 70% of complaints — fix these two first.
  • Pareto is drawn as descending bars with a cumulative percentage line; it prioritizes, it does not explain causes.
  • Pair Pareto with fishbone: Pareto finds what to fix, fishbone finds why it happens.
Takeaway: Draw descending bars + cumulative line + 80% marker; "Pareto prioritizes, fishbone explains".

Q4. Discuss the concept of process capability (Cp and Cpk).

Answer: Process capability measures whether a stable process can meet specification limits: Cp = (USL − LSL)/6σ and Cpk adjusts for off-centering.

Key points:

  • Cp = (USL − LSL)/6σ — compares specification width with natural process spread (6σ).
  • Cpk = min[(USL − μ)/3σ, (μ − LSL)/3σ] — falls below Cp when the process is off-centre.
  • Interpretation: Cp < 1 incapable; 1.33 capable; 1.67 excellent; 2.0 Six Sigma capability.
  • Worked example: USL 26.4, LSL 25.6, μ 26.0, σ 0.1 → Cp = 0.8/0.6 = 1.33 (capable).
  • Capability is measured only after the process is stable (in statistical control).
Takeaway: Write both formulas, the 1.33 threshold and the bottle-cap example — a guaranteed marks-winner.

Q5. Explain benchmarking and its different types.

Answer: Benchmarking is measuring processes against the best performers and adapting their practices to close the gap.

Key points:

  • Types: internal (best unit within the same firm), competitive (direct rivals), functional (same function, different industry), generic (world-class processes anywhere).
  • Steps: plan what to benchmark → collect data → analyse the gap → adapt best practice → implement → review.
  • Example: Xerox benchmarked L.L. Bean's warehousing; hospitals benchmark FedEx-style logistics.
  • Benchmarking feeds ISO 9001 improvement (Clause 10) and strategic planning; follow benchmarking ethics (share, don't steal).
Takeaway: Four types in a 2x2 grid with one example each — internal, competitive, functional, generic.

Q6. What is Six Sigma? Explain the DMAIC methodology.

Answer: Six Sigma is a data-driven improvement methodology targeting 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO), executed through the DMAIC project cycle.

Key points:

  • Define: project charter, problem statement, customer (CTQ) requirements.
  • Measure: collect baseline data, measure the current performance.
  • Analyze: identify root causes with fishbone, Pareto, hypothesis tests.
  • Improve: design and pilot solutions for the root causes.
  • Control: monitor with control charts, standardize, lock in gains.
  • Origins: Motorola (1986), popularized by GE's Jack Welch ($12 billion savings in 5 years); belts: green, black, master black.
Takeaway: One-line definition (3.4 DPMO) + the five-phase flow + Motorola/GE credits.

Q7. Discuss the concept of quality system audit and its types.

Answer: A quality system audit is a systematic, independent examination that verifies the QMS conforms to plans and is effectively implemented.

Key points:

  • Types: first-party (internal) audits by the organization; second-party by a customer evaluating a supplier; third-party by an independent certification body.
  • Seven steps: Planning, Opening Meeting, Document Review, On-site Audit, Closing Meeting, Audit Report, Follow-up (POD-OC-F).
  • Findings: nonconformity, observation, and opportunities for improvement; corrective action fixes the root cause.
  • ISO 19011 guides audit management; ISO 9001 certification uses Stage 1 (readiness) and Stage 2 (implementation) audits.
  • Audits are planned, systematic and evidence-based — not fault-finding exercises.
Takeaway: "Three parties, seven steps (POD-OC-F)" — that single line carries the answer.

Q8. Write short notes on: (a) FMEA (b) Poka-Yoke.

Answer: FMEA anticipates failures before they occur using a Risk Priority Number, while poka-yoke error-proofs processes so defects cannot happen.

Key points:

  • (a) FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis): for each potential failure identify effect, cause, severity (S), occurrence (O) and detection (D); RPN = S × O × D; fix the highest RPNs first.
  • (b) Poka-Yoke: mistake-proofing devices invented by Shigeo Shingo — prevention-type (defect cannot start) and detection-type (defect flagged immediately).
  • Examples: SIM card cut corner (fits only one way), petrol nozzle that cannot fit diesel cars, washer with a hole aligned to the tap.
  • Both support the zero-defects goal and reduce inspection by eliminating errors at source.
Takeaway: RPN = S×O×D for FMEA; "error-proofing, not error-detecting" for poka-yoke.

  • Examples: GE's mandatory Six Sigma ($12 billion saved in 5 years); Toyota's thousands of annual kaizen improvements.
Takeaway: PDCA wheel + five guarantee mechanisms + GE/Toyota proof points.

Q4. Describe the standard tools and methods which are employed in TQM.

Answer: TQM's toolkit is the seven basic tools (CCCHPSF) plus SQC, capability indices, acceptance sampling and advanced methods — DMAIC, 5S, QFD, FMEA, poka-yoke and quality circles.

Key points:

  • Seven tools: check sheet, Pareto, fishbone, histogram, scatter, control chart, flowchart.
  • SQC: X-bar and R charts (variables), p and c charts (attributes), limits at mean ± 3σ.
  • Capability: Cp = (USL − LSL)/6σ; Cpk for off-centre processes; 1.33 capable, 2.0 Six Sigma.
  • Sampling: producer's risk α vs consumer's risk β in acceptance sampling.
  • Advanced: DMAIC, 5S, QFD (House of Quality), FMEA (RPN), poka-yoke, quality circles of 5-10 employees.
Takeaway: Three layers — tools, SQC, advanced — plus the Cp = 1.33 bottle-cap example.

Q5. Does TQM implementation affect the organizational culture? Justify with the help of examples.

Answer: Yes — TQM transforms culture from blame to learning, silos to teamwork and intuition to data; culture change is both precondition and outcome.

Key points:

  • Building blocks force the shift: leadership, customer focus, involvement, improvement, supplier partnership, performance measures.
  • Decisions become data-driven; communication opens across departments; zero-defect mindset replaces "leave it to inspectors".
  • Recognition replaces fear — Deming: "drive out fear", "remove barriers to pride of workmanship".
  • Examples: Ritz-Carlton's $2,000 empowerment; Tata Steel CEO at quality circles; Toyota's andon cord culture.
  • Without leadership commitment the culture shift fails — so it must start at the top.
Takeaway: "Yes, and it is a precondition" + three culture shifts + three company examples.

Q6. What is the role of workers in the TQM implementation process? Illustrate with the help of examples.

Answer: Workers are quality owners — they stop defects at source, join quality circles, run kaizen, feed data into tools and keep learning.

Key points:

  • Total employee involvement is one of the eight TQM principles; quality is created at the point of work.
  • Quality circles: voluntary groups of 5-10 meeting weekly to solve problems (Ishikawa).
  • Empowerment to stop work on defects; daily kaizen suggestions; training (Deming point 6).
  • Examples: Toyota's andon cord; Ritz-Carlton's $2,000 per-guest authority; Tata Steel circles presenting to the CEO; GE's $12 billion workforce-driven savings.
Takeaway: "Workers = problem-solvers, not operators" + four roles + four examples.

Q7. What impact does TQM have on customer satisfaction? Elucidate with the help of suitable examples.

Answer: TQM raises customer satisfaction through customer focus and prevention — quality cuts costs, wins loyalty and market share (Deming's chain reaction).

Key points:

  • Customer defines quality; Deming's chain: quality → lower costs → productivity → market share → survival.
  • COPQ is 15-25% of sales, only 3-5% visible; PAIE ordering makes prevention the priority.
  • Examples: Toyota's reliability and loyalty; Ritz-Carlton (two-time Baldrige winner); Xerox's benchmarking comeback.
  • Counter-example: Volkswagen's Dieselgate erased years of customer goodwill in one scandal.
Takeaway: Chain reaction + COPQ figure + one positive and one negative example.

Q8. Write short notes on the following: (a) Quality audit (b) Certification.

Answer: A quality audit is a systematic independent check that the QMS conforms and works; certification is accredited recognition that the QMS meets a standard like ISO 9001.

Key points:

  • (a) Audit types: first-party (internal), second-party (customer-supplier), third-party (certification body).
  • (a) Seven steps: Planning, Opening Meeting, Document Review, On-site Audit, Closing Meeting, Audit Report, Follow-up (POD-OC-F).
  • (a) Findings: nonconformity, observation; corrective action removes root causes; ISO 9001 uses Stage 1 + Stage 2 audits.
  • (b) Certification steps: Application, Document Review, Stage 1, Stage 2, Certification Decision, Surveillance, Re-certification (A-D-S-S-C-S-R).
  • (b) Certificate valid 3 years with annual surveillance; ISO 9001:2015 has 10 clauses and 7 principles; BIS provides ISI and Hallmark in India.
Takeaway: Two mnemonics — POD-OC-F for audit, A-D-S-S-C-S-R for certification; audit verifies, certification proves.
