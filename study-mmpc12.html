Block-01: Strategy Fundamentals

Unit 1: Concept of Strategy

📖 Strategy — Origin & Definitions

Origin: Greek word Strategos — Stratos (army) + agos (to lead). Originally military, later adapted to business. Without strategy, an organization is "like a ship without a rudder."

Glueck: "Unified, comprehensive and integrated plan relating strategic advantage to environmental challenges, designed to ensure basic objectives through proper implementation."

Chandler (1960): "Determination of basic long-term goals + adoption of courses of action + allocation of resources."

Porter: "Creation of a unique and valued position involving a different set of activities."

Unit 2: Strategic Framework

📖 Strategic Intent & Hierarchy

Strategic Intent (Hamel & Prahalad, 1989): Obsessive ambition for success, resource allocation, organizing efforts, motivating people toward goals.

Hierarchy: Vision → Mission → Business Definition → Objectives → Goals

Strategic Management Process — Detailed:
  • Step 1: Strategic Intent: Define vision, mission, and long-term objectives. Establish direction.
  • Step 2: Environmental Analysis: Scan external (PESTLE) and internal (SWOT, VRIN) environments. Identify opportunities, threats, strengths, weaknesses.
  • Step 3: Strategy Formulation: Develop business-level (cost leadership, differentiation, focus) and corporate-level (growth, stability, retrenchment) strategies.
  • Step 4: Strategy Implementation: Allocate resources, design structure, manage change, develop policies and programs.
  • Step 5: Evaluation & Control: Monitor performance, compare with standards, take corrective action. Use Balanced Scorecard, KPIs.
Vision vs Mission — Key Differences:
  • Vision: Future-oriented, aspirational, inspirational. Answers "Where do we want to be?" Timeless, rarely changes.
  • Mission: Present-oriented, operational, descriptive. Answers "What business are we in?" More specific, may evolve.
  • Good Vision: Brief, clear, inspiring, stable over time. Example: Google's "Organize the world's information and make it universally accessible"
  • Good Mission: Defines customers, products/services, technology, philosophy. Example: Tata's "Improving the quality of life of the communities we serve"
Strategic Intent — Examples:
  • Komatsu (vs Caterpillar): "Encircle Caterpillar" — aggressive goal to overtake market leader
  • Canon (vs Xerox): "Beat Xerox" — focused all resources on copier technology
  • Honda: "We will crush, squash, and slaughter Yamaha" — competitive warfare
  • Infosys: "Win in the flat world" — leveraging globalization

📖 Environmental Scanning & EFE Matrix

Environmental Scanning: Systematic collection and interpretation of environmental data for strategy formulation. Identifies opportunities and threats.

EFE Matrix (External Factor Evaluation): Weighted rating of external factors (opportunities + threats). Steps: List key external factors → Assign weights (0 to 1) → Rate each (1-4) → Multiply weight x rating → Sum total.

  • Opportunities: Emerging markets, technology shifts, regulatory changes, demographic shifts
  • Threats: New competitors, substitute products, economic downturns, unfavorable policy changes
  • Weight range: 0.0 (not important) to 1.0 (very important) — all weights must sum to 1.0
  • Rating scale: 1 = poor response, 2 = average, 3 = above average, 4 = superior response
  • Interpretation: Total weighted score above 2.5 = strong external position; below 2.5 = weak
I/O Model vs RBV — Key Differences:
  • I/O (Outside-In): External environment determines strategy. Industry structure > firm resources. Focus on industry attractiveness. Example: Choosing to enter growing industries (Indian EdTech, EV market)
  • RBV (Inside-Out): Internal resources drive competitive advantage. Firm resources > industry structure. Focus on core competencies. Example: Tata leveraging brand trust across industries
  • Best Approach: Combine both — external analysis identifies opportunities, internal analysis determines capability to exploit them

Unit 5: Competitive Analysis

📖 Porter's Five Forces

5 Forces: Threat of New Entrants, Bargaining Power of Suppliers, Bargaining Power of Buyers, Threat of Substitutes, Competitive Rivalry.

Developed by Michael Porter (1979), this framework helps analyze industry attractiveness and profitability potential. The stronger the forces, the lower the industry's profit potential.

  • Economies of scale (lower cost at higher volume)
  • Learning/Experience curve effects
  • Cost disadvantages independent of scale (patents, locations, raw materials)
  • Brand loyalty and identity
  • Capital requirements
  • Switching costs (financial or psychological)
  • Access to distribution channels
  • Anticipated retaliation from incumbents
  • Regulatory policies and tariffs
Force 2: Bargaining Power of Suppliers — High When:
  • Few suppliers dominate (high concentration)
  • No substitutes for supplier's product
  • Switching costs are high for buyers
  • Credible threat of forward integration

  • Undifferentiated/standard products
  • Buyers are price-sensitive
  • Buyers have accurate cost information
  • Concentrated buyers (few large buyers)
  • Credible threat of backward integration

  • Whether substitutes are attractively priced
  • Whether buyers view substitutes as satisfactory in quality/performance
  • How easily buyers can switch
Force 5: Competitive Rivalry — Intensity Factors:
  • Stability of environment (unstable = hyper-competitive)
  • Life expectancy of competitive advantage (short = more rivalry)

  • Industry growth rate
  • Fixed costs and exit barriers

⚠ Force 1: Threat of New Entrants — Entry Barriers
  • Economies of scale: Lower cost at higher volume deters small entrants
  • Learning/Experience curve: Incumbents have cost advantage from experience
  • Cost disadvantages: Patents, locations, raw materials independent of scale
  • Brand loyalty: Established brands create switching costs for customers
  • Capital requirements: High upfront investment needed to compete
  • Switching costs: Financial or psychological costs for buyers to change
  • Access to distribution: Incumbents control key channels
  • Anticipated retaliation: Incumbents may respond aggressively to new entrants
  • Regulatory policies: Tariffs, licensing requirements, government regulations
⚠ Force 2: Bargaining Power of Suppliers — High When
  • Few suppliers dominate the market (high concentration)
  • No substitutes for the supplier's product exist
  • Switching costs are high for buyers
  • Credible threat of forward integration (supplier enters buyer's industry)
  • Buyer is not important to supplier's total business
⚠ Force 3: Bargaining Power of Buyers — High When
  • Products are undifferentiated or standardized
  • Buyers are highly price-sensitive
  • Buyers have accurate cost and market information
  • Few large buyers dominate (concentrated buyers)
  • Credible threat of backward integration (buyer enters supplier's industry)
⚠ Force 4: Threat of Substitutes
  • Whether substitutes are attractively priced relative to industry product
  • Whether buyers view substitutes as satisfactory in quality and performance
  • How easily buyers can switch to the substitute
⚠ Force 5: Competitive Rivalry Intensity
  • Environmental stability: Unstable environments = hyper-competitive
  • Advantage life expectancy: Short-lived advantages = more rivalry
  • Number and diversity: More competitors = higher rivalry
  • Industry growth rate: Slow growth = more fight for market share
  • Fixed costs and exit barriers: High fixed costs = price wars to fill capacity
📖 Strategic Groups & CPM

Strategic Groups: Conceptual clusters of firms with similar strategies within an industry (e.g., Cars vs MUVs vs Sports Cars in automobile industry). Firms within a group are closer competitors.

Competitive Profile Matrix (CPM): Comparing a firm's critical success factors vs competitors using weighted scores. Factors include market share, brand loyalty, financial position, product quality, customer loyalty, global expansion.

Unit 6: Internal Analysis

📖 RBV & VRIN Framework

Resource-Based View (RBV): Internal resources and capabilities drive competitive advantage. Inside-out perspective.

VRIN (Barney): Resources must be Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Non-substitutable.

SWOT Analysis — Detailed Breakdown:
  • Strengths (S): Internal positive attributes that give competitive advantage — brand reputation, skilled workforce, proprietary technology, financial resources, efficient processes
  • Weaknesses (W): Internal negative factors that hinder performance — high debt, poor brand image, skill gaps, outdated technology, weak distribution
  • Opportunities (O): External factors the firm can exploit — market growth, technological changes, regulatory changes, competitor weaknesses, new segments
  • Threats (T): External factors that could cause trouble — new competitors, changing regulations, economic downturn, shifting consumer preferences, substitute products
IFE Matrix (Internal Factor Evaluation):
  • Step 1: List key internal factors (strengths and weaknesses)
  • Step 2: Assign weights (0.00 to 1.00) based on importance
  • Step 3: Rate each factor (1=Major weakness, 2=Minor weakness, 3=Minor strength, 4=Major strength)
  • Step 4: Multiply weight x rating = weighted score
  • Step 5: Sum all weighted scores (2.5 = average, above = strong, below = weak)
EFE Matrix (External Factor Evaluation):
  • Similar to IFE but for external opportunities and threats
  • Step 1: List key external factors
  • Step 2: Assign weights (0.00 to 1.00) based on importance
  • Step 3: Rate each factor (1=Poor response, 2=Average response, 3=Above average, 4=Superior response)
  • Step 4: Calculate weighted scores and total

📖 IFE Matrix & Core Competencies

Core Competencies (Prahalad & Hamel): Unique strengths that provide competitive advantage. Must meet VRIN criteria. Examples: Honda (engines), 3M (innovation), Apple (design + ecosystem). Core competencies are the glue that holds the organization together.

  • Competencies: Bundle of skills distinguishing from competitors
  • Core Competencies: Unique strengths providing sustainable competitive advantage (VRIN met)
  • Dynamic capabilities: Ability to integrate, build, reconfigure resources for changing environments

Block-03: Strategy Formulation

Unit 7: Business Level Strategies

📖 Porter's Three Generic Strategies

Cost Leadership: Become lowest-cost producer. Differentiation: Offer unique products. Focus: Serve specific niche (cost focus or differentiation focus).

"Stuck in the Middle": Failing to achieve ANY generic strategy = poor performance.

Cost Leadership — Detailed:
  • Become lowest-cost producer in industry while maintaining acceptable quality
  • Serve price-sensitive customers with competitive pricing
  • Prerequisites: Efficient scale, cost reduction from experience curve, tight cost control, marginal account avoidance
  • Sources: Economies of scale, learning curve effects, technology, supply chain optimization
  • Risks: Technology changes making investments obsolete, imitations by competitors, focus on cost ignoring other factors
  • Indian Examples: IndiGo Airlines (operational efficiency), Jio (data at lowest cost), Maruti Suzuki (economies of scale)
Differentiation — Detailed:
  • Offer unique products or services valued by customers, commanding premium prices
  • Sources: Product features, technology innovation, brand image, customer service, distribution network, design
  • Benefits: Customer loyalty, reduced price sensitivity, higher margins, competitive barriers
  • Risks: Price premium too high, differentiation basis becomes less important to buyers, imitation by competitors
  • Indian Examples: Apple (design + ecosystem), BMW (engineering + luxury), Tata (trust + quality), Amul (cooperative model)
Focus Strategy — Detailed:
  • Serve specific market segment (niche) better than competitors serving broad market
  • Can be Cost Focus (lowest cost in niche) OR Differentiation Focus (unique value in niche)
  • Target Segments: Geographic, demographic, product-line segment, customer type
  • Risks: Segment becomes too broad, competitors find sub-segments, differences between segment and broad market shrink
  • Indian Examples: Rolex (luxury niche), Tesla (EV niche initially), Dollar stores (price-sensitive niche), FabIndia (ethnic wear niche)
SBU (Strategic Business Unit):
  • Each business unit treated as independent with its own strategy, mission, competitors
  • Has its own set of competitors, distinct from other SBUs
  • Has a manager responsible for strategic planning and profit performance
  • Example: Reliance Industries has separate SBUs: Jio, Retail, Petrochemicals, Oil & Gas

Unit 8: Competitive Strategy

📖 Competitor Analysis Framework

5-Point Framework: Future Objectives → Current Strategy → Assumptions → Capabilities → Response Profile.

Wheel of Competitive Strategy:
  • Goals at center, functional areas as spokes: Finance, Production, Marketing, HR, Engineering, etc.
  • Each functional area must align with overall competitive strategy
  • Competitive strategy = Goals (ends) + Policies (means) for competing in specific business
Industry Types & Strategy:
  • Fragmented Industry: Many small firms, no dominant player. Strategy: Consolidation (M&A), Specialization (niche focus). Example: Indian restaurants, local retail
  • Emerging Industry: Newly formed or reformed industry. Strategy: First mover advantage, technological leadership, building barriers. Example: Indian EV market, EdTech
  • Declining Industry: Growth has stopped, demand falling. Strategy: Leadership (dominate remaining market), Niche (focus on segments), Harvest (maximize cash), Divest (exit). Example: Landline telecom, DVD rental
  • Mature Industry: Slow growth, established players. Strategy: Cost efficiency, incremental innovation, market share defense. Example: Indian FMCG, steel industry
Competitive Dimensions:
  • Cost: Price competitiveness, operational efficiency
  • Quality: Product reliability, features, performance
  • Flexibility: Ability to adapt, customize, respond to changes
  • Delivery: Speed, reliability of delivery
  • Service: Customer support, after-sales service

Unit 9: Corporate Level Strategy

📖 Corporate Strategy

Question: "What businesses should we be in?"

4 Strategies: Growth, Stability, Retrenchment, Combination.

BCG Matrix — Detailed Quadrant Analysis:
  • Stars (High Growth, High Share): Market leaders in growing industries. Need heavy investment to maintain position. Eventually become cash cows when market matures. Example: Jio in early years (2016-2019), Reliance Retail
  • Cash Cows (Low Growth, High Share): Market leaders in mature, slow-growing markets. Generate more cash than needed. Fund other business units. Example: TCS, HUL's detergent business, Maruti Suzuki
  • Question Marks (High Growth, Low Share): Attractive industries but weak market position. Require significant investment to gain share. Decision: invest heavily or divest. Example: Tata Motors' EV business, Reliance's new energy ventures
  • Dogs (Low Growth, Low Share): Weak position in slow-growing markets. Typically divested or liquidated. May generate enough cash to sustain but don't grow. Example: Landline telephony, legacy IT services
Vertical Integration — Detailed:
  • Forward Integration: Moving toward the customer (downstream). Manufacturer opens retail stores, acquires distributors. Example: Apple opening Apple Stores, Tata launching Tata Cliq
  • Backward Integration: Moving toward raw materials (upstream). Manufacturer acquires suppliers. Example: Tata Steel acquiring coal mines, Reliance acquiring textile mills
  • Benefits: Better coordination, cost savings, quality control, supply security
  • Risks: Increased capital requirements, reduced flexibility, bureaucratic costs
Stability Strategies:
  • Pause/Proceed with Caution: Temporary stop to consolidate resources before next growth phase
  • No Change: Continue current operations in stable environment (rare in practice)
  • Profit: Capitalize on existing position, generate maximum cash flow
  • Harvesting: Maximize short-term cash flow, may sacrifice market share
Retrenchment Strategies:
  • Turnaround: Aggressive cost reduction + revenue improvement when decline is not severe. Example: Air India under Tata Group restructuring
  • Divestiture: Sell off business units to focus on core operations. Example: Tata Group selling Corus Steel UK
  • Liquidation: Sell assets and close business (last resort). Example: Kingfisher Airlines shutdown

📖 Growth Strategies in Detail
  • Concentration: Focus on existing business, increase market share in current market
  • Integration: Expand within the same industry
  • Vertical Integration: Forward = toward customer (manufacturer opens retail); Backward = toward supplier (manufacturer acquires raw material source)
  • Diversification: Enter new businesses — Related (shared technology/marketing) or Unrelated (conglomerate)
  • International: Enter new geographic markets through exporting, licensing, JV, or FDI
📖 Stability Strategies
  • Pause/Proceed with Caution: Temporary stop to consolidate before next growth phase
  • No Change: Continue as-is in a stable environment
  • Profit: Capitalize on existing position, generate cash for future investment
  • Harvesting: Maximize cash flow, may sacrifice market share intentionally
📖 Retrenchment Strategies
  • Turnaround: Aggressive cost reduction + revenue improvement when decline is not severe
  • Divestiture: Sell off non-core or underperforming business units
  • Liquidation: Sell assets and close business — last resort
📖 Combination Strategy

Mix of growth, stability, and retrenchment applied across different business units simultaneously or sequentially. Large diversified corporations like Reliance, Tata Group, and Adani Group typically use combination strategies.

Block-04: Strategy Implementation & Governance

Unit 10: Strategy Implementation

📖 McKinsey 7S Framework

7S = SSSS-SSS: Strategy, Structure, Systems (Hard) + Style, Staff, Skills, Shared Values (Soft).

Implementation = Action. Strategy without implementation is just a wish.

McKinsey 7S — Detailed Element Breakdown:
  • Strategy (Hard): Plan to build competitive advantage. Must be clear, consistently communicated, and adaptive to change.
  • Structure (Hard): How organization is organized. Types: Functional (by department), Divisional (by product/region), Matrix (dual reporting), Network (outsourced/virtual).
  • Systems (Hard): Daily procedures, processes, and workflows. Includes IT systems, reward systems, financial systems, quality systems.
  • Style (Soft): Leadership and management style. Includes leadership approach, decision-making style, organizational culture.
  • Staff (Soft): Employees and their capabilities. Includes recruitment, training, development, retention, motivation.
  • Skills (Soft): Competencies and capabilities of the organization. Includes core competencies, distinctive capabilities, knowledge base.
  • Shared Values (Soft - Central): Core values and culture that guide the organization. The foundation that connects all other elements.
Organizational Structure Types:
  • Functional: Grouped by function (marketing, finance, HR, operations). Advantages: specialization, efficiency. Disadvantages: silos, slow response.
  • Divisional: Grouped by product, region, or customer. Advantages: accountability, flexibility. Disadvantages: duplication, competition between divisions.
  • Matrix: Dual reporting (functional + project). Advantages: resource sharing, flexibility. Disadvantages: confusion, power struggles.
  • Network: Outsourced, virtual organization. Advantages: flexibility, cost savings. Disadvantages: less control, coordination challenges.
Resource Allocation for Implementation:
  • Financial Resources: Budget allocation aligned with strategic priorities
  • Human Resources: Right people in right roles, training and development
  • Physical Resources: Facilities, equipment, technology infrastructure
  • Technological Resources: IT systems, R&D capabilities, innovation capacity
Change Management:
  • Resistance Sources: Fear of unknown, loss of status, disruption of routines, lack of trust
  • Overcoming Resistance: Communication, participation, education, facilitation, negotiation, coercion (last resort)
  • Kotter's 8-Step Model: Create urgency, Form coalition, Create vision, Communicate vision, Empower action, Create quick wins, Build on change, Anchor in culture

Unit 11: Corporate Governance

📖 Corporate Governance

Definition (Cadbury, 1992): System by which organizations are directed and controlled.

5 Pillars: Accountability, Fairness, Transparency, Integrity, Social Responsibility.

Evolution of Corporate Governance:
  • 3rd Century BC: Chanakya's Arthashastra — governance principles for statecraft
  • 1870: German Company Law — early corporate regulation
  • 1992: UK Cadbury Committee — "system by which organizations are directed and controlled"
  • 1999: OECD Principles of Corporate Governance
  • 2002: Sarbanes-Oxley Act (US) — response to Enron/WorldCom scandals
  • 2004: Revised OECD Principles
  • 2013: Companies Act 2013 (India) — strengthened governance norms
5 Pillars — Detailed:
  • Accountability: Owning rewards and risks at all levels. Directors accountable to shareholders, management accountable to board.
  • Fairness: Treating all stakeholders equally. Equal access to information, equal voting rights, minority shareholder protection.
  • Transparency: Disclosing all relevant information to stakeholders. Timely financial reporting, disclosure of related party transactions, risk disclosure.
  • Integrity: Professional culture where each employee is valued. Ethical conduct, honesty, freedom from fraud and corruption.
  • Social Responsibility: Decisions benefiting organization and society. Environmental protection, community development, sustainable practices.
Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX) 2002 — Key Provisions:
  • Section 302: CEO/CFO must certify financial statements
  • Section 404: Internal controls over financial reporting must be assessed
  • Section 802: Criminal penalties for destroying evidence
  • Section 906: Criminal penalties for misleading financial reports
  • PCAOB: Public Company Accounting Oversight Board created
Business Ethics in Governance:
  • Application of ethical principles in business decisions
  • Wealth maximization (long-term stakeholder value) > Profit maximization (short-term shareholder returns)
  • Descriptive ethics: What is (current state of ethics in organization)
  • Normative ethics: What should be (ethical standards and principles)
  • Ethical decision-making frameworks: Utilitarian, Rights-based, Justice-based, Virtue ethics

Unit 12: Strategic Leadership

📖 Strategic Leadership

Definition: Ability to anticipate, envision, maintain flexibility, empower others to create strategic change.

Key Roles: Navigator, Strategist, Entrepreneur, Talent Developer.

Key Roles of Strategic Leaders:
  • Navigator: Charting the course, understanding environment, identifying opportunities and threats
  • Strategist: Long-term planning, setting direction, creating competitive advantage
  • Entrepreneur: Innovation and risk-taking, exploring new opportunities, driving change
  • Talent Developer: Building future leaders, mentoring, developing organizational capabilities
Top Management Team:
  • CEO: Chief Executive Officer — overall strategic direction and leadership
  • CFO: Chief Financial Officer — financial strategy, risk management, investor relations
  • COO: Chief Operating Officer — operational efficiency, day-to-day management
  • CTO: Chief Technology Officer — technology strategy, innovation, R&D
  • Diversity: Diverse backgrounds improve strategic decisions, reduce groupthink
Strategic Decision Making Models:
  • Rational: Comprehensive analysis, optimal choice. Assumes complete information, clear objectives. Best for stable environments.
  • Incremental: Small steps, trial and error. Adjusts as information becomes available. Best for uncertain environments.
  • Mixed Scanning: Combines rational (fundamental decisions) + incremental (routine decisions). Balances thoroughness with practicality.
Corporate Governance in Practice:
  • Board of Directors: Oversees management, sets strategic direction, ensures accountability
  • Shareholders: Exercise voting rights, elect board, approve major decisions
  • Audit Committees: Oversee financial reporting, internal controls, external audits
  • Nomination Committees: Identify and recommend board candidates
  • Compensation Committees: Set executive pay, align incentives with performance

Unit 13: Contemporary Issues in Strategy

📖 Emerging Trends

Digital Strategy: E-commerce, platform businesses, data-driven decisions.

Sustainability: Triple Bottom Line — People, Planet, Profit.

Disruptive Innovation (Christensen): New entrants disrupt incumbents with simpler, cheaper solutions.

Blue Ocean (Kim & Mauborgne): Create uncontested market space.

Digital Strategy — Detailed:
  • E-commerce: Online selling, digital payments, last-mile delivery. Example: Flipkart, Amazon India, Nykaa
  • Platform Businesses: Multi-sided platforms connecting users. Example: Ola, Uber, Zomato, Swiggy
  • Data-Driven Decisions: Analytics, AI/ML for strategic insights. Example: Netflix recommendations, Jio's data analytics
  • Digital Transformation: Integrating digital technology into all business areas. Example: HDFC Bank's digital banking, Reliance's Jio platforms
Sustainability Strategy — Triple Bottom Line:
  • People: Social responsibility, fair labor practices, community development
  • Planet: Environmental protection, carbon neutrality, sustainable sourcing
  • Profit: Economic viability, long-term value creation
  • Examples: ITC's e-Choupal (social + profit), Tata's sustainability initiatives, Infosys' carbon neutrality
Innovation Strategy:
  • Disruptive Innovation (Christensen): New entrants disrupt incumbents with simpler, cheaper solutions. Incumbents focus on high-end customers, leaving low-end open. Example: Jio disrupted telecom with free data, Xiaomi disrupted smartphones with value pricing
  • Blue Ocean Strategy (Kim & Mauborgne): Create uncontested market space instead of competing in existing markets. Example: Cirque du Soleil (circus + theatre), Yellow Cab (app-based taxi in India before Ola/Uber)
  • Agile Strategy: Rapid adaptation, iterative planning, short cycles. Respond quickly to market changes. Example: Indian startups pivoting during COVID-19
Mergers & Acquisitions (M&A):
  • Horizontal: Same industry, same stage (competitor). Example: HDFC Bank + Centurion Bank, Vodafone + Idea
  • Vertical: Different stages of value chain. Example: Reliance acquiring content platforms for Jio
  • Conglomerate: Unrelated businesses. Example: Tata Group acquisitions across industries
  • Benefits: Synergies, market power, diversification, access to resources
  • Risks: Integration challenges, cultural clashes, overpayment, regulatory issues
Dynamic Capabilities (Teece):
  • Ability to integrate, build, reconfigure resources to match changing environment
  • Sensing: Identifying opportunities and threats in the environment
  • Seizing: Mobilizing resources to capture opportunities
  • Transforming: Renewing and reconfiguring organizational resources
  • Example: Tesla sensing EV opportunity, seizing with Gigafactories, transforming automotive industry
Knowledge Management:
  • Creating, sharing, using organizational knowledge as competitive asset
  • Explicit Knowledge: Documented, codified (manuals, databases, patents)
  • Tacit Knowledge: Personal, experience-based (skills, insights, intuitions)
  • Example: Infosys's Knowledge Management System, TCS's global delivery model knowledge base
