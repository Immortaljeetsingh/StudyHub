Answer to Q1: Entrepreneurship, Creative Destruction and Entrepreneur vs Manager

Answer: Entrepreneurship is risk-taking plus opportunity recognition that creates value; Schumpeter's creative destruction says innovation destroys the old equilibrium, and the entrepreneur differs from the manager in bearing risk and owning the venture.

Entrepreneurship is the capacity to assume risk, accountability and challenges in order to explore opportunities, disrupt market norms and create value. Entrepreneurship thinking has evolved through three eras: risk-bearing (the rational agent view from economics), personality traits (from psychology), and opportunity recognition (from the late 1990s). Joseph Schumpeter, the most cited thinker in this subject, introduced creative destruction: the entrepreneur innovates by introducing new products, processes, markets or organizational forms that destroy the old equilibrium and replace it with a new one. Dhirubhai Ambani personified this — he started Reliance with ₹15,000 in 1966, entered petrochemicals when India was still socialist, and disrupted the textile industry through backward integration. Other thinkers include Cantillon (risk-bearing), Adam Smith (profit-seeking enterpriser), Jean-Baptiste Say (coordination of production factors), McClelland (need for achievement), Kirzner (opportunity discovery) and Shane and Venkataraman (opportunity-based entrepreneurship).

An entrepreneur differs from a manager: the entrepreneur bears risk, is opportunity-oriented, is motivated by achievement, treats innovation as a core function, and owns the venture; the manager avoids risk, is resource-oriented, is motivated by power, follows routine, and works as an employee. Elon Musk (own ventures, personal risk) is an entrepreneur; Sundar Pichai (innovating within Google’s resources) is an intrapreneur.

Key points:

  • Entrepreneurship = risk + opportunity + value creation (three eras: risk-bearing, trait-based, opportunity-based).
  • Schumpeter's creative destruction: innovation destroys old equilibrium and builds a new one.
  • Thinker chain C-S-S-G-K-S: Cantillon (risk), Say (coordination), Schumpeter (innovation), Gartner (organizations), Kirzner (discovery), Shane (opportunity).
  • Entrepreneur vs manager: bearer vs avoider of risk; opportunity vs resource oriented; achievement vs power motivation.
  • Intrapreneur = entrepreneur inside an existing organization (company bears the risk).

Takeaway: Quote Schumpeter first, then contrast entrepreneur (risk, ownership) with manager (routine, salary).

Answer to Q2: The 12 Entrepreneurial Competencies and the Intrapreneur

Answer: Competencies are learnable characteristics that produce superior performance; the 12 entrepreneurial competencies (I-S-P-I-C-C-E-S-P-P-I-U) and the entrepreneur-vs-intrapreneur contrast explain why some founders outperform.

Competencies are learnable characteristics that lead to effective performance, and successful entrepreneurs share 12 of them, remembered by the mnemonic "I-S-P-I-C-C-E-S-P-P-I-U". They are: (1) Initiative — acting without being told; (2) Seeing and acting on opportunities — market awareness with quick response, as Zomato showed by spotting food-delivery demand; (3) Persistence — never giving up, like Jack Ma, rejected many times before founding Alibaba; (4) Information seeking — research-driven decisions; (5) Concern for quality — excellence orientation like Apple; (6) Commitment to work contract — meeting deadlines; (7) Efficiency orientation — cost-effective, lean thinking; (8) Systematic planning — plans with milestones; (9) Problem solving — finding root causes; (10) Self-confidence — pitching to investors with conviction; (11) Persuasion — influencing customers and partners; and (12) Use of influence strategies — networking and alliances. Ritesh Agarwal of OYO demonstrated nearly all of them, starting at 17 and persuading SoftBank to invest. These competencies are developed through education, mentorship, practical experience and case studies.

An intrapreneur is an entrepreneur inside an existing organization: the company bears the risk, provides the resources, and rewards the intrapreneur with salary and bonus, while autonomy is limited by corporate structure. An entrepreneur starts his own venture, bears personal financial risk, mobilizes his own resources and keeps the profit.

Key points:

  • 12 competencies: Initiative, Seeing opportunities, Persistence, Information seeking, Concern for quality, Commitment, Efficiency, Systematic planning, Problem solving, Self-confidence, Persuasion, Influence strategies.
  • Competencies are learnable — built via education, mentorship, experience and simulations.
  • Example: Ritesh Agarwal (OYO) showed initiative + persuasion; Jack Ma showed persistence.
  • Intrapreneur vs entrepreneur: company bears risk and gives resources vs personal risk and self-mobilized resources.
  • Reward: salary + bonus for intrapreneur; profit ownership for entrepreneur.

Takeaway: Write the mnemonic I-S-P-I-C-C-E-S-P-P-I-U first, then two examples — one per competency group.

Answer to Q3: Dimensions of Entrepreneurship and Social Entrepreneurship

Answer: Entrepreneurship has seven dimensions (Social, Rural, Women, Group, Strategic, Techno, Education); social entrepreneurship runs a business to solve social problems, measured by the double or triple bottom line.

Entrepreneurship manifests in seven dimensions, remembered as "S-R-W-G-S-T-E". Social entrepreneurship solves social problems through business — Grameen Bank by Muhammad Yunus. Rural entrepreneurship runs enterprises in rural areas for local development — village handicraft cooperatives. Women entrepreneurship covers businesses owned and managed by women with at least 51 per cent ownership — Kiran Mazumdar-Shaw’s Biocon. Group entrepreneurship is collective ventures with pooled resources — the Amul cooperative model. Strategic entrepreneurship integrates strategy with entrepreneurship — corporate venture capital arms. Techno entrepreneurship builds technology-based ventures — Infosys and Flipkart. Education entrepreneurship creates knowledge-based businesses — coaching platforms and EdTech firms.

Social entrepreneurship uses business principles to solve social problems while remaining financially sustainable. It works on a double bottom line (social impact + financial sustainability) and ideally a triple bottom line (People + Planet + Profit). Aravind Eye Care is the classic Indian example: free eye care for poor patients is cross-subsidized by paying patients, and the model treats millions every year. Amul is another: a dairy cooperative founded in 1946 that now supports 3.6 million farmers while being India’s largest food brand.

Key points:

  • 7 dimensions (S-R-W-G-S-T-E): Social, Rural, Women, Group, Strategic, Techno, Education.
  • Women entrepreneurship test: 51% ownership, control and management by women.
  • Group entrepreneurship = pooled resources and shared risk (Amul cooperative).
  • Social entrepreneurship = social mission + business model; Grameen Bank, Aravind Eye Care.
  • Double bottom line = social + financial; triple bottom line = People + Planet + Profit.

Takeaway: Social entrepreneurship is the most-asked type — always pair the definition with Aravind/Amul and the bottom lines.

Answer to Q4: MSME Classification, Government Schemes and Economic Contribution

Answer: MSMEs are classified Micro (₹1 cr investment / ₹5 cr turnover), Small (₹10 cr / ₹50 cr) and Medium (₹50 cr / ₹250 cr); schemes like MUDRA, PMEGP and Startup India support them, and they contribute 30% GDP, 45% manufacturing, 40% exports.

MSMEs — Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises — are the backbone of the Indian economy, classified by investment in plant and machinery and by annual turnover, remembered as "M-S-M = 1-10-50": Micro up to ₹1 crore investment and ₹5 crore turnover; Small up to ₹10 crore investment and ₹50 crore turnover; Medium up to ₹50 crore investment and ₹250 crore turnover. The government supports entrepreneurs through several schemes: the MSME Development Act, 2006 provides the legal framework; Make in India pushes manufacturing; Startup India gives incentives to new ventures; Stand-Up India promotes SC/ST and women entrepreneurship; MUDRA Yojana provides loans up to ₹10 lakh, and has sanctioned over ₹5.5 lakh crore since 2015; and PMEGP generates employment. Institutional support comes from NITI Aayog, SIDBI, NSIC, KVIC and EDII. The industrial policy has evolved from 1948 (mixed economy) to 1956 (public sector), 1991 (LPG reforms) and 2020 (Atmanirbhar Bharat).

MSMEs contribute about 30 per cent of GDP, 45 per cent of manufacturing output and 40 per cent of exports, and employ over 11 crore people — the largest employer after agriculture. They drive employment generation, innovation, regional development, inclusive growth and export promotion; the Tirupur knitwear cluster, with over 800 MSMEs and 5 lakh workers, exports goods worth ₹25,000 crore plus every year.

Key points:

  • M-S-M = 1-10-50 (₹ crore investment) and 5-50-250 (₹ crore turnover).
  • MSME Development Act 2006 = legal framework; Udyam registration is the entry point.
  • Schemes: MUDRA (loans up to ₹10 lakh), PMEGP (jobs), Startup India, Stand-Up India, Make in India.
  • Institutions: NITI Aayog, SIDBI, NSIC, KVIC, EDII.
  • Contribution: 30% GDP, 45% manufacturing, 40% exports, 11 crore+ jobs (largest employer after agriculture).
  • Tirupur knitwear cluster: 800+ MSMEs, 5 lakh workers, ₹25,000 crore+ exports.

Takeaway: Memorize M-S-M = 1-10-50 and the 30-45-40 contribution formula — both are direct exam quotes.

Answer to Q5: Idea Identification with SCAMPER, SWOT and the Break-Even Point

Answer: Ideas are generated with SCAMPER, screened with SWOT and proved with an MVP; the break-even point of the given project is 9,050 units, which is below the annual demand of 10,000 units, so the proposal is sustainable.

A business idea can come from personal experience, industry trends, customer complaints, technological change and government policies, but it must be systematically developed. SCAMPER is the 7-step idea-generation tool: Substitute (plastic with bamboo), Combine (phone + camera = smartphone), Adapt (Airbnb adapted Uber’s sharing model to housing), Modify (Mini Cooper — a small car in a big-car market), Put to another use (baking soda as a cleaning agent), Eliminate (Google removed clutter from its search page), Rearrange (Netflix lets you watch anytime, unlike a TV schedule). Zomato combined food ordering with technology and delivery; Swiggy modified the approach by making logistics the focus. SWOT analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) then tests whether the entrepreneur’s skills and resources match the market opportunity, and the MVP (Minimum Viable Product) — minimum features, maximum learning — validates the idea with real customers before heavy investment.

The break-even point (BEP) is the output at which total revenue equals total cost: BEP = Fixed Cost / (Market Price per unit – Variable Cost per unit).

Worked solution: FC = ₹36,20,000; MP = ₹440/unit; VC = ₹40/unit.
Contribution per unit = 440 – 40 = ₹400.
BEP (units) = 36,20,000 / 400 = 9,050 units.
Check: revenue at BEP = 9,050 × 440 = ₹39,82,000; total cost at BEP = 36,20,000 + (9,050 × 40) = 36,20,000 + 3,62,000 = ₹39,82,000. Revenue = Cost ✓
Since annual demand of 10,000 units > BEP of 9,050 units, the project earns profit and is sustainable.

Key points:

  • SCAMPER = Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Rearrange.
  • SWOT matches the idea to the entrepreneur’s strengths and market opportunities.
  • MVP = minimum features, maximum learning (validate before heavy investment).
  • BEP = FC / (MP – VC) = 36,20,000 / 400 = 9,050 units.
  • Safety check: demand 10,000 > BEP 9,050 ⇒ sustainable; margin = 950 units.

Takeaway: Write the BEP formula, show the contribution margin (₹400) and compare BEP with demand — that is the full 5-mark answer.

Answer to Q6: Financing an Enterprise and the Business Plan

Answer: Financing follows Seed → Startup → Growth → Expansion (S-S-G-E) across internal and external sources, balancing debt (ownership kept) against equity (capital + mentorship); the business plan has eight components (E-C-M-O-M-F-F-F).

Financing follows four stages, remembered as "S-S-G-E". The seed stage (about ₹1–10 lakh) uses personal savings and family money; startup stage (₹10 lakh–5 crore) brings in angel investors and grants; growth stage (₹5–50 crore) adds venture capital and bank loans; expansion stage (₹50 crore plus) uses IPOs and corporate funding. Flipkart illustrates the journey: it began with ₹4 lakh of personal savings (bootstrapping), raised one million dollars from angel investor Accel Partners, received venture capital from Sequoia, and finally sold 77 per cent to Walmart for 16 billion dollars. Sources divide into internal (savings, retained earnings, family and friends) and external (bank loans, angels, venture capital, government schemes). Debt financing (bank loans, debentures) keeps ownership but adds interest obligations; equity financing (angels, venture capital, IPO) gives large capital and mentorship but dilutes ownership; bootstrapping means self-funding and minimizing outside capital.

A business plan is the roadmap of the venture, and its eight components are remembered as "E-C-M-O-M-F-F-F": Executive summary (one-page overview), Company description (mission and legal structure), Market analysis (industry and competition), Organization and management (team and roles), Service/product line (what you sell), Marketing and sales (pricing and distribution), Financial projections (revenue and profit forecasts), and Funding request (how much is needed and for what). Banks and investors also expect a Detailed Project Report (DPR) covering technical feasibility, financial viability, market potential and risk assessment.

Key points:

  • S-S-G-E: Seed → Startup → Growth → Expansion with rising fund sizes.
  • Internal sources: savings, retained earnings, family/friends; external: banks, angels, VC, schemes.
  • Debt keeps ownership but adds interest; equity brings capital and mentorship but dilutes.
  • Business plan components E-C-M-O-M-F-F-F; executive summary is the most-read part.
  • DPR covers technical feasibility, financial viability, market potential, risk assessment.
  • Flipkart journey = bootstrapping → angel (Accel) → VC (Sequoia) → corporate exit (Walmart).

Takeaway: For a financing question, draw the ladder, name a source per stage and end with Flipkart’s journey.

Q1. Trace the evolution of entrepreneurship from various schools of thought.

Answer: Entrepreneurship thinking evolved from risk-bearing (economics) to personality traits (psychology) to opportunity recognition (1990s).

Key points:

  • Cantillon (risk-bearing school): entrepreneur buys at a certain price and sells at an uncertain one.
  • Say (coordination): entrepreneur organizes land, labour and capital into production.
  • Schumpeter (innovation): creative destruction — innovation breaks the old equilibrium.
  • McClelland (traits): need for achievement (n-Ach) drives entrepreneurial behaviour.
  • Kirzner (discovery): alertness to profitable opportunities; Shane & Venkataraman: opportunity-entrepreneur nexus.

Takeaway: Trace the shift from "who bears risk" to "how opportunities are discovered".

Q3. Differentiate between an entrepreneur, a manager and an intrapreneur.

Answer: The entrepreneur owns the venture and bears personal risk; the manager runs resources as a salaried employee; the intrapreneur innovates inside a company with company risk and resources.

Key points:

Q5. What is the break-even point? Explain with the help of an example.

Answer: BEP is the output where total revenue equals total cost; BEP = FC/(MP – VC). Example: FC ₹36,20,000, price ₹440, VC ₹40 ⇒ BEP = 9,050 units.

Worked solution: Contribution per unit = 440 – 40 = ₹400. BEP = 36,20,000 / 400 = 9,050 units. Check: revenue = 9,050 × 440 = ₹39,82,000; cost = 36,20,000 + (9,050 × 40) = ₹39,82,000. Revenue = Cost ✓ Below BEP the firm makes a loss, above it a profit.

Key points:

  • BEP formula: Fixed Cost / (Market Price per unit – Variable Cost per unit).
  • Below BEP = loss zone; above BEP = profit zone.
  • Used to judge whether a project is sustainable (demand must exceed BEP).
  • Also used for pricing and safety-margin decisions.
  • Safety margin = actual output – BEP output (e.g., 10,000 – 9,050 = 950 units).

Takeaway: Show the formula, the contribution margin and the demand-vs-BEP comparison.

Q1. Describe the different types of entrepreneurship. Which entrepreneurship do you feel is most suitable for India? Justify.

Answer: The seven types are Social, Rural, Women, Group, Strategic, Techno and Education; for India, rural and social entrepreneurship are the most suitable given the 65% rural population and inclusive-growth needs.

Key points:

  • Social: solves social problems sustainably (Grameen, Aravind).
  • Rural: village enterprises, local employment, stops migration (Amul).
  • Women, Group, Strategic, Techno, Education — each with one example.
  • Why rural for India: majority rural population, agrarian economy, informal sector — employment at the doorstep.
  • Why social: poverty, health and education gaps solved with sustainable models.
  • Techno adds scale (UPI, ONDC) but needs capital and skills — so a blended answer wins.

Takeaway: Justify with data — 65% rural, 11 crore MSME jobs — not opinion.

Q2. Discuss the different government schemes initiated for the development of women entrepreneurs in India.

Answer: Stand-Up India, MUDRA (with MUDRA Shakti), PMEGP, the Annapurna scheme, NABARD SHG-bank linkage and EDP training programmes develop women entrepreneurs.

Key points:

  • Stand-Up India: loans of ₹10 lakh–1 crore for women and SC/ST entrepreneurs.
  • MUDRA Yojana: loans up to ₹10 lakh (Shishu/Kishor/Tarun) — a majority of borrowers are women.
  • PMEGP: credit-linked subsidy for micro enterprises, strong rural and women focus.
  • Annapurna scheme: food and catering units for women.
  • NABARD: SHG-bank linkage programme — rural women’s credit without collateral.
  • Capacity building: EDPs by EDII/NIESBUD, DIC support, priority-sector lending norms.

Takeaway: Six schemes with the loan range each — MUDRA + Stand-Up India first.

Q3. Suppose you are a small entrepreneur, what problems do you feel you have to face for the successful establishment of an enterprise? Suggest how these problems can be overcome.

Answer: A small entrepreneur faces capital, market access, competition, raw material, infrastructure, regulatory and skill problems — each solvable through government schemes, market research and support institutions.

Key points:

  • Capital shortage → MUDRA, PMEGP, CGTMSE guarantee; start lean (bootstrap).
  • Market access → e-commerce (GeM, ONDC), exhibitions, local marketing.
  • Competition → differentiate, focus on a niche, raise quality.
  • Raw materials → bulk-purchase groups, supplier contracts.
  • Infrastructure → industrial parks, cluster schemes, government incentives.
  • Regulatory burden → Udyam registration, single-window clearance, CA/consultant help.
  • Skill gaps → EDPs, incubators, mentorship.

Takeaway: Problem paired with its solution — never list problems alone.

Q4. What is Angel investment? Discuss the investment approach of Angel investors with suitable examples.

Answer: Angel investment is seed-stage equity from wealthy individuals in exchange for a stake; angels back the founder first — small cheques, active mentoring, syndication — as Accel did for Flipkart.

Key points:

  • Definition: high-net-worth individuals funding early-stage startups for equity.
  • Approach 1: invest small sums (₹25 lakh–2 crore) early, high risk.
  • Approach 2: bet on the founder — credibility, domain skill, execution.
  • Approach 3: take 10-25% equity, mentor actively, open networks.
  • Approach 4: syndicate via angel networks (Indian Angel Network).
  • Motivations: 10x+ return targets, tax benefits (Startup India), passion.
  • Examples: Accel in Flipkart ($1m early), Ratan Tata in Ola and Snapdeal.

Takeaway: Angels invest in the person first — small cheque, big mentorship.

Q5. Write a note on Porter's five force model, with an example.

Answer: Porter’s five forces — rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, supplier power and buyer power — measure industry attractiveness; food delivery shows high rivalry and buyer power.

Key points:

  • Rivalry: intensity of competition (Zomato vs Swiggy duopoly).
  • Threat of new entrants: entry barriers — capital, brand, network effects.
  • Substitutes: alternatives — home cooking, dining out for delivery.
  • Supplier power: restaurants listing on multiple platforms.
  • Buyer power: customers switch for discounts — low switching cost.
  • Use: decide whether to enter an industry and how to position.
  • Example verdict: food delivery has high forces → thin margins, discount wars.

Takeaway: Draw the five boxes, name a force per side and judge the industry.

Q6. Why is marketing important for entrepreneurs? Differentiate between product and service marketing.

Answer: Marketing creates awareness, demand and revenue — the lifeblood of a new venture; product marketing uses the 4Ps, while service marketing needs 7Ps because services are intangible, inseparable, variable and perishable.

Key points:

  • Why important: without customers there is no revenue; marketing validates the product, builds the brand and wins distribution.
  • Product: tangible, standardised, inventoried, separable from the seller.
  • Service: intangible (cannot pre-view), inseparable (delivered with provider), variable (depends on person), perishable (unsold capacity lost).
  • 4Ps vs 7Ps: services add People, Process and Physical evidence.
  • Example: a soap bar is uniform; a trainer’s class quality varies with the trainer.

Takeaway: Services are I-I-V-P (intangible, inseparable, variable, perishable) — hence 7Ps.

Q7. Explain the concept of social entrepreneurship. Differentiate between social entrepreneurship and corporate entrepreneurship.

Answer: Social entrepreneurship solves social problems through a sustainable business; corporate entrepreneurship innovates inside firms for profit — one counts beneficiaries, the other counts ROI.

Key points:

  • Social: mission first → business model → earned income → impact (Grameen, Aravind).
  • Corporate (intrapreneurship): new products and ventures inside existing corporations for profit (Google X, Amazon Prime).
  • Goal: social impact vs profit maximization.
  • Revenue: grants, donations, earned income vs sales and profit.
  • Stakeholders: community and beneficiaries vs shareholders and investors.
  • Measurement: impact metrics vs financial returns (ROI).

Takeaway: Compare on goal, revenue, stakeholders and measurement — four axes.

Q8. What do you understand by Rural Entrepreneurship? Briefly describe the various government's endeavours to facilitate the rural entrepreneurship.

Answer: Rural entrepreneurship runs enterprises in villages on local resources; government facilitates it through PMEGP, KVIC, NABARD SHG-bank linkage, MUDRA, SVEP and SFURTI cluster development.

Key points:

  • Definition: local resource-based enterprises, local employment, stops migration.
  • PMEGP: credit-linked subsidy for micro enterprises with rural and women focus.
  • KVIC: khadi and village industries — honey, pottery, khadi clusters.
  • NABARD: refinance plus the SHG-bank linkage programme.
  • MUDRA: loans up to ₹10 lakh for micro units.
  • SVEP: Start-Up Village Entrepreneurship Programme for village-level enterprises.
  • SFURTI: cluster development of traditional industries.

Takeaway: Six schemes + I-G-C-C forms — close with Amul as the proof.
