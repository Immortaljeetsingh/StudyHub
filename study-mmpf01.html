Unit 1: Introduction to Working Capital

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Working Capital Introduction
▶
Gross vs Net Working Capital
  • Gross Working Capital = Total Current Assets (cash + inventory + receivables + marketable securities)
  • Net Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities
  • Permanent WC = Minimum WC always required regardless of business fluctuations
  • Temporary WC = Additional WC needed during seasonal/peak periods
  • Working capital is the “lifeblood” of business operations
📖 Key Definitions

Working Capital: Capital invested in short-term assets that are convertible into cash within one year (operating cycle).

Operating Cycle: The time gap between cash outflow (purchases) and cash inflow (sales collection). Cash → Raw Materials → WIP → Finished Goods → Receivables → Cash.

💡 Real-World Example

A garment manufacturer needs permanent WC for year-round raw material stock, but temporary WC spikes during festival season demand.

🎯 Exam Tip

Always distinguish between gross and net WC. Numerical problems on calculating WC requirements are common.

Point 3: Importance of Working Capital

Operational ContinuitySolvencyCreditworthinessProfitabilityFlexibility
  1. Operational Continuity: Without adequate WC, production stops, suppliers don't get paid, employees don't get salaries
  2. Solvency: Positive WC ensures firm can meet short-term obligations and avoid bankruptcy
  3. Creditworthiness: Banks and suppliers assess WC position before extending credit
  4. Profitability: Optimal WC level balances liquidity and profitability
  5. Flexibility: Surplus WC allows firm to exploit unexpected opportunities

  • 🔄 Minimize WC cycle
  • 🎯 Balance risk and return
  • 💻 Efficient resource utilization

Unit 2: Working Capital Cycle and Operating Cycle

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Cash Conversion Cycle Explained
▶
Operating Cycle vs CCC
  • Operating Cycle = Inventory Period + Receivables Period
  • Cash Conversion Cycle = Operating Cycle - Payables Period
  • A shorter cycle means faster cash recovery; longer cycle means more funds tied up
  • Working Capital Cycle: Cash → Raw Materials → WIP → Finished Goods → Debtors → Cash
📖 Key Definitions

Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC): The net time period during which cash is tied up in operating activities. CCC = Inventory Days + Receivable Days - Payable Days.

💡 Real-World Example

Amazon has a negative CCC (collects from customers before paying suppliers), while traditional retailers have positive CCC.

🎯 Exam Tip

Understand the difference between operating cycle and cash conversion cycle. Know how to calculate each component.

Unit 3: Determinants of Working Capital

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Factors Affecting Working Capital
  • Nature of Business: Manufacturing firms need more WC than service firms
  • Production Policy: Level production vs seasonal production affects WC
  • Credit Policy: Liberal credit = more receivables = more WC needed
  • Growth Rate: Expanding businesses need more WC
  • Inflation: Rising prices increase WC requirements
  • Operating Efficiency: Better efficiency reduces WC needs
📖 Key Definitions

Determinants of WC: Internal factors (size, production cycle, credit policy) and external factors (inflation, interest rates, competition) that influence WC requirements.

💡 Real-World Example

FMCG companies like HUL need less WC (cash sales), while B2B companies like L&T need high WC (long credit periods).

🎯 Exam Tip

Exam questions often ask to “discuss factors affecting WC requirements” - cover both internal and external factors.

Unit 4: Management of Receivables

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Receivables Management
▶
5 C's of Credit
  • Credit Policy Components: Credit period, discount terms, credit standards, collection effort
  • 5 C’s of Credit: Character, Capacity, Capital, Conditions, Collateral
  • Credit Evaluation Models: Qualitative (5 C’s) and Quantitative (credit scoring)
  • Monitoring Tools: Aging schedule, receivables turnover ratio, average collection period
  • Collection Process: Dunning letters, telephone calls, personal visits, legal action, factoring
📖 Key Definitions

Aging Schedule: A table classifying receivables by age (current, 30 days, 60 days, 90+ days) to identify overdue accounts.

Factoring: Selling receivables to a third party (factor) at a discount to obtain immediate cash.

💡 Real-World Example

IRFC has 99.7% of current assets as debtors; TCS has 88.9% - showing how different industries have vastly different receivable structures.

🎯 Exam Tip

5 C’s of credit is a very common exam question. Also know the difference between credit standards and credit terms.

Unit 5: Management of Cash

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Cash Management Strategies
  • Motives for Holding Cash: Transaction motive, Precautionary motive, Speculative motive
  • Cash Forecasting: Receipts & payments method, Adjusted net income method
  • Cash Budget: Projecting cash inflows and outflows for a future period
  • MIS for Cash Management: Information system to monitor cash position
  • Surplus Cash Management: Invest idle cash in marketable securities
📖 Key Definitions

Transaction Motive: Holding cash to meet day-to-day business obligations (salaries, purchases, expenses).

Precautionary Motive: Maintaining a cash buffer for unexpected emergencies or opportunities.

💡 Real-World Example

Infosys maintains large cash reserves (precautionary) for strategic acquisitions and dividend payments.

🎯 Exam Tip

Know the three motives for holding cash. Cash budgeting problems are common in exams.

Unit 6: Management of Marketable Securities

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Marketable Securities
▶
Baumol Model
  • Types: Treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit, money market mutual funds
  • Selection Criteria: Safety, liquidity, yield (in that order of priority)
  • Baumol Model: Optimal cash balance = sqrt(2 x F x T / K) where F = fixed cost, T = total cash needed, K = opportunity cost
  • Miller-Orr Model: Sets upper and lower limits for cash balance with a return point
📖 Key Definitions

Baumol Model: Treats cash management like inventory management - finds the optimal cash conversion amount to minimize total cost.

Miller-Orr Model: A more realistic model that allows for random cash inflows and outflows, setting control limits.

💡 Real-World Example

Companies like Reliance invest surplus cash in overnight money market instruments for safety and liquidity.

🎯 Exam Tip

Know the formulas for both Baumol and Miller-Orr models. Compare and contrast them.

Unit 7: Management of Inventory

📖 Core Concepts
▶
EOQ Model Explained
▶
ABC Analysis
  • Types of Inventory: Raw materials, WIP, finished goods, consumables/supplies
  • Inventory Costs: Ordering cost, carrying (holding) cost, shortage (stockout) cost
  • EOQ Model: Economic Order Quantity = sqrt(2DS/H) where D = annual demand, S = ordering cost, H = holding cost per unit
  • Safety Stock: Buffer inventory to prevent stockouts due to demand/supply uncertainty
  • ABC Analysis: A = 10% items, 70% value; B = 20% items, 20% value; C = 70% items, 10% value
  • VED Analysis: Vital, Essential, Desirable
  • FSN Analysis: Fast-moving, Slow-moving, Non-moving
📖 Key Definitions

Economic Order Quantity (EOQ): The optimal order quantity that minimizes total inventory costs (ordering + carrying costs).

Safety Stock: Extra inventory held as a buffer against uncertainties in demand or supply lead time.

💡 Real-World Example

A hospital uses VED analysis - vital drugs always in stock, essential ones maintained at moderate levels, desirable ones ordered on demand.

🎯 Exam Tip

EOQ formula is a must-know. Also explain ABC, VED, and FSN analysis with examples.

Unit 8: Theories and Approaches to WC

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Working Capital Financing Approaches
  • Matching Approach (Hedging): Match asset maturity with financing maturity - short-term assets with short-term funds, long-term with long-term
  • Conservative Approach: Use long-term sources for all needs (low risk, low return)
  • Aggressive Approach: Use short-term sources extensively (high risk, high return)
  • Walker’s Approach: WC level relative to sales affects risk and return
  • Trade-Off Approach: Balance between risk and return in WC investment
📖 Key Definitions

Matching Approach: Financing strategy where the maturity of financing matches the life of the asset being financed.

Conservative Approach: Financing all current assets and part of permanent WC with long-term sources.

💡 Real-World Example

Tech startups often use aggressive WC policies (venture debt) to maximize growth, while established FMCG companies prefer conservative policies.

🎯 Exam Tip

Compare all three approaches with risk-return trade-offs. Walker’s four principles are important for descriptive answers.

Unit 9: Payables Management

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Trade Credit Management
  • Trade Credit: “Buy now, pay later” - spontaneous source of financing
  • Cost of Trade Credit: Discount lost / (1 - discount) x (365 / days past discount period)
  • Example: 2/10 net 30 = (2/98) x (365/20) = 37.24% annual cost
  • Stretching Payables: Delaying payments beyond credit terms (risky - damages relationships)
  • Payables as Financing: Free if paid within terms; costly if discount period missed
📖 Key Definitions

Trade Credit: Credit extended by suppliers for purchases, allowing payment at a future date (e.g., net 30 days).

Stretching Payables: Deliberately delaying payment beyond the agreed credit period to conserve cash.

💡 Real-World Example

A retailer getting 2/10 net 30 terms should pay within 10 days (save 2%) unless short-term borrowing costs exceed 37.24% annually.

🎯 Exam Tip

Calculate cost of trade credit formula. Know when to take vs. forgo trade discounts.

🧠 Memory Aid

“2/10 net 30” = 2% discount if paid in 10 days, otherwise full amount in 30 days

Point 1: Cost of Trade Credit

📊 Trade Credit Cost Formula
Cost = [D/(1-D)] × [365/(N-Days)]

D = Discount %; N = Net days; Days = Discount period

⚡ Quick Revision Sheet

One-Liner Concepts

  • WC = lifeblood of business
  • Gross WC = Total CA; Net WC = CA - CL
  • Operating cycle: Cash → RM → WIP → FG → Debtors → Cash
  • CCC = Inventory days + Receivable days - Payable days
  • 5 C’s of credit: Character, Capacity, Capital, Conditions, Collateral
  • EOQ minimizes ordering + carrying costs
  • ABC: A=10% items/70% value, C=70% items/10% value
  • 3 approaches: Matching (hedging), Conservative, Aggressive
  • Trade credit: Free if paid within discount period
  • 2/10 net 30 = 37.24% annual cost if discount missed
  • Banks finance up to 40% of WC gap
  • SMEs: Micro (≤25L), Small (25L-5Cr), Medium (5-10Cr)
  • MNCs use multilateral netting and transfer pricing

Key Differences

  • Gross WC vs Net WC: Total CA vs CA-CL
  • OC vs CCC: OC includes payables period deduction in CCC
  • CC vs OD: CC has sanctioned limit against security; OD is informal
  • Baumol vs Miller-Orr: Baumol assumes uniform cash flows; Miller-Orr allows randomness
  • SME vs Large: SMEs have limited access, weak controls; Large firms have professional management
