Point 1: Definition of Business Environment

Business environment refers to the totality of all factors which are external to and beyond the control of individual business enterprises and their managements. It furnishes the macro-context within which firms must operate. Environmental factors are essentially the "givens" such as:

  • Value system of society
  • Rules and regulations laid down by Government
  • Monetary policies of the central bank
  • Institutional setup of the country
  • Ideological beliefs of the leaders
  • Attitude towards foreign capital and enterprise

Point 2: Characteristics of Business Environment

  1. Dynamic: Business environments are highly flexible and keep on changing due to factors like customer preferences, new competitors, novel technology, new marketing channels, government policies, and changing demography.
  1. Uncertain: It is very difficult to assume with certainty about the factors influencing business environment because they fluctuate quickly.
  1. Complex: The business environment is continuously exposed to uncertain challenges such as technological disruptions, global competition, leadership changes, shifting economic, social, and regulatory conditions.
  1. Relativity: Business environment varies from country to country, region to region due to societal norms and local conditions.
  1. Interrelation: All factors and forces of business environment are related - for example, youth proclivity towards western culture leads to rising demand for fast food.

Point 3: Types of Business Environment

  1. Internal and External Environment:
  • Internal: Factors within organization influencing strengths/weaknesses (raw materials, human resources)
  • External: Factors beyond control providing opportunities/threats (political conditions, technology)
  1. Micro and Macro Environment:
  • Micro: Factors directly affecting particular business (customers, suppliers, competitors)
  • Macro: General environment affecting all businesses (political, economic, technological)
  1. Controllable and Uncontrollable:
  • Controllable: Internal factors governed by business (money, men, materials, machines)
  • Uncontrollable: External factors beyond management influence

Point 4: Importance of Studying Business Environment

Managers must understand environment because:

  • Decisions must consider possible environmental reactions
  • Marketing managers study market environment before product launches
  • Finance managers study capital and money market structures
  • Personnel managers must understand government rules on reservations
  • Environmental analysis helps identify opportunities and threats
Indian Example

Reliance Jio (2016): Mukesh Ambani studied the Indian telecom environment — high data prices, limited smartphone penetration, and a young population hungry for digital content. Jio launched with free voice and ultra-cheap data, disrupting the entire industry. Within 3 years, Jio had 370 million subscribers. This is a textbook case of environmental scanning creating massive opportunity.

Point 6: SWOT vs Environmental Analysis

SWOT examines internal Strengths & Weaknesses and external Opportunities & Threats. It is firm-specific, while PESTEL is industry/macro-level. Best practice: conduct PESTEL first, then derive SWOT from findings.

Point 1: Economic Growth vs Development

Economic Growth refers to increase in real output of goods and services over time, typically measured by GDP growth rate.

Economic Development encompasses broader improvements in living standards including:

  • Income distribution
  • Health and education outcomes
  • Infrastructure development
  • Reduction in poverty levels

Point 2: Determinants of Economic Growth

  1. Physical Capital: Infrastructure, machinery, and equipment investment
  2. Human Capital: Education, training, and skill development of workforce
  3. Natural Resources: Availability of land, minerals, water, and other resources
  4. Technology: Innovation and adoption of new production methods
  5. Institutional Factors: Property rights, rule of law, political stability

Point 3: Economic Development Indicators

  1. GDP Per Capita: Measure of average economic output per person
  2. HDI (Human Development Index): Composite index of life expectancy, education, and per capita income
  3. Poverty Ratio: Percentage of population below poverty line
  4. Gini Coefficient: Measure of income inequality (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality)
  5. Infant Mortality Rate: Indicator of healthcare system effectiveness
  6. Literacy Rate: Measure of educational achievement

Point 4: Economic Development Strategies

  1. Industrialization: Shift from agriculture to manufacturing sector
  2. Infrastructure Development: Transport, power, telecommunications networks
  3. Human Resource Development: Education and health sector investments
  4. Technology Transfer: Adoption of modern technologies and practices
  5. Institution Building: Strengthening legal and regulatory frameworks
Indian Example

Gujarat vs Kerala Growth Model: Gujarat focuses on industrialization and infrastructure (high GDP growth ~10%), while Kerala prioritizes human development (highest literacy 96%, best HDI in India). This illustrates that economic growth and economic development can follow different paths. Gujarat has higher GDP; Kerala has better social indicators. The ideal strategy combines both approaches.

Exam Tip

Always distinguish between Growth (quantitative — GDP increase) and Development (qualitative — HDI, poverty reduction, equality). A common exam question: "Can a country have growth without development?" Answer: Yes — Nigeria has oil-driven GDP growth but poor HDI. India's post-1991 reforms achieved both in varying degrees.

Point 5: NITI Aayog and Planning Evolution

India replaced the Planning Commission with NITI Aayog (2015) to shift from centralized planning to cooperative federalism:

  • Three-year action agenda replacing five-year plans
  • Seven-year strategy and 15-year vision documents
  • Bottom-up approach involving states as partners
  • SDG India Index tracking sustainable development goals

Point 1: Socio-Cultural Environment

Cultural Values: Beliefs, customs, traditions, and social norms that influence business behavior and expectations.

Social Structure: Class systems, family structures, demographic patterns affecting consumption patterns and workforce composition.

Religious Influences: Religious beliefs and practices that shape business conduct and consumer preferences.

Language and Communication: Languages spoken and communication styles affecting business interactions.

Point 2: Politico-Legal Environment

Political System: Type of government and political stability affecting business operations.

Legal Framework: Laws governing business activities including:

  • Company law
  • Contract law
  • Labor law
  • Consumer protection law
  • Environmental regulations

Regulatory Agencies: Government bodies overseeing business conduct and compliance.

Taxation Policy: Direct and indirect tax structures affecting business costs.

Point 3: Impact on Business Operations

  1. Operational Decisions: Cultural preferences influence product design and marketing strategies
  2. Employment Practices: Legal requirements for working conditions, wages, and benefits
  3. Market Entry Strategies: Political climate affects foreign investment decisions
  4. Risk Management: Political instability creates uncertainty and risk exposure

Point 4: Environmental Scanning

Businesses must continuously monitor:

  • Changes in consumer preferences and lifestyles
  • Shifts in demographic patterns
  • New legislation and regulatory changes
  • Political developments affecting business climate
Indian Example

Haldiram's Cultural Adaptation: Haldiram's studied the socio-cultural environment across Indian states and customized its product range — Raj kachori in North, bhujia variants in Rajasthan, South Indian snacks in southern markets. They also adapted to the vegetarian preference in Gujarat and Jain communities by offering onion-garlic-free products. Cultural sensitivity helped them become a ₹5,000 crore brand.

Indian Example — Political-Legal

GST Impact (2017): The Goods and Services Tax transformed India's indirect tax landscape. It subsumed 17 central and state taxes into one unified tax. Businesses had to completely restructure their supply chains — earlier they optimized for tax arbitrage between states; now they optimize for logistics efficiency. E-way bills, GSTN compliance, and input tax credits became new business imperatives.

Mnemonic: Socio-Cultural Factors
"C-S-R-L-D" — "Cool Students Read Lots Daily"
Cultural values, Social structure, Religious influences, Language & communication, Demographics
Exam Tip

For politico-legal environment questions, always mention FEMA 1999 (replaced FERA), Companies Act 2013 (replaced 1956 Act), and Consumer Protection Act 2019 (replaced 1986 Act). These three reforms are examiner favorites and show you understand legislative evolution.

Point 1: Business Ethics

Business ethics refers to moral principles and standards governing business conduct and decision-making.

Key Ethical Issues:

  • Honesty in advertising and communication
  • Fair treatment of employees and customers
  • Environmental responsibility
  • Truthful financial reporting
  • Conflict of interest management

Point 2: Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

CSR involves businesses going beyond legal compliance to address social and environmental concerns.

Dimensions:

  1. Economic Responsibility: Be profitable and sustainable
  2. Legal Responsibility: Obey laws and regulations
  3. Ethical Responsibility: Do what is right and fair
  4. Philanthropic Responsibility: Contribute to community welfare

Point 3: CSR in India

Legal Framework (Section 135, Companies Act 2013):

  • Companies with net worth ≥ ₹500 crore OR
  • Turnover ≥ ₹1,000 crore OR
  • Net profit ≥ ₹5 crore
  • Must spend 2% of average net profits on CSR

Eligible Activities:

  • Education promotion
  • Healthcare and sanitation
  • Rural development
  • Environmental sustainability
  • Women empowerment
  • Skill development

Point 4: Benefits of Ethical Business Practices

  1. Reputation Enhancement: Builds trust and positive brand image
  2. Employee Attraction: Appeals to values-conscious talent
  3. Customer Loyalty: Increases retention among conscious consumers
  4. Risk Reduction: Minimizes legal and regulatory violations
  5. Long-term Sustainability: Creates enduring competitive advantage
Indian Example — CSR Leaders

Tata Group: India's gold standard in CSR. Tata Trusts hold 66% of Tata Sons' equity — profits flow to philanthropy. Major initiatives: Tata Memorial Hospital (cancer care), TCS rural education programs, Tata Water Mission (clean water for 3M+ people). Annual CSR spend: ₹1,000+ crore. Ratan Tata's philosophy: "I don't believe in wealth creation for personal gain."

Infosys Foundation: Spent ₹322 crore on CSR in FY2023. Focus areas: education (1,50,000+ scholarships), healthcare (rural hospitals), rural development (digital literacy). Sudha Murthy personally oversees projects across Karnataka and other states.

Exam Tip — Section 135 CSR Mandate

Memorize the thresholds: Net worth ≥ ₹500 crore OR Turnover ≥ ₹1,000 crore OR Net profit ≥ ₹5 crore → must spend 2% of average net profits on CSR. Failure to spend requires explanation in board report (comply-or-explain). India is the first country to mandate CSR by law — this is a common exam fact.

Mnemonic: CSR Pyramid (Carroll)
"E-L-E-P from bottom to top" — "Elephants Love Eating Peanuts"
Economic → Legal → Ethical → Philanthropic
You must be profitable first (economic), then obey laws, then be ethical, then give back.

Point 5: Whistleblower Protection and Corporate Governance

Whistleblower Protection Act 2014 protects persons reporting corruption or willful misuse of power. Key provisions: anonymity, protection from victimization, competent authority for inquiry.

Corporate Governance Reforms (SEBI): Mandatory independent directors (50% for top 500 companies), audit committees, related-party transaction approvals, board diversity requirements.

Indian Financial System Structure

Point 1: Structure of Indian Financial System

The Indian financial system consists of institutions and markets that facilitate savings, investment, and risk management. It operates through:

Institutional Components:

  • Commercial Banks: Scheduled and non-scheduled banks providing deposit and credit services
  • Cooperative Banks: Member-owned financial institutions at urban and rural levels
  • Regional Rural Banks: Focused on agricultural and rural sector financing
  • Development Financial Institutions: Specialized institutions for term lending (ex: IDBI, SIDBI)
  • Non-Banking Financial Companies: NBFCs providing various financial services
  • Insurance Companies: Life and general insurance providers
  • Pension Funds: Retirement and provident fund institutions

Market Components:

  • Money Market: Short-term funds (up to 1 year)
  • Capital Market: Long-term funds and securities
  • Forex Market: Foreign exchange transactions
  • Debt Market: Government and corporate bonds
  • Derivatives Market: Futures and options trading

Point 2: Reserve Bank of India (RBI) - Key Functions

  1. Monetary Authority: Regulates money supply and interest rates
  2. Banker to Banks: Maintains accounts of scheduled commercial banks
  3. Banker to Government: Manages government accounts and public debt
  4. Lender of Last Resort: Provides emergency funding to banks
  5. Regulator and Supervisor: Oversees functioning of financial institutions
  6. Foreign Exchange Manager: Manages foreign exchange reserves and exchange rates

Point 3: Commercial Banking in India

Types of Banks:

  • Public Sector Banks: Government-owned majority stakes
  • Private Sector Banks: Private ownership and management
  • Foreign Banks: Branch operations of international banks
  • Cooperative Banks: Member-based ownership structure
  • Regional Rural Banks: Serving specific rural areas

Banking Functions:

  • Accepting deposits (savings, current, fixed deposits)
  • Providing credit (working capital, term loans)
  • Underwriting and underwriting services
  • Foreign exchange services
  • Agency services (collections, remittances)

Point 4: Capital Market in India

Primary Market: Where new securities are issued and sold for the first time

  • Initial Public Offering (IPO)
  • Follow-on Public Offering (FPO)
  • Rights Issue
  • Preferential Issue

Secondary Market: Trading of existing securities

  • Stock exchanges (BSE, NSE)
  • Over-the-counter markets
  • Depository participants and demat accounts

Point 5: Regulatory Framework

Primary Regulators:

  • Reserve Bank of India (Banking Regulation Act)
  • Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI)
  • Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority (IRDA)
  • Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA)

Key Regulations:

  • Banking Regulation Act, 1949
  • Companies Act, 2013
  • Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956
  • Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999
Indian Example — UPI Revolution

UPI (Unified Payments Interface): Launched by NPCI in 2016, UPI processed 13.4 billion transactions in January 2024 worth ₹18.4 lakh crore. It transformed India from a cash-dominant economy to a digital payment leader. Google Pay, PhonePe, and Paytm process 95%+ of UPI volumes. UPI is now being adopted by Singapore, UAE, France, and Sri Lanka — India's biggest fintech export.

Exam Tip — RBI Functions

Remember RBI's 6 functions using mnemonic "M-B-B-L-R-F": Monetary authority, Banker to banks, Banker to govt, Lender of last resort, Regulator, Foreign exchange manager. A common trick question: "Is RBI the regulator of stock markets?" No — that's SEBI. RBI regulates banking; SEBI regulates securities markets.

Mnemonic: Financial Market Types
"Money Capital Debt Forex Derivatives" — "Many Citizens Don't Find it Difficult"
Money Market (short-term) → Capital Market (equity/debt) → Debt Market (bonds) → Forex Market → Derivatives Market

Industrial Policy Evolution Timeline

Point 1: Evolution of Industrial Policy

Pre-1991 Era (License Raj):

  • Industrial Licensing under Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951
  • Reservation of industries for public sector
  • MRTP Act restrictions on large business houses
  • Import substitution strategy
  • High tariff protection

Post-1991 Era (Liberalization):

  • Abolition of industrial licensing (except few industries)
  • Opening up to foreign investment
  • Reduction in import tariffs
  • Privatization of public sector enterprises
  • Foreign exchange liberalization

Point 2: Industrial Policy Statements

Industrial Policy 1991:

  • Abolished industrial licensing for all except 18 industries
  • Opened up industries to private sector participation
  • Allowed foreign investment up to 51% under automatic route
  • Reduced monopoly restrictions under MRTP Act

Industrial Policy 2014-2020:

  • "Make in India" initiative focus
  • Emphasis on manufacturing competitiveness
  • Ease of doing business improvements
  • Digital India integration
  • Startup India promotion

Point 3: Current Industrial Classification

Strategic Industries:

  • Defense equipment manufacturing
  • Nuclear energy projects
  • Atomic minerals
  • Railways transport (excluding coaching and wagon manufacturing)

Reserved for Public Sector:

  • Atomic energy
  • Mineral oils and gases
  • Mining and processing of radioactive minerals
  • Rail transport (core infrastructure)

Open to Private Sector:

  • Most manufacturing industries
  • Service sectors
  • Small-scale industries
  • Technology and innovation sectors

Point 4: Foreign Investment Policy

Automatic Route: FDI allowed without prior approval

  • 100% FDI in many sectors including defense, telecom, insurance
  • Government route for sensitive sectors requiring approval

Foreign Exchange Management:

  • Current account convertibility since 1994
  • Capital account partially liberalized
  • External Commercial Borrowing (ECB) framework
  • Portfolio investment through stock exchanges

Point 5: Make in India Initiative

Objectives:

  • Increase manufacturing sector growth to 12-14%
  • Create 100 million additional jobs by 2022
  • Increase manufacturing sector share in GDP to 25%
  • Reduce dependency on imports for critical goods

Key Sectors:

  • Manufacturing and engineering
  • Pharmaceuticals and biotechnology
  • Electronics and IT hardware
  • Automotive and textiles
  • Defense manufacturing
  • Renewable energy
Indian Example — PLI Scheme Success

Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme: Launched in 2020 for 14 sectors with ₹1.97 lakh crore outlay. Results: Apple suppliers (Foxconn, Wistron) now manufacture iPhones in India. Mobile phone exports surged from ₹11,000 crore (2017) to ₹1.2 lakh crore (2023). India is now the world's second-largest mobile phone manufacturer. PLI also covers semiconductors (₹76,000 crore), EV batteries, and white goods.

Exam Tip

The 1991 Industrial Policy is the most frequently examined topic. Remember the 3 reforms: De-licensing (abolished licensing), Dereservation (reduced public sector reservation from 17 to 3), and Decontrol (removed MRTP restrictions). Always cite Manmohan Singh as the architect and PV Narasimha Rao as the political enabler.

Agricultural Revolutions in India

Point 1: Agricultural Sector Overview

Agriculture remains the backbone of Indian economy despite declining share in GDP:

  • Employs about 50% of workforce
  • Contributes approximately 17% to GDP
  • Largest producer of milk, pulses, and spices globally
  • Second largest producer of wheat, rice, cotton

Point 2: Agricultural Inputs and Infrastructure

Land Resources:

  • Net sown area: ~141 million hectares
  • Gross cropped area: ~260 million hectares (multiple cropping)
  • Soil types: Alluvial, black cotton, red and yellow, laterite, mountain soils

Irrigation:

  • Canal irrigation: 40% of irrigated area
  • Groundwater: 60% of irrigated area
  • Major dams and multipurpose projects
  • Micro-irrigation (drip and sprinkler) promotion

Credit and Finance:

  • Cooperative banks and RRBs
  • Commercial banks agricultural advances
  • Kisan Credit Cards (KCC)
  • Agricultural term loans and crop loans

Point 3: Agricultural Marketing and Value Chains

APMC Act: Agricultural Produce Market Committee regulation of wholesale markets

Challenges:

  • Multiple intermediaries reducing farmer incomes
  • Lack of storage and cold chain infrastructure
  • Price volatility and market risks
  • Limited access to modern retail channels

Reforms:

  • Model APMC Act for states
  • Electronic trading platforms
  • Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs)
  • Contract farming frameworks

Point 4: Agricultural Technology and Innovation

Green Revolution: Introduction of HYV seeds, fertilizers, and irrigation

White Revolution: Operation Flood dairy development program

Yellow Revolution: Oilseed production enhancement

Evergreen Revolution: Sustainable agriculture practices

Modern Technologies:

  • Precision farming and IoT applications
  • GM crop adoption (Bt cotton primarily)
  • Organic farming initiatives
  • Digital platforms for market linkages

Point 5: Government Support and Schemes

Price Support: MSP (Minimum Support Price) for major crops

Subsidy Programs: Fertilizer, power, irrigation subsidies

Insurance: Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana

Infrastructure: National Agriculture Market (e-NAM), agricultural roads

Investment: Agricultural investment funds and credit guarantee schemes

Indian Example — Amul Model

Amul (GCMMF): The White Revolution's crown jewel. Founded by Tribhuvandas Patel and led by Verghese Kurien, Amul pioneered the cooperative model — 3.6 million milk producers across 18,600 villages. Daily procurement: 27 million liters. Revenue: ₹72,000 crore (FY2023). The Amul model bypassed middlemen, ensuring 80% of the consumer price goes to farmers. It's studied at Harvard Business School as a masterclass in rural supply chain management.

Exam Tip — Farm Laws 2020

The three farm laws (repealed 2021) are a hot exam topic. They aimed to: (1) Allow farmers to sell outside APMC mandis, (2) Enable contract farming, (3) Remove essential commodities from stock limits. They were repealed due to farmer protests. In exams, present both sides: the reform rationale (market freedom, better prices) vs. farmer concerns (loss of MSP guarantee, corporate exploitation).

Mnemonic: Agricultural Revolutions
"Green White Yellow Blue Golden Silver" — "Good Workers Yield Big Gold Silver"
Green = Food grains (HYV), White = Milk (Operation Flood), Yellow = Oilseeds, Blue = Fish, Golden = Horticulture, Silver = Eggs/Poultry

Point 1: Background and Objectives (1991)

The New Economic Policy (NEP) of 1991 was introduced due to severe balance of payments crisis:

  • Foreign exchange reserves fell to $1 billion (enough for 2-3 weeks imports)
  • Fiscal deficit exceeded 8% of GDP
  • Public sector inefficiency and mounting losses
  • External debt servicing difficulties

Objectives:

  1. Stabilization: Correct external sector imbalances and fiscal deficit
  2. Liberalization: Remove restrictions on business and industry
  3. Globalization: Integrate with world economy and attract foreign investment

Point 2: Key Reforms Introduced

Trade Liberalization:

  • Reduction in import tariffs from 300% to 50%
  • Removal of quantitative restrictions on imports
  • Export promotion through duty-free imports
  • Introduction of Export Promotion Capital Goods scheme

Industrial De-licensing:

  • Abolition of industrial licensing for most industries
  • Reduction of reserved sectors for public sector
  • Entry of private sector in previously restricted areas
  • MRTP Act threshold increased and simplified

Financial Sector Reforms:

  • Establishment of Debt Recovery Tribunals
  • Introduction of SARFAESI Act for asset recovery
  • Banking sector reforms and technology adoption
  • Capital market development and investor protection

Point 3: Foreign Investment Policy

FDI Liberalization:

  • Automatic approval route for FDI up to 51% in many sectors
  • 100% FDI in sectors like pharmaceuticals, hotels, tourism
  • Bilateral investment protection agreements
  • Portfolio investment deregulation

Foreign Exchange Reforms:

  • Current account convertibility achieved in 1994
  • Dual exchange rate system eliminated
  • Market-determined exchange rate regime
  • Capital account convertibility initiated gradually

Point 4: Impact of NEP

Positive Outcomes:

  • GDP growth accelerated to 6-8% range
  • Foreign investment inflows increased significantly
  • Sectoral efficiency improvements
  • Technology transfer and skill development
  • Integration with global value chains

Challenges:

  • Regional disparities in growth benefits
  • Employment generation in organized sector
  • Agricultural sector lagging behind
  • Infrastructure bottlenecks
Indian Example — 1991 Gold Pledge

The Crisis that Changed India: In June 1991, India pledged 47 tonnes of gold to the Bank of England and Union Bank of Switzerland to raise $600 million. Foreign reserves had dropped to just $1.2 billion — barely enough for 2 weeks of imports. RBI Governor S. Venkitaramnan personally flew the gold to London. This humiliation catalyzed the LPG reforms under Finance Minister Manmohan Singh. Within a decade, India's reserves crossed $100 billion.

Point 1: Financial Sector Reforms

Banking Sector:

  • Narasimham Committee recommendations (1991, 1998)
  • Reduction in Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) and Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)
  • Deregulation of interest rates on deposits and advances
  • Introduction of prudential norms for income recognition and asset classification

  • Dematerialization and electronic trading systems
  • Introduction of screen-based trading
  • Derivatives trading introduction

  • IRDA Act 2000 allowing private sector entry
  • Foreign equity participation up to 26%
  • health insurance and pension fund regulations
  • Microinsurance and customized products

Point 2: Fiscal Sector Reforms

Tax Reform:

  • Introduction of Goods and Services Tax (GST)
  • Direct tax reforms and faceless assessment
  • Rationalization of tax rates and exemptions
  • Digital tax administration platforms

Expenditure Management:

  • Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act (FRBM)
  • Medium-term fiscal framework
  • Outcome budgeting initiatives
  • Subsidy reforms and direct benefit transfers

Point 3: FRBM Act 2003

Fiscal Targets:

  • Revenue deficit: 0% by 2008-09 (not achieved)
  • Fiscal deficit: 3% of GDP
  • Effective Revenue Deficit: 2.2% of GDP (new target)
  • Debt-to-GDP ratio: 60% for center and states combined

Deficit Consolidation Path:

  • General government deficit reduction
  • Improved quality of fiscal adjustment
  • Contingent liability management
Indian Example — GST Impact

GST (July 1, 2017): India's biggest tax reform since independence. Subsumed 17 indirect taxes (Excise, VAT, Service Tax, etc.) into one unified tax. Impact: Eliminated cascading effect of "tax on tax," reduced logistics costs by 20% (no more state border checkposts), formalized the economy (GSTN tracks every transaction). Challenges: Multiple rate slabs (5/12/18/28%), compliance burden for SMEs, initial IT glitches. Revenue: ₹1.68 lakh crore monthly collection (April 2023 record).

Exam Tip — Narasimham Committee

Two committees (1991 and 1998) are exam favorites. Key recommendations: Reduce SLR and CRR, allow private banks, recapitalize PSU banks, adopt Basel norms, set up ARCs for bad loans. In 2024 context: many recommendations were implemented (Bandhan Bank, IDFC First Bank are new private banks), but full privatization of PSU banks remains politically sensitive.

Balance of Payments Structure

Point 1: Components of Balance of Payments

Current Account:

  • Trade in goods (visible exports and imports)
  • Trade in services (invisible exports and imports)
  • Income receipts and payments
  • Current transfers

Capital Account:

  • Capital transfers (debt forgiveness, migrants' transfers)
  • Non-resident investments in domestic assets
  • Reserve assets transactions

Financial Account:

  • Direct investment flows
  • Portfolio investment flows
  • Other investment flows
  • Reserve assets

Point 2: BoP Crisis Indicators

Warning Signs:

  • Continuous current account deficit
  • Depleting foreign exchange reserves
  • Widening fiscal deficit
  • Rising external debt
  • Currency depreciation pressures

Point 3: BoP Management Tools

Expenditure Switching:

  • Exchange rate devaluation
  • Import substitution policies
  • Export promotion incentives

Expenditure Reducing:

  • Fiscal consolidation
  • Monetary tightening
  • Structural reforms
Indian Example — 1991 BoP Crisis

The 1991 Crisis: India's current account deficit ballooned to $10 billion, forex reserves fell to $1.2 billion (2 weeks of imports), and India had to pledge 47 tonnes of gold to Bank of England. The crisis forced India to adopt LPG reforms. Fast forward to 2024: India's forex reserves are $650+ billion (10+ months of imports), and India is the world's 5th largest economy. The 1991 crisis was the best thing that happened to India's economy — it forced reform.

Exam Tip

A common exam question: "Differentiate between Current Account Deficit (CAD) and Fiscal Deficit." CAD = imports > exports (external imbalance), Fiscal Deficit = government spending > revenue (internal imbalance). Both can be related: high fiscal deficit → higher imports → higher CAD. India targets CAD < 2.5% of GDP and fiscal deficit < 4.5% of GDP (FY2026 target).

Indian Example — Trade Agreements

India's FTA Network: India has signed FTAs with ASEAN (2010), Japan (2011), South Korea (2010), and the UAE (2022). The India-UAE CEPA boosted bilateral trade by 16% in the first year. India is negotiating FTAs with EU, UK, Canada, and Israel. However, India walked out of RCEP (2020) citing concerns about Chinese goods flooding Indian markets — a protectionist stance to safeguard domestic manufacturing.

Exam Tip — India's Export Composition

India's top exports: (1) Petroleum products (refinery), (2) Gems & jewelry, (3) Pharmaceuticals, (4) IT services, (5) Chemicals. Top imports: (1) Crude oil, (2) Gold, (3) Electronics, (4) Coal, (5) Machinery. India has a structural trade deficit because oil and gold imports are massive and non-negotiable. Key exam fact: India's services exports ($325B) are stronger than merchandise exports — the "IT dividend."
