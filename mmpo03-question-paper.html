  • Q1(a) Solved - Operations Management, Goods vs Services, Scope

    Answer: Operations management is the activity of managing resources and processes that transform inputs into goods and services - it differs sharply between goods (tangible, storable) and services (intangible, perishable) and spans design, planning, control, quality and maintenance in both manufacturing and service firms.

    Operations management is the activity of managing the resources and processes that produce and deliver goods and services. It converts inputs - materials, labour, machines, money and information - into outputs through transformation processes such as manufacturing, cooking, treating, assembling and transporting. It deals with decisions on capacity, layout, process design, quality, inventory, scheduling, maintenance and supply chains.

    Goods are tangible (you can touch and store them), can be inventoried, and production is separated from consumption - a car made in January can be sold in June. Services are intangible, produced and consumed simultaneously (a haircut or a flight seat), cannot be stored, and involve the customer in the process. Services are variable because they depend on who delivers them and when, while goods can be produced to identical specifications. Because of this, service capacity planning is harder - you cannot stockpile empty hotel rooms.

    Scope of operations management: in manufacturing (for example Maruti Suzuki or Tata Steel) it covers plant location, process selection, plant layout, production planning, inventory control, quality control and maintenance. In services (for example a hospital, bank or airline) it covers facility design, capacity and queue management, appointment scheduling, service quality, and managing customer contact. Modern operations also include sustainability, supply chain management and digital technologies, which are covered in Units 3 and 14 of this course.

    Key points to remember: OM converts inputs into outputs; goods are tangible and storable, services are intangible and perishable; scope spans design, planning, control, quality and maintenance.

    Takeaway: Answer with definition + goods/services contrast + scope split between manufacturing and services, citing one Indian company for each.

  • Q1(b) Solved - Operations Strategy

    Answer: Operations strategy is the long-term pattern of decisions that aligns operations with business strategy through five competitive priorities - cost, quality, speed, flexibility and dependability - and covers process, capacity, supply-network and technology choices.

    Operations strategy is the long-term pattern of decisions that determines how the operations function produces and delivers products and services in a way that supports the overall business strategy and gives the firm a competitive advantage. It answers: what should we be good at, and how do we use our operations to win customers? The key components are: (1) competitive priorities - the goals operations pursues, namely cost (produce cheaply), quality (consistently meet specifications), speed (deliver fast), flexibility (handle variety and change) and dependability (deliver on time, every time); (2) product/service decisions - what to make, in-house versus outsource; (3) process choice - job shop, batch, mass or continuous flow, matching process to volume and variety; (4) capacity and facility decisions - how much capacity, where and what kind of plants; (5) supply network decisions - suppliers, outsourcing, vertical integration; (6) technology decisions - automation, ERP, robotics.

    A company cannot excel at everything - it must choose priorities that match its customers. For example, a luxury brand like Titan competes on quality and design, a low-cost airline like IndiGo competes on cost and speed, and a furniture maker competing on variety needs flexible processes. Strategy should be aligned: if marketing promises next-day delivery, operations must build the speed and capacity to deliver it. Operations strategy thus converts business strategy into concrete operating decisions.

    Key points to remember: operations strategy aligns operations with business strategy; five competitive priorities - cost, quality, speed, flexibility, dependability; decisions cover process, capacity, supply network and technology.

    Takeaway: Always list the five competitive priorities and one alignment example, like IndiGo competing on cost and speed.

  • Q2(a) Solved - Capacity Utilization, Efficiency and Break-Even

    Answer: Utilization is 60% (600/1,000) and efficiency is 75% (600/800); break-even is 10,000 units or Rs 5,00,000 in sales value.

    Part 1 - Capacity measures: Utilization = (actual output / design capacity) × 100 = (600 / 1,000) × 100 = 60%. This tells us how much of the theoretical maximum capacity is being used. Efficiency = (actual output / effective capacity) × 100 = (600 / 800) × 100 = 75%. Effective capacity is lower than design capacity because of maintenance, changeovers and lunch breaks; efficiency measures output against this realistic figure.

    Part 2 - Break-even analysis: Contribution per unit = selling price - variable cost = 50 - 30 = Rs 20. Break-even point in units = Fixed cost / Contribution per unit = 2,00,000 / 20 = 10,000 units. Break-even point in rupees = BEP in units × selling price = 10,000 × 50 = Rs 5,00,000. Verification: at 10,000 units, revenue = 5,00,000 and total cost = fixed 2,00,000 + variable 10,000 × 30 = 3,00,000, giving a total of 5,00,000 - exactly equal. Above 10,000 units the product earns profit; below it, it makes a loss.

    Answer: utilization = 60%, efficiency = 75%; BEP = 10,000 units or Rs 5,00,000 in sales value.

    Key points to remember: utilization = actual/design capacity; efficiency = actual/effective capacity; BEP units = FC/(P - VC); BEP value = BEP units × price; verify by equating total cost and revenue.

    Takeaway: Learn the two capacity ratios and the BEP formula set - they are the most repeated numericals in the paper.

  • Q2(b) Solved - Bottleneck in a Process

    Answer: A bottleneck is the lowest-capacity operation that sets the throughput of the whole process, creating waiting upstream and starvation downstream, so managers must identify, exploit and elevate it.

    A bottleneck is the operation in a process that has the lowest capacity, that is, the smallest output per unit of time. Because it is the slowest step, it limits the throughput of the entire process - the whole line can produce no more than the bottleneck can pass on, no matter how fast the other stations work. For example, if a food packaging line has washing (200 packs/hour), filling (150 packs/hour) and sealing (120 packs/hour), sealing is the bottleneck and the line cannot exceed 120 packs per hour; the washing station runs idle 40% of the time.

    Bottlenecks affect capacity in three ways: they set the maximum output of the system (throughput is governed by the bottleneck); they create waiting lines and work-in-progress inventory upstream, because faster stations push work into the queue ahead of the bottleneck; and they waste capacity downstream, because later stations remain starved. Managing the bottleneck is the core of the Theory of Constraints: identify the constraint, exploit it (keep it busy 100% of the time with buffer stocks, breaks rotated, preventive maintenance), subordinate everything else to it, and if output must rise, elevate the constraint by adding capacity - then repeat, because a new bottleneck will appear elsewhere. Bottleneck analysis is closely linked to process and capacity analysis in Unit 4.

    Key points to remember: bottleneck = slowest/lowest-capacity operation; it sets system throughput; manage it via buffer, maintenance and, if needed, extra capacity; the constraint moves after improvement.

    Takeaway: Bottleneck logic = system throughput is set by the slowest step; quote the food-packaging line example.

  • Q3(a) Solved - Types of Facility Layouts

    Answer: Layouts are chosen to match volume and variety: product layout for high-volume assembly lines, process layout for variety, cellular layout for part families, and fixed-position layout for huge immovable products.

    Product layout arranges machines and workstations in the sequence of the product's processing steps, like an assembly line; material moves continuously from one station to the next. Advantages: very high output, low handling cost, simple control, low unit cost; limitations: inflexible, high capital investment, stoppage at one station stops the line. Example: Maruti Suzuki's car assembly lines in Gurugram and Manesar.

    Process layout groups similar machines together - all lathes in one section, all welding in another - and batches move between sections as per their routing. Advantages: high flexibility for many products and volumes, better machine utilization, lower investment; limitations: more material handling, longer throughput time, more complex scheduling and control. Example: a job-order machine shop or a hospital, where patients move between radiology, pathology and consultation.

    Cellular layout groups machines needed for a family of similar parts into a cell; parts with similar processing needs flow within the cell. It combines the flexibility of process layout with the speed of product layout, reducing movement and work-in-progress; its limitation is the difficulty of defining part families. Example: cells in precision auto-component plants producing similar gear housings. Fixed-position layout keeps the product in one place and brings men, machines and materials to it. Advantages: suits huge, immovable products; limitations: low utilization of equipment, skilled coordination needed. Example: shipbuilding at Cochin Shipyard, or construction of a large dam or aircraft.

    Key points to remember: product layout = line flow, high volume-low variety; process layout = grouped machines, low volume-high variety; cellular = family-based cells; fixed-position = product stays, resources move.

    Takeaway: Match each layout to volume and variety - one Indian example per layout earns full marks.

  • Q3(b) Solved - Factors Influencing Facility Layout

    Answer: Facility layout is driven by product type, volume-variety mix, material handling, building constraints, process needs, safety, flexibility and cost - with customer flow and visibility added in services.

    A facility layout is decided by several interacting factors. First, the type of product and production system: high-volume standardized products favour product layout, low-volume customized products favour process layout, and part-family production favours cellular layout. Second, volume and variety: the volume-variety mix is the single biggest driver of layout choice. Third, material handling requirements: the nature, weight, size and fragility of materials, and the handling equipment available, influence the arrangement; layouts should minimize backtracking and unnecessary movement. Fourth, the building and space: shape, size, columns, doors, floor strength and utilities such as power, water, drainage and ventilation constrain the layout. Fifth, process requirements: machines with vibration, heat, fumes or heavy power needs must be placed appropriately, with safe distances and clearances. Sixth, safety and comfort: escape routes, fire safety, lighting, ventilation and worker convenience are mandatory considerations.

    Seventh, future flexibility: the layout must allow expansion and reconfiguration when products or volumes change. Finally, cost: the layout should minimize total cost - construction, installation, handling and operating cost combined. Service layouts add customer flow and visibility considerations, for example a supermarket arranging fast-moving items so customers pass more shelves, or a bank placing ATMs for convenience. The best layout balances smooth flow, low handling cost, safety and flexibility.

    Key points to remember: key factors - product type, volume-variety mix, material handling, building constraints, process needs, safety, flexibility, cost; services add customer flow and visibility.

    Takeaway: Remember the factor list as product-volume-handling-building-process-safety-flexibility-cost, then add the service twist.

  • Q4(a) Solved - Forecasting Numerical (Moving Average and Exponential Smoothing)

    Answer: The 3-month moving average forecast for April is 125 units; exponential smoothing with α = 0.3 gives 120, 123, 123.6 and 128.5 for February to May; and MAD of the smoothing forecasts for February-April is 9.47 units.

    Part 1 - 3-month moving average for April: F(April) = (Demand Jan + Feb + Mar)/3 = (120 + 130 + 125)/3 = 375/3 = 125 units. The moving average smooths out random fluctuations by using only recent data.

    Part 2 - Exponential smoothing with α = 0.3: formula Fₛ₁ = Fₛ + α(Dₛ - Fₛ), with initial forecast F(Jan) = D(Jan) = 120. F(Feb) = 120 + 0.3(120 - 120) = 120. F(Mar) = 120 + 0.3(130 - 120) = 120 + 3 = 123. F(Apr) = 123 + 0.3(125 - 123) = 123 + 0.6 = 123.6. F(May) = 123.6 + 0.3(140 - 123.6) = 123.6 + 4.92 = 128.5 units.

    Part 3 - MAD of the smoothing forecasts: errors for February-April: |130 - 120| = 10; |125 - 123| = 2; |140 - 123.6| = 16.4. MAD = (10 + 2 + 16.4)/3 = 28.4/3 = 9.47 units. MAD is the average absolute forecast error; here the April error is large because demand jumped to 140, which a smoothing model with low α reacts to slowly. A higher α (e.g., 0.5) would track such changes faster but would also be more volatile.

    Key points to remember: MA = average of last n periods; Fₛ₁ = Fₛ + α(Dₛ - Fₛ); high α = fast response, low α = more smoothing; MAD = average of |actual - forecast| over the periods.

    Takeaway: Moving average uses recent data, smoothing uses all past forecasts; a high MAD signals fast-changing demand.

  • Q4(b) Solved - Qualitative vs Quantitative Forecasting

    Answer: Qualitative methods rely on judgment (Delphi, surveys) for new products and scarce data, while quantitative methods extrapolate history through time-series and causal models - best practice combines both and tracks forecast errors.

    Qualitative (judgmental) methods rely on opinion, intuition and experience rather than historical numbers. They include: Delphi method (experts answer questionnaires in rounds until consensus), executive opinion (senior managers pool their views), sales force composite (field salespeople estimate future demand in their territories), and market research / consumer surveys. They are used when data is scarce, when launching new products, or when the environment changes suddenly - for example, estimating demand for a new smartphone model or forecasting sales during a pandemic. Their weakness is subjectivity and bias.

    Quantitative methods use past data and mathematical models. Time series methods extrapolate patterns in history: moving averages, exponential smoothing, trend projection and seasonal decomposition. Causal methods link demand to explanatory factors - regression analysis and econometric models, for example relating car sales to income and fuel prices. Quantitative methods are objective and repeatable but assume that the past pattern will continue, which fails when market structure changes. In practice, firms combine both: quantitative models provide the base forecast and qualitative judgment adjusts it for known events such as promotions, strikes or new competitors. A good forecasting system also tracks errors (MAD, MSE, MAPE) to choose the best model and to trigger review when accuracy deteriorates.

    Key points to remember: qualitative = judgment (Delphi, surveys), for new products and scarce data; quantitative = time series and causal models, needs history; best practice combines both and tracks error measures.

    Takeaway: Contrast judgment vs data, then say real firms combine both and monitor MAD, MSE and MAPE.

  • Q5(a) Solved - EOQ, Total Cost and Reorder Point Numerical

    Answer: EOQ is 100 units (√(2 × 1,200 × 100 / 24)), giving 12 orders a year at a total annual cost of Rs 2,400, and the reorder point is 30 units (4 × 5 + 10).

    Given: annual demand D = 1,200 units; ordering cost S = Rs 100 per order; holding cost H = Rs 24 per unit per year.

    Step 1 - EOQ: EOQ = √(2DS/H) = √(2 × 1,200 × 100 / 24) = √(2,40,000/24) = √10,000 = 100 units. So the company should order 100 units each time.

    Step 2 - Number of orders and total cost: Number of orders = D/EOQ = 1,200/100 = 12 orders per year. Ordering cost = 12 × 100 = Rs 1,200. Average inventory = EOQ/2 = 50 units; holding cost = 50 × 24 = Rs 1,200. Total annual cost = ordering + holding = 1,200 + 1,200 = Rs 2,400. At EOQ the two costs are equal - this is the defining property of the optimum.

    Step 3 - Reorder point: Daily demand d = D/working days = 1,200/300 = 4 units per day. ROP = d × lead time + safety stock = 4 × 5 + 10 = 20 + 10 = 30 units. The company should place a new order when stock falls to 30 units; the 10 units of safety stock protect against demand or lead-time variation, and the order of 100 units arrives exactly when the safety stock would begin to be consumed.

    Key points to remember: EOQ = √(2DS/H); ordering cost = (D/Q)S, holding cost = (Q/2)H; at EOQ both are equal; ROP = daily demand × lead time + safety stock.

    Takeaway: At EOQ, ordering cost equals holding cost - that equality is the easiest way to verify your answer.

  • Q5(b) Solved - ABC Analysis of Inventory

    Answer: ABC analysis classifies inventory by annual usage value (unit cost × demand) - few high-value A items get tight control while many low-value C items get simple control - so management effort follows money.

    ABC analysis (also called Pareto analysis or the 80-20 rule) classifies inventory items into three classes by annual usage value, which is unit cost multiplied by annual demand. Class A items have the highest annual usage value - usually the top 10-20% of items accounting for 60-80% of total value; Class B items are the middle group, roughly 20-30% of items and 15-25% of value; Class C items are numerous but cheap - 50-70% of items contributing only 5-10% of value. The classification is done by listing all items with their usage value, sorting them in descending order, and cumulating value percentages to fix the A, B and C boundaries.

    Control effort is then matched to importance. A items deserve tight control: frequent review, accurate records, lower safety stock with careful reorder points, daily or weekly counting, and managerial attention, since they tie up most capital. B items get moderate control: monthly review, normal records and periodic counting. C items get simple control: high safety stock, bulk ordering, less frequent review and yearly counting, because the cost of detailed control would exceed any saving. For example, an electronics warehouse may treat smartphones (A items) with real-time tracking, place fast-moving electronics near dispatch doors, and keep cables and screws (C items) in bulk bins with simple reorder cards. ABC analysis thus concentrates management effort where it saves the most money.

    Key points to remember: classify by annual usage value = cost × demand; A = few items, high value - tight control; C = many items, low value - simple control; effort follows value.

    Takeaway: Always define classes by annual usage value (cost x demand), not by quantity.

  • Q6(a) Solved - Six Sigma DMAIC and TQM Principles

    Answer: Six Sigma cuts defects to 3.4 per million opportunities through the DMAIC cycle (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control), and TQM rests on customer focus, continuous improvement, employee involvement and data-driven decisions.

    Six Sigma is a disciplined, data-driven methodology for reducing defects to 3.4 per million opportunities. It uses the DMAIC cycle for improvement of existing processes: Define the problem, the customer requirements and project scope; Measure current performance by collecting data on the defect rate and the key metrics; Analyze the data to find the root causes of variation using tools such as cause-effect diagrams, Pareto charts and hypothesis tests; Improve by designing and piloting solutions that remove the root causes; Control by putting in place monitoring systems - control charts, standard operating procedures - so the gains are sustained. (For new product/process design, DMADV - Define, Measure, Analyze, Design, Verify - is used.) The statistical definition of Six Sigma means process capability is so high that only 3.4 defects occur per million opportunities.

    TQM (Total Quality Management) is a management philosophy where everyone in the organization, at every level, continuously works on improving quality. Its key principles: customer focus (quality means satisfying customers, internal and external); continuous improvement (Kaizen - small improvements every day); employee involvement and empowerment (teams, suggestion schemes, quality circles); process-centred thinking with data-driven decisions; top-management commitment; and supplier partnership. Tools like PDCA, Ishikawa diagrams and statistical process control support it. Indian examples include Tata Steel's quality circles and Maruti Suzuki's TQM culture; both approaches aim at the same goal - zero defects and total customer satisfaction.

    Key points to remember: DMAIC = Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control; Six Sigma target = 3.4 defects per million; TQM pillars = customer focus, continuous improvement, employee involvement, data-driven decisions.

    Takeaway: Define DMAIC step by step, pair it with the TQM pillar list, and close with the JIT-lean link.

  • Q6(b) Solved - Maintenance Management and Types of Maintenance

    Answer: Maintenance management keeps equipment reliable, safe and economical, blending breakdown repair (fix after failure), preventive maintenance (fix at fixed intervals) and predictive maintenance (fix when condition monitoring signals failure).

    Maintenance management is the set of activities that keeps machines, equipment and facilities in a condition that allows reliable, safe and economical operation. Poor maintenance causes breakdowns, quality problems, delivery delays and accidents, so maintenance planning - schedules, spare parts stock, crew training and records - is a core operations function (Unit 14 of this course).

    Breakdown (corrective) maintenance repairs equipment only after it fails. It is simple and uses the machine's full life, but it causes sudden stoppages, emergency costs, and can damage other equipment or workers. It suits cheap, non-critical equipment where breakdowns are rare or cheap. Preventive maintenance performs planned inspections, lubrication, adjustments and part replacement at fixed time intervals, based on the item's expected life, to prevent failures before they occur. It reduces breakdowns and extends life but costs money even when the machine would have run fine, and replacement intervals may be too early or too late. Predictive maintenance monitors the condition of equipment continuously - vibration, temperature, oil analysis - and intervenes only when the data signals that a failure is approaching, so maintenance is done just in time; it minimizes both breakdowns and unnecessary maintenance but needs sensors and analytical skills. Modern plants combine all three, using preventive schedules for routine items and predictive monitoring for critical machines such as turbines, crushers and CNC machines.

    Key points to remember: breakdown = fix after failure; preventive = fix at fixed intervals; predictive = fix when condition monitoring signals failure; the mix balances cost against downtime risk.

    Takeaway: Compare the three maintenance types on one axis - when the fix happens: after failure, at fixed intervals, or on condition.
