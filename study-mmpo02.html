Unit 1: Introduction to Project Management

Definition

Project: A temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result. Project Management: Application of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to meet project requirements.

Key Distinctions: Projects are temporary (have a definite beginning and end) and unique (produce something new). Operations are ongoing and repetitive. Programs are groups of related projects managed in a coordinated way. Portfolios are collections of projects and programs managed together for strategic objectives.

▶
Project Management Fundamentals
▶
What is a Project?

Project: A temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result. Project Management: Application of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to meet project requirements.

Key Concepts

  • Iron Triangle: The four competing constraints â€” Scope, Time, Cost, Quality
  • Project Life Cycle: Initiation > Planning > Execution > Monitoring & Controlling > Closure
  • Stakeholders: Sponsor, project team, customers, end users, PM, functional managers
  • Project vs Operations: Temporary & unique vs Ongoing & repetitive
  • Program: A group of related projects managed in a coordinated way
  • Portfolio: A collection of projects and programs managed together for strategic objectives

Project Management Knowledge Areas (PMI)

  1. Integration Management: Coordinating all project elements
  2. Scope Management: Defining and controlling what's included
  3. Schedule Management: Planning and controlling timelines
  4. Cost Management: Budgeting and cost control
  5. Quality Management: Ensuring standards are met
  6. Resource Management: Managing team and materials
  7. Communications Management: Stakeholder information flow
  8. Risk Management: Identifying and responding to uncertainties
  9. Procurement Management: Acquiring external resources
  10. Stakeholder Management: Engaging all affected parties
Real-World Example

Building a new airport terminal is a project (temporary, unique). Running the airport daily is operations (ongoing, repetitive).

Unit 2: Project Feasibility Analysis

Definition

Feasibility Analysis: Assessment of a proposed project's practicality and viability. Determines whether the project should proceed, be modified, or abandoned. Evaluates technical, economic, legal, operational, and schedule aspects (TELOS framework).

▶
NPV Explained
▶
IRR vs NPV

NPV: The difference between the present value of cash inflows and outflows over time. IRR: The discount rate that makes NPV zero. Payback Period: Time required to recover the cost of investment.

Key Concepts

  • Feasibility Types (TELOS): Technical, Economic, Legal, Operational, Schedule
  • Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA): Compare total costs vs total benefits in monetary terms
  • NPV: Sum of all discounted future cash flows. NPV > 0: Accept | NPV < 0: Reject
  • IRR: Discount rate at which NPV = 0. IRR > Required rate: Accept
  • Payback Period: Time to recover the initial investment (simplest method)
  • ROI: (Net Profit / Cost of Investment) x 100

Feasibility Analysis Framework (TELOS)

TypeKey QuestionExample
TechnicalCan we build it?Technology availability, skilled labor
EconomicShould we build it?NPV, IRR, Payback analysis
LegalAre we allowed to?Permits, regulations, compliance
OperationalWill it work?Integration with existing systems
ScheduleCan we do it on time?Timeline feasibility, deadlines

Investment Appraisal Methods Comparison

MethodFormulaDecision RuleAdvantageLimitation

NPV Calculation Example

Project costs Rs 100 lakh. Cash inflows: Year 1 = Rs 30 lakh, Year 2 = Rs 40 lakh, Year 3 = Rs 50 lakh. Discount rate = 10%.

  • Year 1 PV = 30 / (1.10)^1 = Rs 27.27 lakh
  • Year 2 PV = 40 / (1.10)^2 = Rs 33.06 lakh
  • Year 3 PV = 50 / (1.10)^3 = Rs 37.57 lakh
  • Total PV = Rs 97.90 lakh
  • NPV = 97.90 - 100 = Rs -2.10 lakh (Reject)
Real-World Example

A company evaluating a new factory: if NPV = Rs 50 lakh at 10% discount rate, the project creates value and should be accepted.

Exam Tip

NPV and IRR calculations are almost guaranteed in exams. Practice numerical problems with different cash flow patterns.

Memory Aid

TELOS = Technical, Economic, Legal, Operational, Schedule feasibility

Summary
  • TELOS: Technical, Economic, Legal, Operational, Schedule
  • NPV > 0 accept; IRR > required rate accept
  • Payback = simplest; ROI = (Net Profit / Investment) x 100

Unit 4: Project Scope Management

Definition

Scope Management: The processes required to ensure that the project includes all the work required, and only the work required, to complete the project successfully. It defines what is inside and outside the project boundaries, preventing scope creep and ensuring stakeholder alignment.

▶
WBS Work Breakdown
▶
Scope Management

WBS: A deliverable-oriented grouping of project elements that organizes and defines the total scope. Work Package: The lowest level of WBS; can be estimated for cost and duration. Scope Creep: Uncontrolled scope expansion.

Key Concepts

  • Scope Statement: Defines what is IN and OUT of the project (boundaries)
  • WBS: Hierarchical decomposition of total work into manageable chunks
  • Work Packages: Lowest level of WBS where time, cost, and resources can be estimated
  • Responsibility Matrix (RM): Maps tasks to team members (who does what)
  • Scope Creep: Uncontrolled expansion of project scope without adjustments to time, cost, or resources
  • Gold Plating: Adding extra features not requested by customer (different from scope creep)

WBS Levels

  1. Level 1: Project (complete)
  2. Level 2: Major Deliverables (2-10 parts)
  3. Level 3-4: Sub-deliverables
  4. Level 5: Cost/Resource Account
  5. Level 6: Work Packages

WBS Creation Approaches

AnalogyUse WBS from similar past projectRecurring project types
Rolling WaveDetail near-term, plan later in phasesLong projects with uncertainty

Real-World Example

Building a motorcycle WBS: Motor > Frame > Gears > Wheels > Brakes, each broken into sub-components (design, materials, assembly).

WBS diagram questions are common. Practice drawing WBS for real-world scenarios (wedding, software project, construction).

  • Work packages = lowest level, estimable
  • Scope creep = uncontrolled expansion without adjusting constraints

Unit 5: Project Network Analysis (PERT & CPM)

Definition

Project Network Analysis: A set of techniques used to plan, schedule, and control projects. PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique) uses probabilistic time estimates for uncertain projects. CPM (Critical Path Method) uses deterministic estimates for well-understood projects. Both identify the critical path â€” the longest sequence of activities that determines minimum project duration.

▶
PERT and CPM
▶
Critical Path Method

Critical Path: The longest sequence of activities that determines the minimum project duration. Float/Slack: The amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the project. PERT: Probabilistic time estimation using three estimates. CPM: Deterministic time estimation using a single estimate.

Key Concepts

  • PERT: Program Evaluation and Review Technique (probabilistic â€” uses 3 time estimates)
  • Expected Time = (O + 4M + P) / 6
  • Variance = ((P - O) / 6)^2
  • O = Optimistic, M = Most Likely, P = Pessimistic
  • CPM: Critical Path Method (deterministic â€” single time estimate)
  • Critical Path: Longest path through the network (zero float)
  • Float/Slack: LS - ES = LF - EF
  • Forward Pass: Calculate Early Start (ES) and Early Finish (EF)
  • Backward Pass: Calculate Late Start (LS) and Late Finish (LF)

Network Diagram Types

  • AOA (Activity on Arrow): Activities represented by arrows, nodes represent events
  • AON (Activity on Node): Activities represented by nodes, arrows show dependencies

Activity with O = 4 days, M = 6 days, P = 14 days.

  • Expected Time = (4 + 4Ã—6 + 14) / 6 = 42/6 = 7 days
  • Variance = ((14 - 4) / 6)^2 = (10/6)^2 = 2.78
  • Standard Deviation = sqrt(2.78) = 1.67 days

Real-World Example

In a house construction project, the critical path might be: Foundation > Walls > Roof > Finishing. Any delay in these delays the whole project.

Unit 6: Project Scheduling

Definition

Project Scheduling: The process of determining the planned start and finish dates for project activities. Scheduling creates a time-based plan that shows when each activity should start and end, identifies dependencies, and establishes milestones. Gantt charts and network diagrams are the primary scheduling tools.

▶
Gantt Charts
▶
Project Scheduling

Gantt Chart: A bar chart that shows project activities against time. Milestone: A significant point or event in the project (zero duration).

Key Concepts

  • Gantt Chart: Horizontal bar chart showing activities vs time (visual scheduling tool)
  • Milestones: Key checkpoints/deliverables in the project (zero-duration events)
  • Resource Loading: Assigning resources to activities
  • Resource Leveling: Smoothing resource usage to avoid peaks and valleys
  • Slack Time: How long an activity can be delayed without affecting project end date
  • Resource Smoothing: Adjusting activities within float to level resources

Scheduling Terminology

  • Activity Duration: Time required to complete an activity
  • Lead Time: Overlap between activities (start successor before predecessor finishes)
  • Lag Time: Delay between activities (must wait after predecessor finishes)
  • Fast Tracking: Performing activities in parallel
  • Crashing: Adding resources to shorten duration
Real-World Example

A Gantt chart for a software project shows coding (weeks 1-4), testing (weeks 3-5), deployment (week 6) as parallel bars.

Exam Tip

Gantt charts vs Network diagrams â€” know when to use each. Gantt = time visualization. Network = dependency and critical path.

  • Gantt = visual time chart; Milestones = zero-duration events
  • Resource leveling smooths peaks and valleys
  • Gantt for time viz, Network for dependencies

Unit 7: Project Crashing

Definition

Project Crashing: A schedule compression technique that reduces project duration by adding resources to critical path activities. The goal is to achieve the maximum schedule compression with the least cost increase. Only critical path activities can be crashed, and the cheapest crash option is selected first.

▶
Project Crashing
▶
Fast Tracking

Crashing: Compressing the schedule by adding resources to critical path activities. Fast Tracking: Performing phases in parallel that would normally be done sequentially.

Key Concepts

  • Crashing: Reducing project duration by adding resources (cost-time tradeoff)
  • Fast Tracking: Performing activities in parallel that were originally sequential
  • Crash Cost per Period = (Crash Cost - Normal Cost) / (Normal Time - Crash Time)
  • Rule: Crash critical path activities first, starting with the cheapest option

Crashing vs Fast Tracking

AspectCrashingFast Tracking
MethodAdd resources (overtime, extra staff)Perform activities in parallel
Cost ImpactIncreases costMay not increase cost
RiskLower riskHigher risk (rework)
Best ForCritical path activitiesActivities with dependencies
LimitationNot all activities can be crashedNot all activities can overlap

Crashing Procedure

  1. Identify the critical path
  2. Find activities that can be crashed
  3. Calculate crash cost per period for each
  4. Select cheapest critical activity to crash
  5. Crash by one time period
  6. Recalculate critical path (may change)
  7. Repeat until target duration reached or no more crashing possible

Crashing Example

ActivityNormal TimeCrash TimeNormal CostCrash CostCost/Period
A5 days3 daysRs 10,000Rs 14,000Rs 2,000/day
B4 days2 daysRs 8,000Rs 12,000Rs 2,000/day
C6 days4 daysRs 12,000Rs 18,000Rs 3,000/day

If A, B, C are all on critical path: Crash A first (cheapest at Rs 2,000/day), then B (same cost), then C.

Real-World Example

A delayed building project: hire extra workers (crashing) or start interior work before exterior is complete (fast tracking).

Exam Tip

Crashing numerical problems are very common. Practice calculating crash cost per period and determining which activities to crash.

Memory Aid

"Crash Cheap Critical First" = Always crash the cheapest critical activity

Summary
  • Crashing = add resources; Fast tracking = parallel activities
  • Crash Cost/Period = (CC-NC)/(NT-CT)
  • Always crash cheapest critical activity first

Unit 8: Earned Value Analysis (EVA)

Definition

Earned Value Analysis: A project management technique that integrates scope, schedule, and cost data to assess project performance and progress. It answers: "Are we on time?" and "Are we on budget?" by comparing what we planned (PV), what we accomplished (EV), and what we spent (AC).

▶
Earned Value Analysis
▶
EVM Explained

Earned Value: The value of work actually completed, measured in budget terms. CPI: Cost Performance Index â€” efficiency of budget utilization. SPI: Schedule Performance Index â€” efficiency of time utilization.

Key Concepts

  • PV (Planned Value): Budgeted cost of work scheduled (what we planned to spend)
  • EV (Earned Value): Budgeted cost of work performed (what we actually accomplished)
  • AC (Actual Cost): Actual cost of work performed (what we actually spent)
  • BAC (Budget at Completion): Total approved budget
  • CV = EV - AC (Cost Variance; positive = under budget)
  • SV = EV - PV (Schedule Variance; positive = ahead of schedule)
  • CPI = EV / AC (>1 = efficient; <1 = over budget)
  • SPI = EV / PV (>1 = ahead; <1 = behind)
  • EAC = BAC / CPI (Estimate at Completion)
  • ETC = EAC - AC (Estimate to Complete)
  • VAC = BAC - EAC (Variance at Completion)
  • TCPI = (BAC - EV) / (BAC - AC) (To-Complete Performance Index)

Complete EVA Example

Given: Project BAC = Rs 500 lakh. At month 6: PV = Rs 200 lakh, EV = Rs 180 lakh, AC = Rs 220 lakh.

  • CV = 180 - 220 = Rs -40 lakh (over budget)
  • SV = 180 - 200 = Rs -20 lakh (behind schedule)
  • CPI = 180 / 220 = 0.82 (spending Rs 1.22 for every Rs 1 of work)
  • SPI = 180 / 200 = 0.90 (completing 90% of planned work)
  • EAC = 500 / 0.82 = Rs 609.76 lakh (project will cost Rs 109.76 lakh more)
  • ETC = 609.76 - 220 = Rs 389.76 lakh (remaining cost)
  • VAC = 500 - 609.76 = Rs -109.76 lakh (overrun)

Real-World Example

Project BAC = Rs 100 lakh. At month 3: PV = Rs 30 lakh, EV = Rs 25 lakh, AC = Rs 35 lakh. CPI = 25/35 = 0.71 (over budget). SPI = 25/30 = 0.83 (behind schedule).

Exam Tip

EVA is the HIGHEST frequency numerical question. Memorize: CV = EV-AC, SV = EV-PV, CPI = EV/AC, SPI = EV/PV. Always EV comes first!

  • CV = EV - AC; SV = EV - PV
  • CPI = EV/AC; SPI = EV/PV
  • EAC = BAC / CPI

Unit 10: Project Monitoring and Control

Definition

Monitoring & Control: The process of tracking, reviewing, and regulating project progress and performance. Monitoring involves collecting data on actual performance, while controlling involves comparing actual vs planned performance and taking corrective action when necessary. This ensures the project stays on track and deviations are addressed promptly.

▶
Monitoring and Control
▶
Change Control

Change Control Board (CCB): Group that reviews, approves, or rejects change requests. Baseline: The approved version of the project plan against which progress is measured.

Key Concepts

  • Monitoring: Collecting data on actual performance (passive observation)
  • Controlling: Comparing actual vs planned and taking corrective action (active intervention)
  • Change Control: Formal process for managing changes to project baselines
  • Configuration Management: Tracking versions of deliverables and documentation
  • Variance Analysis: Comparing planned vs actual performance

Monitoring vs Controlling

AspectMonitoringControlling
FocusCollecting dataTaking action
NaturePassive (observe)Active (intervene)
OutputPerformance reportsChange requests, corrective actions
Question"What is happening?""What should we do about it?"

Change Control Process

  1. Identify Change: Recognize need for change
  2. Document Change: Create change request form
  3. Evaluate Impact: Assess effect on scope, time, cost, quality
  4. CCB Review: Change Control Board reviews request
  5. Approve/Reject: CCB makes decision
  6. Update Baselines: If approved, update project plans
  7. Communicate: Inform stakeholders of changes
  8. Track: Monitor implementation of change

Configuration Management Activities

  • Configuration Identification: Identify items to be controlled
  • Configuration Control: Manage changes to configuration items
  • Configuration Status Accounting: Track current status of items
  • Configuration Audit: Verify items match specifications

Change Request Form Contents

  • Change ID and date
  • Description of change
  • Reason for change
  • Impact analysis (scope, time, cost, quality)
  • Alternatives considered
  • Recommendation
  • Approval/rejection with date
Real-World Example

Client requests a new feature. The change request goes to CCB, which evaluates impact on scope, time, and cost before approving or rejecting.

Exam Tip

Distinguish between monitoring (data collection) and controlling (corrective action). Change control process steps are a common question.

Memory Aid

M-C-V = Monitor, Control, Variance analysis

Summary
  • Monitoring = data collection; Controlling = corrective action
  • Change control = formal process for baseline changes
  • Configuration management = version tracking

Unit 11: Project Risk Management

Definition

Risk Management: The systematic process of identifying, analyzing, and responding to project risks. Risks can be negative (threats) or positive (opportunities). Effective risk management increases the probability of project success by proactively addressing uncertainties before they become issues.

▶
Risk Management
▶
Risk Register

Risk Register: A document listing all identified risks, their analysis, and response plans. Contingency Reserve: Budget/time set aside for identified risks. Management Reserve: Budget/time set aside for unknown risks.

Key Concepts

  • Risk: An uncertain event that, if it occurs, affects project objectives (positive or negative)
  • Risk Identification: Brainstorming, Delphi technique, SWOT, checklists, interviews
  • Qualitative Analysis: Probability-Impact matrix (prioritize risks)
  • Quantitative Analysis: Monte Carlo simulation, decision trees, sensitivity analysis
  • Negative Risk Response (ATMA): Avoid, Transfer, Mitigate, Accept
  • Positive Risk Response (ESEA): Exploit, Share, Enhance, Accept
  • Contingency Plan: Response for identified risks (Plan B)
  • Fallback Plan: Response for residual risks that remain after contingency

Risk Management Process

  1. Plan Risk Management: Define how to approach risk activities
  2. Identify Risks: Determine which risks may affect the project
  3. Qualitative Analysis: Prioritize risks by probability and impact
  4. Quantitative Analysis: Numerically analyze risk effects
  5. Plan Responses: Develop options to enhance opportunities and reduce threats
  6. Monitor Risks: Track identified risks, identify new ones, evaluate process

Probability-Impact Matrix

Probability / ImpactLow ImpactMedium ImpactHigh Impact

Negative vs Positive Risk Responses

Risk Register Contents

  • Risk ID and description
  • Risk category (technical, external, organizational, PM)
  • Probability (1-5 scale)
  • Impact (1-5 scale)
  • Risk score (Probability x Impact)
  • Priority (High/Medium/Low)
  • Response strategy (ATMA/ESEA)
  • Response plan details
  • Owner (who is responsible)
  • Status (open/closed)
Real-World Example

Construction project risk: monsoon delay (identified risk). Contingency plan: schedule buffer. Transfer: insurance for equipment damage.

Exam Tip

Risk response strategies (ATMA for negative) are frequently asked. Know the difference between contingency and fallback plans.

Memory Aid

Unit 13: Project Contracts and Partnering

Definition

Project Contract: A legally binding agreement between the buyer and seller that defines the terms and conditions under which work will be performed. Contract types include Fixed Price, Cost Reimbursable, and Time & Material, each with different risk allocations between buyer and seller.

▶
Project Contracts
▶
BOT Model

Fixed Price Contract: Total price is set; seller absorbs cost overruns. Cost Reimbursable: Buyer pays all legitimate costs plus a fee to the seller. BOT: A project delivery method where the contractor builds, operates for a period, then transfers ownership.

Key Concepts

  • Fixed Price (FP): Seller bears cost risk (buyer knows total cost upfront)
  • Cost Reimbursable (CR): Buyer bears cost risk (seller gets actual costs + fee)
  • Time & Material (T&M): Hybrid (hourly/daily rates + materials)
  • PPP (Public-Private Partnership): Government + Private sector collaboration
  • BOT: Build-Operate-Transfer (private builds, operates, then transfers to government)
  • BOOT: Build-Own-Operate-Transfer

Unit 14: Project Audit and Closure

Definition

Project Audit: An independent examination of project performance, processes, and deliverables to assess whether the project met its objectives. Project Closure: The final process group that formally completes all project activities, releases resources, and archives documentation. Proper closure ensures lessons are captured and organizational knowledge is preserved.

▶
Project Closure
▶
Lessons Learned

Lessons Learned: Knowledge gained during a project that can be used to improve future projects. Administrative Closure: The process of finalizing all activities across all process groups.

Key Concepts

  • Project Audit: Independent examination of project performance and processes
  • Concurrent Audit: During the project | Post-Project Audit: After completion
  • Closure Types:
    • Normal: Completed as planned
    • Premature: Completed early (scope reduced)
    • Perpetual: Never-ending (continuous changes)
    • Failed: Did not meet objectives
    • Changed Priority: Cancelled due to strategic shift
  • Lessons Learned: Document what went well and what didn't
  • Administrative Closure: Finalize all activities, release resources, archive documents
  • Contract Closure: Complete and settle each contract

Lessons Learned Best Practices

  • Document throughout the project (not just at the end)
  • Include both positive and negative experiences
  • Be specific â€” what happened, why, what to do differently
  • Make lessons accessible to future project teams
  • Review lessons at the start of new projects
  • Assign owners for implementing improvements

Project Closure Checklist

  • Verify all deliverables accepted by customer
  • Complete all contract closures
  • Document lessons learned
  • Release project resources (team, equipment)
  • Archive project documents
  • Update organizational process assets
  • Celebrate success with team
  • Conduct post-project review/audit
Real-World Example

After completing a software project, the team holds a retrospective: "We should have involved QA earlier" becomes a lesson learned for the next project.
