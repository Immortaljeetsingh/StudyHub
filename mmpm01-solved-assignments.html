Q1. Define consumer behaviour and explain its three components with suitable examples. (10 marks)

Answer: Consumer behaviour (CB) is the study of the processes individuals and organizations use to select, purchase, use, and dispose of products, services, ideas, or experiences to satisfy their needs and desires. It draws on psychology, sociology, anthropology, and economics, and helps marketers segment markets, position brands, and design effective strategies. The three components are: (1) the cognitive component - beliefs and knowledge, such as a buyer who believes Bournvita contains DHA for brain development; (2) the affective component - feelings and emotions, such as the nostalgia a consumer feels for Amul's "Utterly Butterly" advertisements; and (3) the behavioural component - the action tendency, such as actually buying Maggi because the brand is linked with childhood comfort. A good Indian illustration is Flipkart's Big Billion Days: the cognitive component is comparing prices and deals, the affective component is the excitement of flash sales, and the behavioural component is stockpiling products during the sale. Marketers must understand all three because a favourable belief without positive emotion rarely converts into a purchase.

Key points:

  • Consumer behaviour (CB) is the study of the processes individuals and organizations use to select, purchase, use, and dispose of products, services, ideas, or experiences to satisfy their needs and desires.
  • The three components are: (1) the cognitive component - beliefs and knowledge, such as a buyer who believes Bournvita contains DHA for brain development; (2) the affective component - feelings and emotions, such as the nostalgia a consumer feels for Amul's "Utterly Butterly" advertisements; and (3) the behavioural component - the action tendency, such as actually buying Maggi because the brand is linked with childhood comfort.
  • It draws on psychology, sociology, anthropology, and economics, and helps marketers segment markets, position brands, and design effective strategies.

Takeaway: Marketers must understand all three because a favourable belief without positive emotion rarely converts into a purchase.

Q2. Explain Maslow's hierarchy of needs and its application in Indian marketing. (10 marks)

Answer: Maslow (1943) proposed that human needs are arranged in five levels, and lower-order needs must be reasonably satisfied before higher-order needs motivate behaviour. (1) Physiological needs - food, water, shelter; Amul milk, Tata Salt, and Britannia bread cater to these. (2) Safety needs - security, health, and financial stability; LIC insurance, Godrej locks, and Himalaya personal care address them. (3) Belongingness needs - love, family, and social acceptance; Tanishq jewellery and Cadbury celebrations tap into family bonding. (4) Esteem needs - status, recognition, and self-respect; Royal Enfield, Apple iPhone, and luxury brands appeal here. (5) Self-actualization - reaching one's full potential; Patanjali's spiritual branding and organic food brands resonate with this level. Application: a marketer must identify which need level the product serves and design the message accordingly. For example, a two-wheeler advertisement may sell safety (ABS, helmet) to a family-oriented buyer but freedom and adventure to a young professional. The hierarchy is not rigid - in Indian markets, festival buying often triggers several levels at once.

Key points:

  • Maslow (1943) proposed that human needs are arranged in five levels, and lower-order needs must be reasonably satisfied before higher-order needs motivate behaviour.
  • (1) Physiological needs - food, water, shelter; Amul milk, Tata Salt, and Britannia bread cater to these.
  • (2) Safety needs - security, health, and financial stability; LIC insurance, Godrej locks, and Himalaya personal care address them.

Takeaway: The hierarchy is not rigid - in Indian markets, festival buying often triggers several levels at once.

Q3. Explain the five stages of the consumer decision process with an Indian example. (10 marks)

Answer: The consumer decision process (CDP) has five stages: (1) Problem recognition - a gap between the actual and desired state, triggered by internal stimuli such as hunger or external stimuli such as an advertisement; (2) Information search - internal search from memory and external search through friends, YouTube reviews, and stores; (3) Evaluation of alternatives - comparing brands on criteria such as price, quality, and features, and forming an evoked set; (4) Purchase decision - choosing the brand, store, timing, and payment method, often using EMI and festival offers in India; (5) Post-purchase behaviour - satisfaction leads to loyalty and positive word-of-mouth, while dissatisfaction causes complaints and switching. Example: buying an iPhone in India. A slowing old phone creates problem recognition; the buyer watches YouTube reviews, takes friends' opinions, and visits the Apple store; the iPhone 15 is compared with the Samsung S24 and OnePlus 12; purchase happens on Flipkart during a sale; and post-purchase, the customer shares an unboxing on Instagram but may feel dissonance about the price, which Apple counters through ecosystem lock-in. Habitual purchases skip stages, while complex purchases use all five.

Key points:

  • The consumer decision process (CDP) has five stages: (1) Problem recognition - a gap between the actual and desired state, triggered by internal stimuli such as hunger or external stimuli such as an advertisement; (2) Information search - internal search from memory and external search through friends, YouTube reviews, and stores; (3) Evaluation of alternatives - comparing brands on criteria such as price, quality, and features, and forming an evoked set; (4) Purchase decision - choosing the brand, store, timing, and payment method, often using EMI and festival offers in India; (5) Post-purchase behaviour - satisfaction leads to loyalty and positive word-of-mouth, while dissatisfaction causes complaints and switching.

Q4. What is cognitive dissonance? How can marketers reduce it after purchase? (10 marks)

Answer: Cognitive dissonance, proposed by Festinger (1957), is the psychological tension a consumer experiences after purchase when they doubt whether they made the right choice. It is strongest when the purchase is expensive, infrequent, and involves close alternatives - for example, choosing between an iPhone and a Samsung flagship. Consumers try to reduce dissonance by seeking positive information about the chosen brand, avoiding negative information, rationalizing the decision, or returning the product. Marketers can reduce post-purchase dissonance by providing warranty cards and user manuals, making follow-up calls and satisfaction surveys, offering easy returns and exchange policies, and creating loyalty rewards that reinforce the decision. In India, Amazon's easy return policy and Tanishq's "exchange any gold" scheme reduce perceived post-purchase risk, while Tanishq's personalized thank-you messages, free jewellery cleaning, and event invitations create delight that drives repeat purchase - its retention rate exceeds 70 per cent. Since 96 per cent of unhappy Indian consumers never complain and simply switch brands silently, proactive post-purchase engagement is essential for building long-term loyalty.

Key points:
