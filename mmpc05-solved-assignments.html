Q1. Distinguish between primary data and secondary data, with Indian examples.

Answer:Data may be collected from two broad sources: primary and secondary. Primary data are original data collected firsthand by the investigator for the specific purpose of the study at hand. They are fresh, tailored to the research problem, and therefore more reliable, but they are costly and time-consuming to collect. Examples include Census of India enumerators visiting households and Nielsen conducting consumer surveys for Hindustan Unilever. Secondary data, on the other hand, are already in existence, having been collected by someone else for a different purpose. They are cheaper and quicker to obtain, but they may not fit the current research need perfectly. Examples include the RBI Bulletin, CMIE data, the Economic Survey of India and company annual reports. The choice between the two depends on the research objective, budget and time available. As a rule, managers should exhaust relevant secondary sources first, and use primary collection only when the required information is not available, is outdated, or must be gathered specifically for the decision in hand.

Key points:

  • Data may be collected from two broad sources: primary and secondary.

  • Primary data are original data collected firsthand by the investigator for the specific purpose of the study at hand.

Takeaway: As a rule, managers should exhaust relevant secondary sources first, and use primary collection only when the required information is not available, is outdated, or must be gathered specifically for the decision in hand.

Q2. A factory has three machines A, B and C producing 40%, 35% and 25% of the items respectively, with defect rates of 3%, 4% and 5%. An item picked at random is found defective. What is the probability that it came from machine B? State the theorem applied.

Answer:The theorem applied is Bayes' theorem, which revises prior probabilities in the light of new evidence. Let A, B and C denote production by machines A, B and C respectively, and D denote a defective item. Given P(A) = 0.40, P(B) = 0.35, P(C) = 0.25 and P(D|A) = 0.03, P(D|B) = 0.04, P(D|C) = 0.05, we require P(B|D). By Bayes' theorem: P(B|D) = [P(D|B) x P(B)] / [P(D|A)P(A) + P(D|B)P(B) + P(D|C)P(C)] = (0.04 x 0.35) / [(0.03 x 0.40) + (0.04 x 0.35) + (0.05 x 0.25)] = 0.014 / (0.012 + 0.014 + 0.0125) = 0.014 / 0.0385 = 0.3636, that is, about 36.36%. Even though machine B has the second-highest defect rate, the probability that a defective item came from it is roughly one in three because production shares differ across machines. The same technique is used in medical diagnosis and quality control to work backwards from an observed outcome to its likely cause.

Q3. When should a manager use the arithmetic mean, median and mode? Why is the geometric mean preferred for averaging growth rates?

Answer:The arithmetic mean is the most widely used average because it is based on every observation and is suited to further algebraic treatment; it should be used when the data are fairly symmetric, as in the quality control of a stable process. Its main limitation is that extreme values distort it, so it is misleading for income or property data. The median, being a positional average based only on the middle value, is robust against outliers and is the right choice for skewed data such as household incomes in India. The mode, the most frequently occurring value, is used for categorical or product decisions, such as identifying the best-selling price point or the most popular size. For growth rates, the geometric mean is preferred because the arithmetic mean overstates growth by ignoring compounding. For example, with revenue growth of 12%, 8% and 15%, the arithmetic mean is 11.67%, but the geometric mean is about 11.63%, which correctly reflects the compounded result over the three years. In general, AM ≥ GM ≥ HM, with equality only when all values are identical.

Q4. Describe the step-by-step procedure of testing of hypotheses and distinguish between Type I and Type II errors.

Answer:Testing of hypotheses is a formal procedure for deciding, on the basis of sample evidence, whether a claim about a population parameter is acceptable. The steps are: (1) state the null hypothesis H0 and the alternative hypothesis H1; (2) choose the level of significance alpha, commonly 0.05 or 0.01; (3) select the appropriate test statistic — the z-test when sigma is known or the sample is large, and the t-test when sigma is unknown and the sample is small; (4) compute the test statistic and compare it with the critical value, or convert it to a p-value; (5) draw the conclusion — reject H0 if the p-value is less than alpha, otherwise do not reject H0. Two types of error are possible. A Type I error (alpha) is rejecting a true null hypothesis, a false positive, like convicting an innocent person. A Type II error (beta) is failing to reject a false null hypothesis, a false negative, like acquitting a guilty person. Reducing alpha lowers the chance of a Type I error but increases beta, so the sample size must be large enough to keep both risks acceptable.
