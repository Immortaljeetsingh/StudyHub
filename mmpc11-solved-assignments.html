Q1. Explain the Big Five personality traits and their relevance in organisations. 10 Marks

Answer:The Big Five model, also known as OCEAN, is the most widely accepted framework for understanding personality differences in organisations. It describes five dimensions: Openness (curiosity and willingness to try new things), Conscientiousness (self-discipline, organisation and reliability), Extraversion (sociability and assertiveness), Agreeableness (cooperation and trust) and Neuroticism (emotional instability and anxiety). Together with perception, learning and attitudes, personality shapes individual behaviour in the workplace. The model helps managers understand why employees behave differently in identical situations, and supports recruitment, selection and balanced team building. For example, Infosys uses Big Five-style personality assessments during recruitment to evaluate cultural fit, while Tata Group emphasises attitude over skills on the belief that skills can be taught but attitude is innate. Conscientiousness is generally the strongest predictor of job performance across roles. In conclusion, the Big Five framework converts personality from an abstract idea into a measurable tool for selection, placement and leadership development in organisations.

Q2. Compare Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs with Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory of motivation. 10 Marks

Answer:Both are content theories of motivation, meaning they explain what motivates people at work. Maslow proposed five levels of needs arranged in a hierarchy: physiological, safety, love and belonging, esteem and self-actualisation. Lower-level needs must be substantially satisfied before higher-level needs become motivating. Herzberg proposed two independent sets of factors: hygiene factors (salary, working conditions, company policy and security), which prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate, and motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility and growth), which create satisfaction and genuine motivation. The theories are similar in that both arrange needs in a progression and both imply money alone cannot motivate. They differ in structure: Maslow uses one continuum while Herzberg argues that satisfaction and dissatisfaction are two separate continua, so the opposite of dissatisfaction is not satisfaction but no dissatisfaction. Maslow's lower levels broadly correspond to hygiene factors and his higher levels to motivators. Practically, managers must first fix hygiene issues and then offer motivators: Google India's 20 per cent free-time policy applies Herzberg by giving employees achievement and responsibility. In conclusion, Maslow explains what needs exist, while Herzberg adds that only higher-order factors truly motivate.

  • Maslow proposed five levels of needs arranged in a hierarchy: physiological, safety, love and belonging, esteem and self-actualisation.

Q3. Explain Tuckman's model of group development and its relevance for team building. 10 Marks

Answer:Tuckman's model describes how groups develop through five stages: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing and Adjourning. In Forming, members get acquainted, roles are unclear and behaviour is polite and dependent; the leader must direct and clarify goals. In Storming, conflicts and power struggles emerge as members assert themselves; the leader coaches and mediates. In Norming, cohesion, trust and consensus develop and norms are established; the leader facilitates and empowers. In Performing, the group matures and focuses on tasks with interdependence and synergy; the leader delegates and supports. In Adjourning, the task ends and the group disbands; the leader recognises and celebrates contributions. The model also clarifies the difference between groups, which pursue individual goals with individual accountability, and teams, which share goals, complementary skills and mutual accountability. Wipro applies the Tuckman model in its team development programmes, and the Indian cricket team illustrates a high-performing team built on complementary roles. In conclusion, Tuckman's stages help managers anticipate the natural lifecycle of a team, time their interventions correctly and move teams quickly from storming to performing.

Key points:

  • Answer: Tuckman's model describes how groups develop through five stages: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing and Adjourning.

  • In Forming, members get acquainted, roles are unclear and behaviour is polite and dependent; the leader must direct and clarify goals.

Q4. Discuss the five conflict management styles of the Thomas-Kilmann model. 10 Marks

Answer:The Thomas-Kilmann model classifies conflict-handling styles on two dimensions: assertiveness (concern for one's own interests) and cooperativeness (concern for others' interests). Competing (high assertiveness, low cooperativeness) pursues a win-lose outcome and suits urgent, decisive situations. Collaborating (high, high) seeks a win-win solution and is ideal when both parties' concerns matter, as Tata Group reportedly uses for inter-departmental conflicts. Compromising (medium, medium) splits the difference and suits parties of equal power under time pressure, as the Indian government applies in labour disputes between unions and management. Avoiding (low, low) withdraws from the issue and is appropriate when the matter is trivial or emotions run high. Accommodating (low, high) yields to the other party to preserve the relationship. Conflict is not always harmful: functional, task-related conflict improves decisions and creativity, while dysfunctional, personal conflict damages relationships and productivity. In conclusion, effective managers match the style to the situation, prefer collaboration for important issues, and treat well-managed conflict as a source of better decisions.

Key points:

  • Answer: The Thomas-Kilmann model classifies conflict-handling styles on two dimensions: assertiveness (concern for one's own interests) and cooperativeness (concern for others' interests).
  • Collaborating (high, high) seeks a win-win solution and is ideal when both parties' concerns matter, as Tata Group reportedly uses for inter-departmental conflicts.
  • Competing (high assertiveness, low cooperativeness) pursues a win-lose outcome and suits urgent, decisive situations.
