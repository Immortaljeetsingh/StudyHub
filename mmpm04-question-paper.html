Q1(a). Define International Human Resource Management and explain the factors that make it more complex than domestic HRM

Answer: IHRM procures, allocates and utilises human resources across national boundaries, and it is more complex than domestic HRM because of extra employee categories, cultural and legal differences, expatriation activities and higher risk.

International Human Resource Management (IHRM) is the process of procuring, allocating and effectively utilizing human resources across national boundaries to achieve the objectives of an international firm. It covers the same functions as domestic HRM - recruitment, selection, training, performance appraisal, compensation and employee relations - but performs them for employees spread over many countries and cultures.

IHRM is more complex than domestic HRM for several reasons. First, it deals with more employee categories: parent-country nationals (PCNs), host-country nationals (HCNs) and third-country nationals (TCNs), each needing different policies. Second, national differences in culture, law, language and economic conditions change how HR practices work; a reward system that motivates workers in the United States may fail in Japan or India. Third, there are additional activities such as managing expatriation, inpatriation and repatriation, international taxation, visas and work permits, and foreign currency effects on pay. Fourth, the firm's staffing orientation and strategy differ across countries, so decisions such as who manages a foreign subsidiary involve more stakeholders. Fifth, greater exposure to risk - political instability, exchange-rate swings and cultural misunderstandings - means IHRM mistakes are costlier. Finally, international firms must balance headquarters control against local responsiveness in every HR decision.

Key points to remember
  • IHRM = HR activities performed across national boundaries.
  • Complexity sources: more employee types, cultural/legal differences, expatriation, risk.
  • Balance global control with local responsiveness.

Takeaway: Every IHRM decision must balance headquarters control with local responsiveness.

Q1(b). Distinguish between PCNs, HCNs and TCNs with one example of each

Answer: PCNs come from the headquarters country, HCNs from the host country and TCNs from a third country - an Indian manager posted from Bengaluru (PCN), local US staff (HCN) and an expatriate specialist (TCN) show the difference.

These three categories describe where an employee comes from in relation to the firm's home and host countries:

  • Parent-Country National (PCN): a citizen of the country where the firm's headquarters is located, working in a foreign subsidiary. Example: an Indian manager from Infosys headquarters in Bengaluru posted to Infosys's office in the United States. PCNs are usually expatriates and carry the parent company's culture and control.
  • Host-Country National (HCN): a citizen of the country where the subsidiary operates. Example: a local American employee working at Infosys's US office. HCNs bring local market knowledge, lower cost and legal familiarity, so firms recruit them to build local acceptance.
  • Third-Country National (TCN): a citizen of a country other than both the parent and host countries. Example: a British executive working for an Indian multinational in its Dubai office. TCNs offer international experience and are often chosen for their global expertise.

The mix a firm uses reflects its staffing orientation: ethnocentric firms rely on PCNs, polycentric firms rely on HCNs, and geocentric firms hire the best person regardless of nationality (TCNs included).

Key points to remember
  • PCN = from headquarters country; HCN = local; TCN = from a third country.
  • The mix reflects the firm's EPRG staffing orientation.
  • Example anchors: Infosys (PCN), local US staff (HCN), expat specialist (TCN).

Takeaway: The PCN-HCN-TCN mix directly mirrors the firm's ethnocentric, polycentric or geocentric staffing orientation.

Q2(a). Explain Hofstede's cultural dimensions and their impact on international HRM practices

Answer: Hofstede's six dimensions - power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation and indulgence - are the collective programming of the mind that shapes every HR practice.

Hofstede defined culture as "the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one group from another" and measured national cultures on six dimensions:

  • Power Distance (PDI): how far a society accepts unequal distribution of power. High PDI (India, Mexico) means autocratic leadership; low PDI (Denmark) means participative styles.
  • Individualism (IDV): whether people look after themselves or belong to collectives. Individualist (USA, UK) favours individual incentives; collectivist (Japan, China) favours teamwork and seniority pay.
  • Masculinity (MAS): preference for competition and achievement over care and quality of life. High MAS values aggressive targets; low MAS values work-life balance.
  • Uncertainty Avoidance (UAI): comfort with ambiguity. High UAI (Germany, Japan) needs clear rules and contracts; low UAI (India, UK) tolerates flexible, informal practices.
  • Long-Term Orientation (LTO): future versus short-term focus. Long-term cultures invest in training and loyalty; short-term cultures focus on immediate results.
  • Indulgence (IVR): how freely a society allows enjoyment of life, which affects rewards and work-life policies.

Impact on IHRM: recruitment and selection must fit local values (high PDI cultures accept boss-driven hiring); appraisal systems must match expectations (individual feedback works in individualist cultures, group feedback in collectivist ones); compensation must suit motivation patterns; and training must prepare people for cultures different from their own. An HR policy that ignores these dimensions is likely to be resisted or fail in the host country.

Key points to remember
  • Six dimensions: PDI, IDV, MAS, UAI, LTO, IVR.
  • Culture = collective programming of the mind (Hofstede).
  • Every HR function must adapt to the host country's dimension scores.

Takeaway: An HR policy that ignores the host country's cultural scores will be resisted or fail.

Q2(b). How does culture influence cross-cultural business negotiations ?

Answer: Culture shapes negotiation through context (high versus low), power distance, time orientation, uncertainty avoidance and competitive versus collaborative style.

Negotiation is communication aimed at agreement, and culture shapes how parties communicate, decide and trust. In high-context cultures such as Japan and China, much meaning is conveyed indirectly through silence, relationships and context, so negotiators build personal trust first and treat contracts as frameworks; in low-context cultures such as Germany and the United States, communication is direct and explicit, and the written contract is final. Power distance decides who may speak: in high-PDI societies junior members defer to the senior leader, while in low-PDI societies experts of any rank can contribute. Time orientation also matters: monochronic cultures (USA, Germany) treat time as linear and value punctuality and quick closure, whereas polychronic cultures (India, Arab countries) treat time flexibly and expect relationship building before business. Uncertainty avoidance affects the appetite for detail: high-UAI negotiators want exhaustive clauses and guarantees, while low-UAI negotiators accept flexible arrangements. Finally, the negotiation style itself differs - competitive, win-lose styles versus collaborative, win-win styles - so an international HR manager and marketer must study the counterpart's culture in advance, use local etiquette, and allow more time for trust building when negotiating across cultures.

Key points to remember
  • High-context = indirect, relationship-first; low-context = direct, contract-first.
  • Power distance, time orientation and uncertainty avoidance shape tactics.
  • Prepare culturally before every international negotiation.

Takeaway: Preparing culturally before an international negotiation is as important as preparing commercially.

Q3(a). What is culture shock ? Describe its stages with the U-curve model and explain the selection criteria for international assignments

Answer: Culture shock is the disorientation of living in a new culture, and the U-curve model tracks four stages - honeymoon, crisis, adjustment and mastery - while assignment selection weighs adaptability and family support.

Culture shock is the anxiety and disorientation a person feels when living in a culture different from their own, because familiar cues of behaviour are replaced by unfamiliar ones. Oberg's U-curve model describes four stages. (1) Honeymoon: the expatriate is excited and curious about everything new. (2) Crisis: daily difficulties - language, food, bureaucracy, loneliness - accumulate and the person may feel frustrated, depressed or hostile toward the host culture; this is the deepest point of the U. (3) Adjustment: the person learns the local language and customs, makes friends, and starts functioning normally. (4) Mastery: the expatriate fully adapts, works effectively and may even prefer the host culture. Reverse culture shock occurs in the same pattern when the person returns home.

Selection criteria for international assignments include: technical and managerial competence; cross-cultural adaptability, openness and emotional stability; family situation, because spouse and children deeply affect success; language ability; motivation and interest in the assignment; health and stress tolerance; and the firm's staffing orientation. Firms that select on technical skill alone suffer high failure rates; IBM cut its expatriate failure rate from 25% to 5% after adding cultural adaptability and family support to selection and giving pre-departure cultural training.

Key points to remember
  • Stages: Honeymoon → Crisis → Adjustment → Mastery (U-curve).
  • Selection: technical skill + cultural adaptability + family support.
  • Expatriate failure is costly and mostly due to adjustment, not skill.

Takeaway: Selecting on technical skill alone is the classic cause of expatriate failure.

Q3(b). Why do expatriates fail, and what is the cost of expatriate failure ?

Answer: Expatriates fail mainly because of family and personal adjustment problems rather than job skill, and the direct cost of an early return is two to three times the expatriate's annual salary.

Expatriate failure means the expatriate returns home before the assignment is complete, or performs poorly during it. Research shows the main causes are adjustment-related rather than technical: the inability of the spouse and family to adjust to the host culture; the expatriate's own culture shock, stress and loneliness; poor selection based only on technical competence; inadequate pre-departure training; lack of organizational and headquarters support; and unclear career plans, because employees fear the assignment will hurt their careers. When the failure is in a developing or hardship location, early return is even more common.

The cost of expatriate failure is very high. Direct costs include the expatriate's salary, relocation, housing, schooling, tax and other allowances already paid, plus travel and settling-in costs, and estimates put the direct cost at two to three times the expatriate's annual salary. Indirect costs are often larger: the damage to customer and government relationships in the host country, loss of business opportunities, low morale of host-country staff who lose faith in headquarters, and the negative career impact on the employee. For a company with many international operations, repeated failures signal a weak IHRM system, so firms invest in proper selection, cross-cultural training and family support, which is far cheaper than failure.

Key points to remember
  • Main cause of failure: family and personal adjustment, not job skill.
  • Direct cost: 2-3 times the expatriate's annual salary.
  • Prevention: better selection, training, family and HQ support.

Takeaway: Prevention through better selection, cross-cultural training and family support is far cheaper than failure.

Q4(a). Discuss the three phases of training and development in the international context : pre-departure, on-site and repatriation

Answer: International training runs in three phases - pre-departure training, on-site orientation and repatriation support - covering language, culture and family.

International training prepares employees to work effectively across cultures and happens in three phases. (1) Pre-departure training is given in the home country before the assignment. It includes language training, cultural briefings on the host country's values, etiquette and business practices, factual information about living conditions, and family preparation, because spouse and children are trained together. Role plays, simulations and contact with returned expatriates make this training realistic; the goal is to reduce culture shock and raise the success rate. (2) On-site training continues in the host country through orientation programmes, mentoring by an experienced local or expatriate colleague, language practice and gradual delegation of responsibility. It helps the expatriate interpret real experiences, handles practical problems such as banking and schooling, and builds relationships with host-country staff. (3) Repatriation training and support prepares the employee and family for return home, covering reverse culture shock, updated information about changes at headquarters, career planning and debriefing, so the firm retains the knowledge and skills the expatriate gained abroad. Indian IT firms such as Infosys and TCS run structured global deployment and repatriation programmes to keep their offshore talent motivated, and this three-phase cycle - often remembered as P-O-R: pre-departure, on-site, repatriation - is the backbone of international training.

Key points to remember
  • Phases: Pre-departure → On-site → Repatriation (P-O-R).
  • Include the family in pre-departure preparation.
  • Repatriation support keeps skills and knowledge inside the firm.

Takeaway: A three-phase cycle that includes the family turns training into retention of global skills.

Q4(b). What is repatriation ? Why is re-entry often difficult for returning expatriates ?

Answer: Repatriation is the planned return and reintegration of an expatriate into the parent company, and re-entry is hard because of reverse culture shock, changed workplaces and unclear career roles.

Repatriation is the process of bringing an expatriate back to the home country and reintegrating them into the parent company at the end of the international assignment. It is the reverse of expatriation but equally needs planning, covering the return move, career planning, and support against reverse culture shock - the surprise and discomfort of finding that home no longer feels familiar and that the workplace has changed.

Re-entry is difficult for several reasons. The expatriate may have spent years abroad and built habits, friendships and status in the host country, so returning feels like losing independence. The home environment may have changed - new colleagues, new technology, new strategies - while the returnee's expectations of being rewarded for international experience are often disappointed. In many firms returning expatriates face role ambiguity: no clear job, reduced authority, or a position that ignores their new skills, leading to frustration and high turnover shortly after return. Personal and family adjustment, such as children changing schools and the spouse restarting a career, adds to the strain. Companies reduce these problems through career planning before return, debriefing and mentoring, recognition of acquired competencies, and communication with the family, turning repatriation from a liability into a competitive advantage.

Key points to remember
  • Repatriation = return and reintegration at assignment end.
  • Reverse culture shock + unclear career = post-return turnover.
  • Plan the return job and career before the expatriate lands home.

Takeaway: Plan the return job and career before the expatriate lands home or risk post-return turnover.

Q5(a). Compare the Balance Sheet and Going Rate approaches to international compensation with their advantages and disadvantages

Answer: The Balance Sheet approach builds compensation from home-country base plus allowances to preserve the home standard of living, while the Going Rate approach pays host-country market rates.

The Balance Sheet (build-up) approach keeps the expatriate's standard of living equal to the home country by building the package from home-country pay plus allowances. It calculates: base salary at home-country level; goods-and-services allowance for the difference in costs; housing, education and tax allowances to cover the gap between home and host costs; and a premium for hardship or relocation. Advantages: equity with home peers, easy to calculate and administer, and it protects the expatriate from host-country inflation; this is why Shell uses it to keep expatriates at a UK-standard living worldwide. Disadvantages: it is complex and expensive, can create large pay differences between expatriates and host-country nationals doing the same job, and it ties compensation to the home country even when host-country pay is higher.

The Going Rate (localization) approach pays the expatriate according to the host-country market rates for comparable jobs. Advantages: it is simple, keeps local competitiveness, avoids inequity with host-country colleagues, and often costs less. Disadvantages: inequity between expatriates of different nationalities in the same location, difficulty attracting expatriates when the host rate is low, and problems with repatriation, because the employee's pay drops on return. Many firms today blend the two: local rates for routine assignments and the balance sheet for key strategic posts, and both approaches must satisfy the principles of internal equity, external competitiveness and expatriate motivation.

Key points to remember
  • Balance Sheet: maintain home standard - equitable but complex and costly.
  • Going Rate: host-country rates - simple but creates expatriate inequity.
  • Blended packages are common in practice.

Takeaway: Balance Sheet is equitable but complex and costly; Going Rate is simple but creates inequity, so many firms blend both.

Q5(b). List the main components of an expatriate compensation package

Answer: An expatriate compensation package combines base salary with cost-of-living, housing, education and tax-equalization allowances, plus relocation, hardship premium and benefits.

An expatriate package is designed to keep the employee whole and motivated abroad. Its main components are:

  • Base salary: the home-country or host-country salary, the foundation of the package.
  • Cost-of-living allowance: compensates for the difference in prices between home and host countries, so purchasing power is maintained.
  • Housing allowance: covers the cost of suitable accommodation, which is often higher abroad than at home.
  • Education allowance: pays for international or local schooling for the expatriate's children.
  • Tax equalization: protects the employee from paying more tax than at home; the firm pays any excess or uses tax protection.
  • Relocation and settling-in allowance: one-time payments for moving, shipping, temporary accommodation and setting up the new home.
  • Hardship and mobility premium: an additional percentage of salary for difficult locations or as an incentive to accept the assignment.
  • Benefits and perquisites: continued medical, retirement and insurance cover, home leave travel, company car and club memberships.

Companies must also decide whether to pay in home, host or a mix of currencies, and how the package will change when the expatriate repatriates, since benefits such as housing and schooling normally end on return.

Key points to remember
  • Core: base salary, cost-of-living, housing, education, tax equalization.
  • Plus: relocation, hardship premium, benefits, home leave.
  • The package must balance home equity, local competitiveness and motivation.

Takeaway: The package keeps the expatriate whole, motivated and able to focus on the assignment.

Q6(a). Explain Perlmutter's EPRG model and show how each orientation affects the staffing of international assignments

Answer: Perlmutter's EPRG model - ethnocentric, polycentric, regiocentric and geocentric - determines whether assignments are staffed with PCNs, HCNs, regional TCNs or the best person anywhere.

Perlmutter's EPRG model describes four orientations a multinational firm can adopt, and each changes how international staffing is done:

  • Ethnocentric orientation - headquarters' ways are considered superior and control is centralized. Staffing relies heavily on parent-country nationals (PCNs) for key positions abroad; expatriates transfer the home culture and keep control, but it is costly, blocks local talent and may fail to adapt to local markets. Many Indian firms start this way, posting home managers to new overseas offices.
  • Polycentric orientation - each subsidiary is managed by host-country nationals, because locals understand their market best. Staffing hires HCNs for subsidiary management, with only a few headquarters people; it is cheaper, builds local acceptance and reduces adjustment problems, but coordination across subsidiaries is weak and PCN careers lose international exposure.
  • Regiocentric orientation - a middle position where managers move within a region, such as Asia or Europe, and regional headquarters coordinates staffing. It develops regional managers (often TCNs) and balances local responsiveness with regional consistency.
  • Geocentric orientation - a global approach that hires and promotes the best person for each job regardless of nationality. Staffing uses PCNs, HCNs and TCNs wherever they add most value, developing a truly global leadership pool, as leading MNCs do; it is the hardest to implement because of cost, legal constraints and the need for a global mindset.

Firms typically evolve from ethnocentric to polycentric and eventually geocentric as they mature internationally, and the EPRG choice is remembered as the four orientations: ethnocentric, polycentric, regiocentric, geocentric.

Key points to remember
  • EPRG = Ethnocentric, Polycentric, Regiocentric, Geocentric.
  • Ethnocentric → PCNs; polycentric → HCNs; geocentric → best person anywhere.
  • Orientation evolves as the firm matures internationally.

Takeaway: Orientations evolve from home-country control to hiring the best person anywhere as the firm matures.

Q6(b). What is a High Performance Work System (HPWS) ? Briefly state its main features

Answer: HPWS is an integrated set of HR practices - selective hiring, training, performance pay, participation, information sharing and job security - that raises performance through ability, motivation and opportunity.

A High Performance Work System (HPWS) is an integrated set of human resource practices designed to enhance employees' abilities, motivation and opportunities, so that the organization as a whole performs at a higher level than the sum of its parts. It is built on the idea that people, not machines, create sustainable competitive advantage.

Its main features include: selective recruitment to hire the right talent; extensive training and development to build knowledge and skills; performance-based pay, gain sharing and incentives linked to results; employee participation, empowerment and self-managed teams; information sharing so employees understand strategy and performance; job security and internal career ladders; flexible job design and multi-skilling; and strong communication and a supportive culture. In the international context, HPWS must be adapted to local culture - for example, individual performance pay may be toned down in collectivist societies - and it is linked to better productivity, quality and lower turnover; studies in India show that manufacturing and IT firms adopting HPWS practices report higher employee commitment and plant-level performance.

Key points to remember
  • HPWS = ability + motivation + opportunity for high performance.
  • Features: selective hiring, training, performance pay, participation, job security.
  • Adapt HPWS to the local culture in international settings.

Takeaway: HPWS works by building ability, motivation and opportunity together, adapted to the local culture.
