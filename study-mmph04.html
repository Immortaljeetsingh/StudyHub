Unit 1: Concept and Approaches to Industrial Relations

📖 Key Definitions

Industrial Relations (IR): The relationship between employers and employees (and their organisations) and the government, arising out of employment. ILO Definition: "IR covers the relationship between the state on the one hand and the employer's and workers' organisations on the other, and the relationship between the occupational organisations themselves." Scope: Includes collective bargaining, dispute resolution, grievance handling, discipline, worker participation, and labour legislation.

📖 Objectives of IR

Primary Objectives: (1) Protect interests of workers and employers; (2) Avoid industrial disputes; (3) Increase productivity; (4) Establish industrial democracy; (5) Social justice. Secondary Objectives: (1) Collective bargaining; (2) Workers' participation in management; (3) Fair wages and working conditions; (4) Grievance redressal; (5) Discipline maintenance.

📖 Core Definition

Industrial Relations = relationship between employers and employees in the course of employment. ILO: relationships between the state, employers' and employees' organisations. IR encompasses cooperation, negotiation, and mutual adjustment. Scope: Includes collective bargaining, dispute resolution, grievance handling, discipline, worker participation, and labour legislation. Actors: Workers (through unions), Employers (through associations), Government (through legislation and administration).

📖 Significance of IR

For Workers: Fair wages, job security, safe working conditions, collective voice, grievance redressal. For Employers: Industrial peace, productivity, employee retention, reputation, legal compliance. For Government: Economic growth, social stability, tax revenue, reduced litigation. For Society: Reduced inequality, social justice, democratic values, economic development.

📖 Key Concept — Dunlop's Systems Theory

Dunlop's Systems Theory (1958): IR system consists of 3 actors (workers, employers, government) operating within 3 contexts (technology, market, power distribution), bound by shared ideology, producing 2 types of rules (substantive — wages, hours; procedural — grievance, discipline). Contexts: Technology determines nature of work; market forces shape wages; power distribution affects bargaining balance. Ideology: Shared beliefs that bind the system — can be cooperation or conflict-oriented. Rules output: Substantive (wages, hours, conditions) and procedural (how disputes are resolved).

📖 Pluralistic Approach — Deep Dive

Pluralistic View: Organisation consists of multiple groups (management, workers, shareholders, customers) with inherently different interests. Conflict is natural and inevitable — not pathological. The goal is not to eliminate conflict but to manage it through institutional mechanisms like collective bargaining, grievance procedures, and arbitration. Key implication: Strong unions are essential for balancing power — workers need collective voice to counter management dominance.

📖 Marxist Approach — Deep Dive

Marxist View: Industrial relations reflect the fundamental class struggle between capital (employers) and labour (workers) in capitalist society. Workers sell their labour power; employers extract surplus value. Conflict is structural and inherent in capitalism — cannot be resolved through negotiation alone. Key implication: Unions should be revolutionary tools for class consciousness, not just bargaining agents. True worker emancipation requires overthrowing the capitalist system.

Unit 2: Evolution of Industrial Relations in India

📖 Key Definitions

Evolution of IR: The historical development of employer-employee-government relationships in India — from colonial exploitation to modern codes-based system. 4 Phases: Colonial (1875-1947), Post-Independence (1947-1991), Liberalisation (1991-2014), New Codes (2020+). Key Trend: Shift from state-led, union-dominated IR to market-driven, employer-friendly system.

📖 Colonial Era IR (1875-1947)

Context: British industrialisation in India — textile mills in Mumbai, jute mills in Kolkata, railways. Worker conditions: Long hours (12-16), low wages, no safety, child labour. Early unions: 1918 Madras Labour Union (B.P. Wadia) — first formal union. AITUC (1920): First national federation — Lala Lajpat Rai. TU Act (1926): Legal framework for union registration — minimum 7 members. Whitley Commission (1929): Royal Commission on Labour — recommended conciliation machinery.

📖 Code of Discipline (1958)

Code of Discipline: Voluntary code adopted in 1958 by Indian Labour Conference. Employers and unions agreed to maintain industrial peace through self-regulation. Key commitments: Employers won't resort to lockouts; unions won't resort to strikes; both will follow grievance procedures. Significance: First attempt at voluntary self-regulation in Indian IR — established the principle of bipartite cooperation.

📖 New Economic Policy (1991)

NEP 1991: Economic liberalisation under PM Narasimha Rao and FM Manmohan Singh. Disinvestment of PSUs, FDI promotion, deregulation. Impact on IR: Decline of public sector employment, rise of private sector, outsourcing, contract labour, flexible employment, weakening of trade unions, shift from collective bargaining to individual employment contracts.

Unit 3: Constitution, ILO and Legal Framework

📖 Key Definitions

Legal Framework of IR: The constitutional provisions, ILO conventions, and domestic legislation that govern employer-employee relationships. Constitution: Fundamental Rights (enforceable) + DPSP (non-justiciable but guide legislation). ILO: International Labour Organization — tripartite (Govt + Employers + Workers), sets international labour standards. Domestic Laws: TU Act 1926, ID Act 1947, Factories Act 1948, MW Act 1948, IR Code 2020.

📖 Fundamental Rights vs DPSP

Fundamental Rights (Part III): Articles 12-35 — enforceable in courts, justiciable. Labour-relevant FR: Article 14 (equality), 19(1)(c) (form associations), 23-24 (against exploitation). DPSP (Part IV): Articles 36-51 — non-justiciable, guide legislation. Labour-relevant DPSP: Article 38 (social order), 39 (equal pay), 41 (right to work), 42 (humane conditions), 43 (living wage). Key distinction: FR are negative (state can't violate); DPSP are positive (state should promote).

📖 ILO — Key Facts

ILO (International Labour Organization): Established 1919, Geneva. UN agency for labour standards. Tripartite structure: Governments + Employers + Workers (unique among UN agencies). India: Founding member, ratified 8 core conventions. Functions: Set international labour standards, develop policies, promote decent work. Key outputs: Conventions (binding), Recommendations (non-binding), Declarations.

📖 TU Act 1926 — Key Provisions

Trade Unions Act 1926: Provides for registration of trade unions. Minimum 7 members required for registration. Rights: Legal entity status, can own property, sue and be sued, immunity from civil/criminal liability for trade disputes. Obligations: Annual returns, audit of accounts, amendment of rules. Penalties: Fine for false representation, failure to submit returns.

Unit 4: Labour Administration in India

📖 Key Definitions

Labour Administration: The institutional framework for implementing labour policy — includes central government, state government, and tripartite bodies. Central Level: Ministry of Labour (policy), CLC (enforcement), CIRM (dispute resolution). State Level: Labour Department (state laws), Labour Courts (adjudication). Tripartite: ILC (advisory), SLC (interim). New Codes 2020: 4 codes (WISO) consolidate 29 central labour laws.

📖 Chief Labour Commissioner (CLC)

CLC: Central government agency responsible for enforcement of labour laws. Functions: Conciliation in industrial disputes, inspection of establishments, enforcement of minimum wages, verification of union membership. Structure: Central CLC (Delhi) + Regional CLCs (Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata, Kanpur). Tools: Inspection, investigation, prosecution, conciliation conferences.

📖 Indian Labour Conference (ILC)

ILC: Apex tripartite body for labour policy. Members: Central government + State governments + Employers' associations + Workers' unions. Functions: Advises on labour legislation, reviews labour policy, recommends minimum wages, discusses ILO conventions. Frequency: Annual meeting, chaired by Union Labour Minister.

📖 Definition

Collective Bargaining: Process of negotiation between employers (or employers' organisations) and workers' representatives (trade unions) for determining terms and conditions of employment — ILO. The term was coined by Sidney & Beatrice Webb (1897) in their study of British trade unions.

📖 Key Characteristics

CB is: (1) Collective — group action, not individual; (2) Bipartite — two parties negotiate directly; (3) Continuous — ongoing relationship, not one-time; (4) Dynamic — adapts to changing conditions; (5) Rule-making — produces substantive + procedural rules.

5 Functions of Collective Bargaining

📋 Market & Government
  • Bipartism: Two-party negotiation (no government interference)
  • Flexibility: Adapts to local conditions better than legislation
📋 Peace & Democracy
  • Peace mechanism: Prevents strikes through negotiation
  • Industrial democracy: Workers participate in decisions
  • Equalisation: Balances power between capital and labour
💡 Real-World Example

2019 Tata Steel-Workers' Union settlement was integrative — productivity-linked pay (company) in exchange for job security (workers). 📖 Sidney & Beatrice Webb (1897)

Webbs: British socialists who coined "Collective Bargaining" in their study "Industrial Democracy" (1897). Key insight: Workers as individuals have unequal bargaining power against employers — only through collective action (unions) can they negotiate on equal terms. Legacy: Founded London School of Economics (LSE), shaped British labour movement, influenced ILO formation.

📖 Definition

BATNA: Your best course of action if negotiations fail. It sets your "walk-away" point — you should never accept a deal worse than your BATNA. The stronger your BATNA, the more power you have at the table.

💡 Real-World Example

2012 Maruti Manesar — failure to separate people from problem (personal animosity) escalated a wage dispute into violence. In contrast, Tata Steel 2019 negotiations used principled negotiation: separated the automation fear (people) from productivity targets (problem), achieving integrative outcomes. BATNA example: If Maruti workers had strong BATNA (alternative jobs in auto sector), they wouldn't have accepted management's terms. Management's BATNA: automation could replace some workers — gave them leverage.

📖 Fisher & Ury — "Getting to Yes" (1981)

Fisher & Ury: Harvard Negotiation Project — developed principled negotiation framework. Key book: "Getting to Yes" (1981) — bestseller on negotiation. 4 Principles: (1) Separate people from problem — don't attack persons; (2) Focus on interests, not positions — understand WHY; (3) Generate options for mutual gain — be creative; (4) Use objective criteria — standards, precedents, market rates. Impact: Changed negotiation from adversarial to collaborative — widely used in business, diplomacy, labour relations.

📖 Definition

📖 Key Definitions

Discipline: Orderly behaviour by employees in accordance with organisational rules and standards. Types: Positive (self-discipline, intrinsic) vs Negative (punitive, extrinsic). 📖 Definition

Discipline: Orderly behaviour by employees in accordance with organisational rules, standards, and codes of conduct. Discipline ensures smooth operations, safety, and productivity. It can be positive (self-discipline, intrinsic motivation) or negative (punitive, imposed through sanctions).

📖 Positive vs Negative Discipline

Positive Discipline: Self-imposed, voluntary compliance — employees follow rules because they understand and accept them. Builds morale, trust, and long-term commitment. Negative Discipline: Punitive approach — employees follow rules out of fear of punishment. Creates resentment, low morale, and adversarial relations.

💡 Real-World Example

State of UP v. Shatrughan Lal (1998) — Supreme Court held that termination without domestic inquiry violates natural justice. Every employee must get a chance to defend themselves — this is the audi alteram partem principle in action. Domestic Inquiry: Must follow natural justice — charge sheet, suspension, inquiry officer, evidence, defence, verdict. Proportionality: Punishment must be proportionate to misconduct — dismissal for minor tardiness is excessive.

📖 Suspension — Types

Subsistence suspension: Employee receives subsistence allowance (usually 50% of salary) during inquiry period. Punitive suspension: Imposed as punishment after inquiry — no salary. Precautionary suspension: To prevent interference with inquiry — employee can't influence witnesses or destroy evidence. ID Act: Section 33 requires employer to pay subsistence allowance during suspension pending inquiry.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

These questions address commonly confused concepts and frequently asked exam questions. Use them for quick reference and last-minute revision.

❓ Q1: What is the difference between Industrial Relations and Employment Relations?

Answer: Industrial Relations (IR) traditionally focuses on employer-employee-government relationships in organised industry — emphasising unions, collective bargaining, and disputes. Employment Relations (ER) is broader — includes all employment relationships (organised + unorganised, permanent + temporary, gig workers). Modern usage prefers ER as it covers non-union firms, IT sector, gig economy. But in Indian academic context, IR is still the standard term — IGNOU uses "Industrial Relations" for this course.

❓ Q2: Why are trade unions declining in India?

Answer: Multiple reasons: (1) Globalisation — outsourcing, contract labour, flexible employment; (2) IT sector — non-union culture, high salaries, ESOPs; (3) Politicisation — workers lose faith when unions serve parties; (4) Multiplicity — too many unions weaken collective voice; (5) Legal changes — IR Code 2020 makes it harder for unions to strike; (6) Gig economy — new forms of work don't fit traditional union model. Counter-trend: NITES (2021) shows IT workers organising — unions adapting to new economy.

❓ Q3: Is the Code of Discipline still relevant?

Answer: Code of Discipline (1958) was India's first attempt at voluntary self-regulation. Relevance declining: Voluntary compliance weakening, new codes have statutory provisions, employers prefer flexibility. Still relevant: Principle of bipartite cooperation, foundation for future legislation, moral force in PSUs. Exam tip: Compare Code of Discipline (voluntary) with IR Code 2020 (statutory) — shows evolution of IR regulation.

❓ Q4: How does the new IR Code 2020 affect collective bargaining?

Answer: Multiple impacts: (1) 300-worker threshold — companies can lay off without government permission (up from 100); (2) Fixed-term contracts — formalises temporary employment; (3) Union recognition — 51%+ or secret ballot for sole agent; (4) Strike notice — 14 days for all establishments (not just public utilities); (5) Gig inclusion — platform workers get social security. Impact: More employer flexibility, less worker protection, but gig workers get benefits for first time.

❓ Q5: What is the role of ILO in Indian IR?

Answer: ILO (est. 1919, Geneva) — UN agency for labour standards. India's role: Founding member, ratified all 8 core conventions. Functions: Set international labour standards (Conventions + Recommendations), develop policies, promote decent work. Impact on India: Shaped labour legislation (TU Act, ID Act), influenced tripartite model (ILC, SLC), guided new labour codes. Decent Work Agenda: Employment + Social Dialogue + Rights + Social Protection. Limitation: Conventions are recommendations, not binding — India implements through domestic legislation.
