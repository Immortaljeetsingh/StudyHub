Q1. Explain ABC analysis and EOQ in inventory management.

Answer: Inventory management balances the cost of holding stock against the risk of stock-outs. ABC analysis applies the Pareto principle to classify inventory by annual value. A items are about 20% of items but 80% of value, and need tight, frequent review; B items are roughly 30% of items and 15% of value, needing moderate control; C items are about 50% of items but only 5% of value and receive simple, infrequent control. The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) minimizes total inventory cost, the sum of ordering cost and holding cost: EOQ = √(2DS/H), where D is annual demand, S the ordering cost per order and H the holding cost per unit per year. For example, D = 12,000 units, S = Rs 500 and H = Rs 10 gives EOQ = √(2 x 12,000 x 500/10) = 1,095 units, about 11 orders per year and a cycle of 33 days. The reorder point ROP = d x L + safety stock triggers replenishment, where safety stock = Z x σ x √L buffers demand variability. Maruti Suzuki pioneered JIT in India, keeping only 2-4 hours of component inventory, while Flipkart uses ABC slotting in its fulfilment centres during events like the Big Billion Days.

Key points:

  • Inventory management balances the cost of holding stock against the risk of stock-outs.
  • For example, D = 12,000 units, S = Rs 500 and H = Rs 10 gives EOQ = √(2 x 12,000 x 500/10) = 1,095 units, about 11 orders per year and a cycle of 33 days.
  • ABC analysis applies the Pareto principle to classify inventory by annual value.

Takeaway: Maruti Suzuki pioneered JIT in India, keeping only 2-4 hours of component inventory, while Flipkart uses ABC slotting in its fulfilment centres during events like the Big Billion Days.

Q2. Differentiate between chase, level and mixed aggregate planning strategies.

Answer: Aggregate planning is a medium-term production plan, typically 3-18 months, that sets overall output, workforce and inventory levels to match forecast demand at minimum cost. The chase strategy adjusts capacity to match demand by hiring and firing workers as demand rises and falls. Inventory stays low but hiring and firing costs can be high and morale may suffer; it suits seasonal demand, as HUL does with ice cream in summer. The level strategy keeps production constant and uses inventory or backlog to absorb demand fluctuations. The workforce stays stable, avoiding hiring costs, but holding inventory costs money; Indian Railways coach factories follow this approach. The mixed strategy combines both - a stable base workforce supported by overtime, subcontracting and temporary inventory swings. Cadbury India runs its Pune plant at a level rate for Dairy Milk year-round and adds overtime and outsourced packaging during the October-December festival surge. The choice depends on relative costs: high hiring and firing costs favour level, high inventory costs favour chase, and a stable base with seasonal peaks favours mixed.

Key points:

  • Aggregate planning is a medium-term production plan, typically 3-18 months, that sets overall output, workforce and inventory levels to match forecast demand at minimum cost.
  • The workforce stays stable, avoiding hiring costs, but holding inventory costs money; Indian Railways coach factories follow this approach.
  • The chase strategy adjusts capacity to match demand by hiring and firing workers as demand rises and falls.

Q3. What is DMAIC in Six Sigma? Explain its five phases.

Answer: Six Sigma is a quality methodology targeting 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO), a 99.99966% yield. DMAIC is its structured problem-solving framework for improving existing processes: Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve and Control. Define identifies the problem, project scope and critical-to-quality characteristics, using tools such as SIPOC and the voice of the customer. Measure collects baseline data, validates the measurement system with Gage R&R and establishes the current sigma level. Analyze finds root causes using fishbone diagrams, 5-Why, Pareto charts and regression, verifying each cause with data. Improve generates and pilots solutions, using design of experiments (DOE), brainstorming and poka-yoke before full implementation. Control sustains the gains by monitoring with control charts, documenting standard operating procedures and handing over to the process owner. For new products or processes, DMADV (Define-Measure-Analyze-Design-Verify) is used instead. Tata Steel, a Deming Prize winner, and Wipro, which cut post-delivery defects by 40%, are well-known Indian adopters. The belt hierarchy runs from Master Black Belt down to Green, Yellow and White Belts.

Key points:

  • Six Sigma is a quality methodology targeting 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO), a 99.99966% yield.
  • Define identifies the problem, project scope and critical-to-quality characteristics, using tools such as SIPOC and the voice of the customer.
  • DMAIC is its structured problem-solving framework for improving existing processes: Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve and Control.

Takeaway: The belt hierarchy runs from Master Black Belt down to Green, Yellow and White Belts.

Q4. Explain OEE and its three components in maintenance management.

Answer: Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) measures how well a manufacturing operation is utilized, and world-class OEE is 85% or more. It is the product of three factors: OEE = Availability x Performance x Quality. Availability is the percentage of planned production time the equipment actually runs - uptime divided by planned production time, or equivalently MTBF/(MTBF + MTTR), where MTBF is mean time between failures and MTTR is mean time to repair. Performance compares actual output with the maximum possible at rated speed. Quality is the proportion of good units produced. The 6 big losses that reduce OEE are equipment failure, setup and adjustment, idling and minor stops, reduced speed, process defects and startup losses. Maintenance itself has four types: breakdown or reactive maintenance, preventive maintenance on a schedule, predictive maintenance based on condition monitoring, and Total Productive Maintenance (TPM), where operators maintain their own equipment across eight pillars. Example: a machine with 89.6% availability, 93% performance and 96.3% quality has OEE of about 80.1%, below the world-class benchmark. Maruti’s Gurugram plant reports OEE above 92%.

Key points:

  • Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) measures how well a manufacturing operation is utilized, and world-class OEE is 85% or more.
  • Example: a machine with 89.6% availability, 93% performance and 96.3% quality has OEE of about 80.1%, below the world-class benchmark.
  • It is the product of three factors: OEE = Availability x Performance x Quality.
