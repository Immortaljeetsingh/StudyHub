The main distinction between these two types of ticket booking is in the ticket prices. Tatkal ticket charges have been set as a percentage of fare at 10% of the basic fare for second class and 30% of the basic fare for all other classes, subject to the minimum and maximum prices. Tickets for the Premium Tatkal service, which is only available on certain trains, have dynamic fare pricing for classes. The Premium Tatkal fare refers to the fare component that increases with subsequent bookings. Q1(a). How do services differ from products ?

Answer: Services differ from physical products on four fundamental IHIP dimensions - intangibility, inseparability, variability and perishability - which is why they need a different marketing approach.

Services are activities, benefits or satisfactions offered for sale that are essentially intangible; they differ from physical products in four fundamental ways:

  • Intangibility: a service cannot be seen, touched, tasted or stored before purchase. A passenger buying an airline ticket gets a promise of travel, not a physical object; the product's quality can only be judged after use, which raises perceived risk.
  • Inseparability: services are produced and consumed at the same time, and the producer is part of the service. A haircut cannot be separated from the barber; the customer's presence and participation in the process affect the outcome.
  • Variability (heterogeneity): service quality depends on who provides it, when and where, so no two haircuts, lectures or restaurant visits are identical. Human performance makes standardization difficult.
  • Perishability: services cannot be stored for later sale. An empty hotel room or an unsold airline seat loses its revenue forever when the night or the flight passes; there is no inventory to buffer demand and supply.

Because of these differences, services also involve no transfer of ownership, are harder to evaluate before purchase, and depend on people and processes more than goods do, which is why marketing them needs a different approach.

Key points to remember
  • Four differences: Intangibility, Inseparability, Variability, Perishability.
  • No ownership transfer; quality judged during or after consumption.
  • Example anchors: flight (intangible), haircut (inseparable), restaurant (variable), hotel room (perishable).

Takeaway: Memorize IHIP with its example anchors - flight (intangible), haircut (inseparable), restaurant (variable), hotel room (perishable) - they answer the first question of any services exam.

Q1(b). What are the marketing implications of service characteristics ?

Answer: Each service characteristic creates a matching marketing challenge - intangibility demands trust-building, inseparability demands people management, variability demands standardization, and perishability demands demand-and-capacity management.

Each characteristic of services creates a specific challenge for the marketer, and a matching strategy:

  • Intangibility → tangibilize and reduce risk: since customers cannot inspect the service, marketers use physical evidence, concrete figures, testimonials and guarantees. Example: hotels publish photos, spas display certificates, and Oyo advertises "sanitised stays" with visible ratings; banks highlight their branch network as proof of reliability.
  • Inseparability → manage people and interaction: because the provider is part of the product, employee training, grooming and behaviour become part of the brand. Example: Taj Hotels' staff courtesy is itself the experience; airlines train cabin crew as the face of the service.
  • Variability → standardize and control quality: marketers use standard operating procedures, training, technology and even substitutes for people. Example: McDonald's and Domino's use manuals and quality checks so every outlet delivers the same burger, and ATMs standardize banking transactions.
  • Perishability → manage demand and capacity: since unsold service capacity is lost forever, marketers use reservation systems, off-peak pricing, promotions and part-time staff. Example: IRCTC's Tatkal quota, hotel weekend deals and cinema matinee pricing all sell otherwise-empty capacity.

In short, the four characteristics force service marketers to work harder on trust-building, people management, quality control and demand management than goods marketers do.

Key points to remember
  • Intangibility → tangibilize; Inseparability → train people.
  • Variability → standardize; Perishability → manage demand and capacity.
  • Every implication must be matched with a strategy.

Takeaway: Pair every characteristic with its strategy - intangibility to tangibilize, inseparability to train people, variability to standardize, perishability to manage demand and capacity.

Q2(a). Differentiate between high-contact, medium-contact and low-contact services

Answer: Services are classified by the extent and duration of customer contact - high-contact (customer present throughout), medium-contact (partial interaction) and low-contact (delivered through technology or intermediaries) - and the level of contact shapes location, technology, skill and pricing decisions.

Services can be classified by the extent and duration of contact between the customer and the service provider during delivery:

  • High-contact services: the customer is present throughout most of the service delivery and interacts personally with the provider. Examples: hospitals and doctors, beauty salons, hotels, restaurants and personal tuition. The customer is physically involved, waits in the system, and the quality of the employee interaction directly determines satisfaction; the "moment of truth" matters most here.
  • Medium-contact services: the customer interacts with the service system for only part of the delivery, with some steps done away from the customer. Examples: banks and post offices, where a customer queues at the counter for deposit or withdrawal but the back-office processing happens later, and most retail stores, where contact is at purchase but stocking and accounting are backstage.
  • Low-contact services: the customer is not physically present during most of the delivery; the service reaches the customer through technology or intermediaries. Examples: courier and parcel services, online banking and mobile recharges, e-commerce delivery, TV and internet connections, and repair services where the technician works on the product without the owner present.

As contact falls, the service becomes more like a standardized, tangible product - easier to control, cheaper to deliver, but harder to personalize - which is why the level of contact guides decisions on location, technology, employee skill and pricing.

Key points to remember
  • High contact: customer present throughout (hospital, salon, hotel).
  • Medium contact: partial interaction (bank counter).
  • Low contact: delivered via technology or intermediaries (courier, online banking).

Takeaway: As contact falls, services become more standardized and cheaper to deliver but harder to personalize - quote an example for each level to score full marks.

Q2(b). How are people's roles different in these services ? Explain with examples

Answer: People's roles shift with the level of contact: in high-contact services employees are the product itself, in medium-contact services staff split into front-line and back-office roles, and in low-contact services people design and maintain the systems that replace personal delivery.

The role of people changes with the level of contact. In high-contact services, employees are part of the product itself - their courtesy, skill and behaviour create the experience. Example: in a hotel, the front-desk staff, bell boys and waiters directly deliver the service; a rude receptionist ruins the stay even if the room is perfect. Training, grooming and empowerment of such frontline staff become the core of marketing, which is why Indian hotels like Taj and ITC invest heavily in staff development.

In medium-contact services, people have a supportive role: counter staff handle the interface, while a large back-office team - clerks, supervisors, settlement officers - processes the work unseen. Example: in a bank, the teller greets and transacts with the customer, but loan sanctions, cheque clearing and reconciliation are done by back-office employees, so both front-line courtesy and back-office accuracy matter, and errors surface later as complaints.

In low-contact services, the role of people shifts from delivery to design, maintenance and exception handling. Example: in courier services, the customer rarely meets anyone; pick-up and delivery personnel are the only contact points, while tracking, routing and logistics staff work behind the scenes, and technology such as SMS tracking and chatbots replaces most human interaction. Customers judge such services by speed and reliability rather than personal warmth, so firms substitute people with systems and reserve staff for problem cases.

Key points to remember
  • High contact: employees are the product; train and empower them.
  • Medium contact: frontline interface + back-office support.
  • Low contact: people design systems; technology replaces contact.

Takeaway: Match people to contact level: high contact = employees are the brand, medium contact = front-line plus back-office, low contact = people build the systems that replace interaction.

Q3. Describe the gaps model and explain the significance of the five gaps that the model identifies

Answer: The gaps model of service quality (Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry) explains dissatisfaction through five gaps - knowledge, standards, delivery, communication and the customer gap - and closing gaps 1-4 automatically closes gap 5.

The gaps model of service quality (Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry) explains why customers may be dissatisfied even when the company believes it is doing well: satisfaction depends on the gap between what customers expect and what they perceive they received. The model identifies five gaps:

  • Gap 1 - Knowledge gap: the difference between customer expectations and management's understanding of those expectations. It is closed by marketing research, complaint analysis and direct customer contact.
  • Gap 2 - Standards (design) gap: the difference between what management understands and the service standards it sets. It is closed by setting measurable, customer-focused standards.
  • Gap 3 - Delivery gap: the difference between the service standards specified and what employees actually deliver. Poor training, overload or weak supervision cause this gap. It is closed by training, motivation, teamwork and technology support.
  • Gap 4 - Communication gap: the difference between what the firm promises in advertising and what it actually delivers. It is closed by making realistic promises and coordinating marketing with operations.
  • Gap 5 - Customer gap: the difference between the service customers expect and what they perceive they received; it is the overall measure of service quality and equals the combined effect of gaps 1 to 4.

Significance: the model shows that quality failures can start at any stage - from wrong research to poor delivery to exaggerated ads - and that closing gaps 1-4 automatically closes gap 5. It also gave rise to the SERVQUAL instrument, which measures service quality on five dimensions: reliability, assurance, tangibles, empathy and responsiveness (RATER).

Key points to remember
  • Five gaps: knowledge, standards, delivery, communication, customer.
  • Gap 5 = expectations minus perceptions; closes when gaps 1-4 close.
  • SERVQUAL measures on RATER dimensions.

Takeaway: Draw the five-gap chain in the exam - closing gaps 1-4 closes gap 5, and SERVQUAL measures the result on the five RATER dimensions.

Q4. Write short notes on any two of the following

Answer: Short notes: the pricing tripod balances cost, competition and customers; physical evidence tangibilizes the intangible; advocacy is trusted word-of-mouth; and technology has transformed service delivery through self-service and online channels.

(a) Pricing Tripod - Service pricing must balance three bases: costs (the floor below which the firm loses money), competitors (prices charged for similar services), and customers (what the market is willing to pay). All three legs are needed - a price set on cost alone may be too high or too low for the market. Example: airlines price tickets above cost and within what travellers will pay on a given route.

(b) Role of Physical Evidence in Marketing of Services - Because services are intangible, physical evidence - premises, equipment, uniforms, furniture and brochures - is the visible proof customers use to judge quality before buying. Example: the marble lobby, polished counters and uniformed staff of a five-star hotel signal luxury, while a clean, orderly bank branch signals safety. Evidence sets expectations and supports the brand image.

(c) Positive/Negative Advocacy - Advocacy is customer word-of-mouth, the most trusted source of information for services. Positive advocacy follows a delighting experience and brings new customers free; negative advocacy follows a service failure and spreads fast, especially on social media and review sites, damaging reputation. Example: one bad experience posted online can deter many travellers, so firms track reviews and invest in service recovery.

(d) Impact of Technology on Customer Services - Technology has transformed service delivery: self-service (ATMs, kiosks, self-checkout), online booking (IRCTC, Oyo, Zomato), chatbots and AI support, CRM systems that personalize offers, and real-time tracking. Example: IRCTC moved railway bookings online, enabling Tatkal and e-ticketing. Technology lowers cost, standardizes quality and raises convenience.

Key points to remember
  • The question asks for any two - choose the two you know best.
  • Pricing tripod = cost, competition, customers.
  • Each note needs a definition, 2-3 points and an Indian example.

Takeaway: For any short note give a definition, two to three points and an Indian example - the pricing tripod, physical evidence, advocacy and technology are the most examinable notes.

Q5(a). Justify the differential pricing strategy for services (IRCTC Tatkal case)

Answer: Differential pricing charges different prices for the same service to different segments or at different times, and it is justified for services whenever capacity is perishable, customers differ in willingness to pay, and demand can be segmented by time or urgency - exactly the conditions behind IRCTC's Tatkal scheme.

Differential (discriminatory) pricing means charging different prices to different customers, for different versions or at different times, even though the cost of the service is the same. IRCTC's Tatkal and Premium Tatkal scheme is a classic justification of this strategy for services:

  • Perishable capacity: a train seat is a perishable service - if it is not sold, the revenue is lost forever. Differential pricing sells seats that would otherwise remain empty, especially in peak travel seasons, and converts wastage into revenue.
  • Capturing consumer surplus: different travellers value the same seat differently. A business traveller urgently needing a ticket is willing to pay more than a budget traveller. Premium Tatkal, priced above standard Tatkal, captures the surplus of those who value speed and certainty of booking most.
  • Managing demand and supply: higher prices for Premium Tatkal reduce excess demand, ensure that the most urgent passengers get tickets, and lower the chance of queue-jumping and black-marketing in the ordinary quota.
  • Segment-based fairness: the scheme lets a budget passenger pay the normal fare and an urgent passenger pay a premium - a rational segmentation of the same service, justified by urgency, timing and availability rather than by any extra cost to IRCTC.

Thus differential pricing for services is justified whenever capacity is perishable, customers differ in willingness to pay, and the service can be segmented by time or urgency - exactly the conditions of the IRCTC Tatkal scheme.

Key points to remember
  • Differential pricing = different prices for the same service to different segments.
  • Justified by perishability, surplus capture and demand management.
  • Tatkal vs Premium Tatkal = urgency-based segmentation of one service.

Takeaway: Tatkal versus Premium Tatkal is the classic segmentation-based differential pricing example on perishable capacity - quoting the 10% and 30% fares adds authenticity.

Q5(b). What are the possible advantages or disadvantages of differential pricing over uniform pricing ?

Answer: Differential pricing earns more revenue, fills off-peak capacity and widens the served market, but it risks perceived unfairness, administrative complexity, arbitrage and customer resentment - so service firms trade it off against simpler, fairer uniform pricing.

Advantages of differential pricing over uniform pricing:

  • Higher revenue and profit: by charging more to those who can pay more and less to those who cannot, total revenue exceeds what one uniform price would earn; airlines and hotels routinely increase revenue this way.
  • Better capacity utilization: lower prices fill off-peak seats, hotel rooms and counters that would otherwise lie idle, spreading fixed costs over more units.
  • Market segmentation and access: budget-priced options (such as Tatkal at 10-30% of fare) keep the service affordable for lower-income segments while premium options serve the affluent, widening the served market.
  • Demand management: higher prices in peak periods discourage over-demand and queue-jumping, balancing load over time.

Disadvantages over uniform pricing:

  • Perceived unfairness: customers who pay more for the same seat feel exploited, and complaints rise when the price difference is visible, harming trust and brand image.
  • Administrative complexity: different rates, quotas and rules are costly to design, communicate and enforce, and invite errors and disputes, as IRCTC's tiered Tatkal rules show.
  • Arbitrage and resale: price differences create opportunities to buy low and resell high, feeding ticket black-marketing unless tightly controlled.
  • Customer resentment and migration: regular customers may wait for cheaper periods, shift demand unpredictably, or switch to competitors that offer simpler, uniform pricing.

Uniform pricing, in contrast, is simple, transparent and perceived as fair, but it leaves revenue on the table and cannot manage perishable capacity - so service firms usually trade off the two, using differential pricing where segments are clearly distinguishable.

Key points to remember
  • Advantages: more revenue, capacity utilization, segmentation, demand control.
  • Disadvantages: perceived unfairness, complexity, arbitrage, resentment.
  • Uniform pricing is simple and fair but wastes revenue from perishable capacity.

Takeaway: End with the trade-off - differential pricing maximizes revenue from perishable capacity, while uniform pricing wins on simplicity and perceived fairness.
