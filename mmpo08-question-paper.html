Q1. What is R&D? Explain the three types of R&D and their characteristics.

Answer: R&D is systematic investigation that converts new knowledge into better products, processes and services through three types - basic research, applied research and experimental development.

R&D (Research and Development) is systematic investigation carried out to discover new knowledge and turn it into new or improved products, processes and services. It is a long-term investment, not a short-term cost, and it must be aligned with business strategy.

There are three types of R&D:

  • Basic research: original investigation to gain new knowledge without a specific application in view. Highest risk, longest timeline, usually government funded, output is publications.
  • Applied research: directed towards a specific practical aim. Medium risk, mixed funding, output is patents and prototypes.
  • Experimental development: systematic work using existing knowledge to produce new materials, products or processes. Lowest risk, shortest timeline, corporate funded, output is commercial products.

Example: In a pharmaceutical company, basic research discovers the molecule, applied research develops the drug formulation, and experimental development runs the clinical trials that bring the medicine to market.

Key points to remember
  • Types follow the lifecycle: Basic → Applied → Development → Commercialisation.
  • Risk falls and cost rises as you move from basic research to development.
  • R&D intensity = R&D expenditure ÷ sales revenue; pharma spends 15-20%.

Takeaway: Balance the portfolio, because risk falls but cost and commercial focus rise as research moves from basic to development.

Q2. Distinguish between incremental, radical and disruptive innovation with suitable examples.

Answer: Incremental innovation improves existing offerings step by step, radical innovation creates breakthroughs new to the world, and disruptive innovation starts at the low end of the market and displaces established leaders.

Innovation is the implementation of a new or significantly improved product, process or method. It differs from invention because it must be commercialised, not just created.

  • Incremental innovation: small, continuous improvements to existing products or processes. Low risk, low uncertainty. Example: each new iPhone model after the original.
  • Radical innovation: a breakthrough that is new to the world and creates entirely new products or markets. High risk, high uncertainty. Example: the jet engine or the first mobile phone.
  • Disruptive innovation: Christensen's concept - a cheaper, simpler offering that starts at the low end of a market and slowly displaces established competitors. Example: the iPhone disrupted Nokia and BlackBerry; Netflix disrupted video rental stores.

Example: The original iPhone was disruptive (it created the smartphone era), while every later model is an incremental improvement of that breakthrough.

Key points to remember
  • Incremental = improve the same market; radical = new technology; disruptive = displace leaders from below.
  • Disruption is about market entry strategy, not just technology.
  • Innovation can also be classified by type: product, process, service, business model, organisational.

Takeaway: Disruption is a market-entry strategy from below, not just a new technology.

Q3. Explain the technology lifecycle S-curve and Rogers' adoption categories. What is meant by "crossing the chasm"?

Answer: Technology performance follows an S-curve - introduction, growth, maturity, decline - while adoption spreads across five Rogers categories, and the chasm is the gap between early adopters and the early majority where many innovations die.

Technology evolves through an S-curve with four stages: introduction (slow), growth (rapid), maturity (slow again) and decline. Performance improves slowly at first, then jumps during growth, then flattens as the technology matures.

Adoption of a new technology follows a bell curve with five categories (Rogers):

  • Innovators (2.5%): risk-takers who try anything new.
  • Early adopters (13.5%): opinion leaders who buy for the vision.
  • Early majority (34%): pragmatists who need proven solutions.
  • Late majority (34%): sceptics who adopt under peer pressure.
  • Laggards (16%): the last group, resistant to change.

Crossing the chasm (Geoffrey Moore) is the gap between early adopters and the early majority. Early adopters tolerate imperfections; the early majority wants complete, proven products. Many technologies fail because they cannot cross this gap.

Example: Electric vehicles followed the S-curve - slow adoption from 2010-2018, rapid growth from 2019 onwards, as Tesla and others crossed the chasm from enthusiasts to the mainstream buyer.

Key points to remember
  • Adoption order: innovators → early adopters → early majority → late majority → laggards.
  • The chasm lies between early adopters and the early majority.
  • Diffusion can be technology push (R&D-driven) or market pull (customer-need-driven).

Takeaway: To cross the chasm, give the pragmatic early majority a complete, proven product.

Q4. Explain the various modes of technology transfer with Indian examples.

Answer: Technology moves from source to recipient through licensing, joint ventures, FDI and turnkey projects, each balancing control, cost and risk, with India absorbing technology along the AAAII path.

Technology transfer is the movement of technology, knowledge and skills from a source organisation to a recipient. Firms choose a mode by balancing control, cost and risk.

  • Licensing: the owner grants rights to use technology in exchange for royalties. Low control, low risk. Example: franchise agreements.
  • Joint venture: two firms share ownership, risk and rewards. Example: the Maruti-Suzuki JV brought Japanese manufacturing technology to India - India gained manufacturing capability and Suzuki gained market access.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): setting up wholly owned operations. High control, high risk. Example: Samsung India's factories.
  • Turnkey projects: the supplier delivers a complete, ready-to-run facility. High cost, medium risk. Example: power plant projects.

Example: After acquiring Jaguar Land Rover in 2008, Tata Motors absorbed the technology through the stages of acquisition, assimilation, adaptation, improvement and innovation (AAAII).

Key points to remember
  • Control vs risk trade-off: licensing (low control) → JV (shared) → FDI (high control).
  • Absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal) decides whether transferred technology creates real capability.
  • Indian absorption path: AAAII - acquire, assimilate, adapt, improve, innovate.

Takeaway: The recipient's absorptive capacity decides whether transferred technology becomes real capability.

Q5. Describe the main steps in R&D project selection and explain any one method of selecting R&D projects.

Answer: R&D project selection runs from idea generation through screening, evaluation, portfolio balancing and review, and scoring models are the simplest transparent method of choosing between projects.

R&D project selection is the process of choosing which projects to fund from the many ideas generated, because resources are always limited. The main steps are:

  • Idea generation: collect proposals from scientists, marketers, customers and partners.
  • Screening: reject ideas that do not fit the company's strategy or resources.
  • Evaluation: score each project on criteria such as technical feasibility, market size, cost and risk.
  • Portfolio balancing: choose a mix of safe short-term projects and risky long-term ones.
  • Review: re-evaluate projects at milestones and stop failures early.

One method - scoring models: each project is rated on factors like market attractiveness, technical success probability and strategic fit; the weights are multiplied by the scores and projects with the highest total score are funded. It is simple, transparent and easy to compare many projects.

Example: A consumer electronics firm scores every new product idea out of 10 on cost, market size and technology readiness, and only projects scoring above 24 get budget.

Key points to remember
  • Selection steps: generate → screen → evaluate → balance portfolio → review.
  • Other methods include checklists, NPV/IRR analysis and Delphi.
  • Never put all funds in one type of project; balance risk across the portfolio.

Takeaway: Score projects on weighted criteria and balance safe short-term with risky long-term projects.

Q6. What are Intellectual Property Rights? Briefly explain patent, copyright and trademark.

Answer: IPR are legal rights protecting creations of the mind - patents protect inventions, copyright protects creative works, and trademarks protect brand identity.

Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) are legal rights that protect creations of the human mind - inventions, writings, designs and brand names - and give the creator exclusive ownership for a limited time, in return for making the creation public.

  • Patent: exclusive right over an invention (a product or process) for about 20 years. The invention must be new, inventive and industrially applicable. Example: a patent on a new drug molecule.
  • Copyright: right over original literary, artistic and creative works - books, music, films, software. It protects the expression of an idea, not the idea itself, and lasts for the author's lifetime plus 60 years in India. Example: a textbook or a song.
  • Trademark: a distinctive sign - a word, logo or symbol - that identifies goods or services of one firm and distinguishes them from competitors. Example: the "Make in India" logo or the Amul girl.

Example: Tata's R&D lab protects its new engine designs with patents, its training manuals with copyright, and its Tata name and logo with trademarks.

Key points to remember
  • Patents protect inventions; copyright protects creative works; trademarks protect brand identity.
  • IPR rewards innovation by giving temporary monopoly in exchange for disclosure.
  • In India, patents are governed by the Patents Act 1970, copyright by the Copyright Act 1957.

Takeaway: IPR give a temporary monopoly in exchange for public disclosure, which is how innovation gets both rewarded and spread.
