Q1. Describe the various roles of materials management in the context of internal and external interfaces to materials management system.

Answer: Materials management plays internal roles (production, finance, marketing, engineering, stores) and external roles (suppliers, transporters, government, customers, banks), making it the nervous system that controls 50-70% of product cost.

Materials management plans, procures, stores and controls the flow of materials from suppliers through the plant to customers, with the twin goals of right quality, right quantity, right time, right price. Its roles can be seen through the two interfaces it serves.

Internal interfaces: With production/operations, it supplies materials to keep machines and lines running, prevents shortages and coordinates with the production schedule. With finance, it controls the money locked in inventory, prepares budgets, and keeps working capital low. With marketing, it supports sales promises by ensuring finished goods are available for delivery. With engineering and design, it provides data on materials, substitutes and standards for new products. With stores and quality control, it manages receiving, storage, issue and inspection of materials. In this way materials management acts as the nervous system connecting every department that touches materials.

External interfaces: With suppliers, it negotiates price, terms and delivery schedules, develops and rates vendors, and builds partnerships. With transporters and logistics providers, it arranges inbound and outbound movement and warehousing. With government and regulatory bodies, it handles taxation (GST), import-export documentation, pollution and safety compliance. With customers, it feeds demand forecasts into procurement planning. With banks and financial institutions, it manages payment terms, letters of credit and insurance.

Because materials cost 50-70% of a manufacturing firm’s product cost, the materials manager’s role is strategic: cost reduction, supply assurance, inventory control and inter-departmental coordination. A classic example is how Maruti Suzuki’s materials department coordinates with its supplier park - internal lines receive components just in time, while externally, suppliers operate right next to the plant.

Key points to remember:

  • Internal interfaces: production, finance, marketing, engineering, stores, quality.
  • External interfaces: suppliers, transporters, government, customers, banks.
  • Materials = 50-70% of product cost - a strategic profit lever.
  • Right quality, quantity, time and price is the core role.

Takeaway: The four-rights discipline (quality, quantity, time, price) at both interfaces is what makes materials management strategic.

Q2. “The dynamic relationship between the buyer and seller enhances business transactions.” Comment.

Answer: Yes - a dynamic buyer-seller relationship built on trust, information sharing and joint planning improves transactions through reliable supply, better quality, stable demand and lower cost for both sides.

This statement is largely true in modern supply chains. A dynamic buyer-seller relationship is one that evolves from a one-time, price-driven transaction into a long-term partnership with trust, information sharing and joint problem solving - and this genuinely improves business transactions on both sides.

For the buyer, a strong relationship brings reliable supply, better quality (suppliers invest in the buyer’s standards), faster response to urgent orders, and often better prices through volume commitments and transparency of costs. For the seller, it brings stable, predictable demand, lower marketing cost, early involvement in the buyer’s product design, and faster payments. Information flows both ways - the buyer shares forecasts, the seller shares capacity and delivery status - which reduces uncertainty, stockouts and excess inventory for both. Dynamic relationships also allow joint development of new materials and early-warning of problems, as seen in the Japanese keiretsu model where suppliers and assemblers plan together (the Toyota and Suzuki approaches covered in the study material).

However, there is a balance to strike. Long-term partnering must not become complacency: prices must stay competitive, and dependence on one seller is risky - if that supplier fails, the buyer’s whole plant can stop. Indian firms manage this with vendor rating, periodic renegotiation, and a small set of preferred suppliers rather than exclusive dependence. The relationship should be dynamic - trust combined with regular performance review - so that mutual benefit, not inertia, keeps transactions healthy. In short, yes: a living, two-way relationship raises quality, lowers cost and stabilises flow, provided both parties keep the relationship performance-driven.

Key points to remember:

  • Dynamic buyer-seller relationships build trust, transparency and joint planning.
  • Benefits: reliable supply, better quality, lower risk, faster response.
  • Risk: over-dependence and complacency on price.
  • Solution: vendor rating, renegotiation, preferred supplier set.

Takeaway: Keep the relationship performance-driven with vendor rating and renegotiation, or dependence and complacency will hurt.

Q3. Define WIP inventory. State its advantages and disadvantages. How do supply chain and production strategies influence in-process inventory?

Answer: WIP is material caught inside production that buffers breakdowns but locks up capital and hides bottlenecks, and its level depends on push or pull production logic and supply-chain reliability.

WIP (Work-in-Process) inventory is the material that has entered the production process but is not yet a finished product - components being machined, sub-assemblies, goods waiting between workstations. It is material whose value has increased by the labour and overhead already applied, so it is more expensive to hold than raw material.

Advantages: WIP buffers the production line - if one machine breaks down, work-in-process keeps downstream stations busy; it smooths uneven processing times between operations; it helps utilise machines fully by avoiding idle time; and it protects against supplier or quality surprises.

Disadvantages: WIP ties up working capital in half-finished goods; it occupies floor space; it carries risk of damage, obsolescence and quality defects (a defect hidden in WIP may only be found late, causing rework); it lengthens manufacturing lead time; and it hides production problems - a plant with piles of WIP cannot see its bottlenecks, a point central to lean thinking.

How strategies influence WIP: In push systems, production is driven by forecasts and batches, so WIP builds up between stages and is high. In pull systems (JIT), each stage produces only what the next stage consumes, so WIP is deliberately kept near zero. Production strategy matters too: large lot sizes and long setups raise WIP; small-lot, flexible lines cut it. Supply chain strategy matters as well - with reliable suppliers and short lead times (e.g., a supplier park next to the plant), firms can cut safety stock and WIP; with long, unreliable supplier lead times, firms carry more in-process inventory to protect schedules.

EOQ mini-example (materials side): Lot sizing also shapes inventory. A Mumbai textile mill uses 10,000 kg of cotton yarn a year, ordering cost Rs 2,000 per order, holding cost Rs 50 per kg per year. EOQ = √(2 × D × S ÷ H) = √(2 × 10,000 × 2,000 ÷ 50) = √8,00,000 = 894 kg. Orders per year = 10,000 ÷ 894 ≈ 11.2 orders; total inventory cost = √(2 × 10,000 × 2,000 × 50) = Rs 44,721. Ordering in EOQ-sized lots balances ordering and holding cost and keeps inventory - including WIP feeding schedules - at its economical level.

Key points to remember:

  • WIP = material in production, not yet finished; costly to hold.
  • Pros: buffers breakdowns, utilises machines. Cons: locks capital, hides problems.
  • Push systems raise WIP; pull/JIT systems cut it.
  • EOQ = √(2DS/H); order quantity that minimises total cost.

Takeaway: Lean pull systems cut WIP to near zero because high WIP raises cost and conceals problems.

Q4. Describe the six stages of the spare parts life cycle and activities in each step.

Answer: Spare parts pass through six life-cycle stages - identification, procurement, receipt/storage, issue, repair/reconditioning and disposal - each with defined activities that keep machines running.

Spare parts are items kept to replace failed or worn parts of machines and equipment. Their life cycle has six stages, each with specific activities:

  • 1. Identification and pre-purchase analysis: Identify which spare is needed, whether it is critical to production, its failure rate and lead time. Decide the stocking policy - critical spares (one failure stops the plant) may be stocked even at high cost, while cheap, common items are stocked freely and expensive, rarely-used ones are purchased on demand.
  • 2. Procurement: Source the spare from the original equipment manufacturer (OEM) or approved vendors, invite quotations, negotiate price and delivery, and place the purchase order. Original spares cost more but give quality and warranty; local substitutes are cheaper but riskier.
  • 3. Receipt, inspection and storage: Verify quantity and quality against the order, inspect for damage or wrong specifications, then code, catalogue and store the part properly - special care for items needing dry, dust-free or temperature-controlled storage.
  • 4. Issue/use (consumption): Issue the spare against authorised requisitions, record which machine consumed it, update stock records, and analyse usage patterns and failures. Accurate issue data tells you which spares are really needed and how often.
  • 5. Repair, reconditioning and overhaul: For repairable parts (motors, pumps, valves), send failed units for repair or reconditioning instead of scrapping. The repaired part re-enters the stock or returns to service, saving purchase cost and extending equipment life.
  • 6. Disposal/obsolescence: When equipment is replaced or technology changes, old spares become obsolete. Identify such surplus stock, dispose of it by transfer to other plants, sale at scrap value, or return to the supplier, and update records so dead stock does not sit in the warehouse.

Managing all six stages well - through ABC/VED classification (Vital, Essential, Desirable), proper records and regular review - ensures machines keep running without capital being wasted on dead spares.

Key points to remember:

  • Six stages: identification, procurement, receipt/storage, issue, repair/reconditioning, disposal.
  • Classify spares by criticality (VED) to decide stocking policy.
  • Issue data drives future purchase decisions.
  • Repair before scrap; dispose obsolete stock promptly.

Takeaway: Use VED classification to stock vital spares at any cost and dispose obsolete ones promptly.

Q5. Why is warehouse layout important? What problems can occur with errors in the layout of a warehouse?

Answer: Warehouse layout matters because it sets the cost and speed of every movement, and layout errors produce slow picking, congestion, poor space use, damage, accidents and weak stock control.

Warehouse layout means the physical arrangement of storage areas, aisles, receiving, picking and dispatch zones, and material handling equipment within the warehouse. Layout is important because it determines the cost and speed of every movement in the warehouse - a warehouse exists to receive, store and dispatch goods, and each of those activities involves movement, which costs time, labour and equipment use.

A good layout follows a clear flow (receipt → inspection → storage → picking → packing → dispatch), puts fast-moving (A-class) items in the most accessible positions, keeps aisles wide enough for equipment, uses vertical space, and places dispatch near exit doors. The result is short travel distances, high storage utilisation, fast order filling and safe operations. Indian e-commerce and retail warehouses (Amazon, Flipkart, BigBasket, DMart) compete on this - their fulfilment centres are designed so pickers walk the shortest possible route.

Errors in layout cause serious problems:

  • Long travel times - slow order picking raises labour cost and delays deliveries.
  • Congestion and bottlenecks - narrow aisles and wrong placement create queues at receiving or dispatch.
  • Poor space utilisation - too much floor space wasted on wide or mislaid aisles, or single-level storage where racks could stack higher.
  • Higher damage and errors - poorly arranged goods get knocked, wrong items get picked, and returns increase.
  • Safety hazards - blocked fire exits, awkward equipment movement and poor lighting cause accidents.
  • Hindered inventory control - chaotic storage makes counting, locating and rotating stock (FIFO) difficult, leading to expiry and obsolescence.

Since warehouse operating cost is a large share of logistics cost, a layout error is not a one-time nuisance but a recurring cost on every single order the warehouse processes.

Key points to remember:

  • Layout decides movement cost and speed - it shapes every order.
  • Flow: receipt → storage → picking → packing → dispatch.
  • Keep fast movers closest; use vertical space.
  • Bad layout = slow picking, congestion, damage, accidents, poor stock control.

Takeaway: Follow the receipt-storage-picking-dispatch flow and place fast-moving A-class items closest to dispatch.

Q6. What are the advantages of organizing a purchasing department into specialized sub-units? What are its disadvantages? How can a firm overcome these disadvantages?

Answer: Specialised purchasing sub-units give deep commodity expertise, strong supplier ties and clear accountability, but risk duplication and lost coordination - which centralised common items, shared data and cross-functional teams fix.

In large firms, purchasing is often split into specialised sub-units - by material type (raw materials, packaging, capital equipment, MRO items), by function (order processing, vendor development, price analysis, contracts), or by buyer who owns a commodity from sourcing to delivery.

Advantages: Buyers develop deep expertise in their commodity - knowing the market, technology and cost structure of, say, steel or electronics; they build strong supplier relationships, which give better prices, priority supply and early information; responsibility is clear and accountable; decision-making is faster because each sub-unit can act within its area; and training is easier since each unit focuses on a manageable range of items. This is why large Indian companies like Tata Steel and Larsen & Toubro have separate purchase groups for different materials.

Disadvantages: There can be duplication of effort - several sub-units ordering from the same supplier separately lose volume discounts; lack of coordination - one sub-unit may buy an item another is already buying at a different price; over-specialisation makes buyers inflexible and creates a bottleneck if one expert leaves; internal rivalry and inconsistent policies can appear; and total workload balancing becomes difficult.

Overcoming the disadvantages: (1) Centralise common items - MRO and stationery - under one cell while specialised cells handle strategic materials; (2) share supplier and price data through a common purchasing database or ERP so every sub-unit sees the same rates; (3) use cross-functional teams for major contracts; (4) rotate buyers across sub-units to build multi-skill and reduce dependency; (5) set uniform policies and procedures for all cells, with one purchasing manager coordinating them; and (6) consolidate orders with the same supplier into one negotiation. With these measures, a firm keeps the expertise of specialisation without losing scale and coordination.

Key points to remember:

  • Specialised sub-units = expertise, strong vendor relations, clear accountability.
  • Risks: duplication, poor coordination, lost discounts, rigidity.
  • Fixes: centralise common items, shared data, cross-functional teams, rotation, uniform policy.

Takeaway: Specialise for expertise, then centralise coordination so scale discounts are not lost.

Q7. Short notes (any three of five)

Answer: MRP nets the MPS, BOM and inventory records into planned orders; push systems make to forecast while JIT pulls on demand; standardisation cuts variety but limits flexibility; the Japanese approach treats inventory as waste; and the materials manager coordinates all of it.

(a) Objectives of MRP: MRP (Materials Requirements Planning) is a computer-based system that plans the quantities and timing of materials needed for production. Its objectives are: (1) ensure availability - have the right materials, in right quantity, available when production needs them, avoiding stockouts; (2) minimise inventory - carry only what is actually required by the production schedule, cutting holding cost; (3) plan manufacturing activities, delivery schedules and purchasing - generate order releases to the shop floor and purchase orders to suppliers; (4) reschedule priorities when orders change or deliveries are late; and (5) provide accurate, timely information on net requirements for management decisions. MRP works from three inputs - the Master Production Schedule (MPS), the Bill of Materials (BOM) and inventory records - and computes net requirements as gross requirements minus scheduled receipts minus on-hand stock, then plans orders. The payoff is lower inventory with higher service levels.

Key points to remember:

  • MRP objectives: availability, low inventory, planned orders, rescheduling, accurate data.
  • Inputs: MPS + BOM + inventory records.
  • Net requirements = gross - scheduled receipts - on-hand.

(b) Push Based Materials Management: In a push system, materials are produced and moved forward based on a forecasted schedule - each stage makes its batch and pushes it to the next stage whether or not it is needed now. Production is planned in advance from the MPS and sales forecasts. Advantages: it suits stable demand and allows economies of scale through large batches; planning is simpler and resources can be loaded in advance. Disadvantages: it creates high WIP and finished-goods inventory, hides production problems, and reacts slowly to demand changes - forecast errors produce either stockouts or piles of unwanted goods (the bullwhip effect). Push-based management is typical of mass production; the opposite, pull-based (JIT) systems, produce only on actual demand signals like kanban cards. Many Indian manufacturers run push planning for the long-term schedule and pull execution at the shop-floor level.

Key points to remember:

  • Push = make to forecast; material moves forward on schedule.
  • Pros: scale economies, simple planning; cons: high inventory, slow response.
  • Contrast with pull/JIT: make only what the next stage consumes.

(c) Disadvantages of Standardisation: Standardisation means using common specifications, sizes and grades of materials across products and plants. It brings huge benefits - fewer stock-keeping units, bulk buying, interchangeability - but it also has disadvantages: (1) it restricts choice and flexibility, so a designer cannot use a technically better or cheaper material if it is not in the standard list; (2) it may stifle innovation, because new products are forced into existing standards; (3) standards can become obsolete as technology advances, and updating them is slow; (4) over-standardisation can increase cost if the standard grade is higher quality (and costlier) than a particular application needs; (5) it creates dependence on one supplier or one specification, raising supply risk; and (6) implementation requires initial investment in classification, codification and training. The answer is selective standardisation - standardise fast-moving, common items and allow controlled exceptions where design or economics justify them.

Key points to remember:

  • Standardisation cuts variety but limits flexibility and innovation.
  • Standards can become obsolete or costlier than needed.
  • Selective standardisation balances benefits and drawbacks.

(d) Japanese approach to Materials Management: The Japanese approach, developed by Toyota and others, treats inventory as waste and aims to eliminate it. Its pillars are Just-in-Time (JIT) - materials arrive exactly when needed, neither early nor late, so stock, WIP and finished goods stay minimal; Kanban - simple cards or signals through which the consuming stage pulls exactly what it needs from the producing stage; close supplier partnerships - few suppliers, long-term contracts, frequent small deliveries, and suppliers located near the plant, with the buyer often helping suppliers improve quality; Total Quality Management - quality built at the source, since a defect in a zero-stock line stops production immediately; and continuous improvement (Kaizen) - workers at every level constantly reduce waste and improve flow. Results are very low inventory, short lead times, low cost and high quality. Limitations: it needs stable demand, reliable suppliers and disciplined operations, which is why Indian firms apply it selectively - Maruti Suzuki’s supplier park is a famous JIT adaptation.

Key points to remember:

  • Japanese approach = inventory is waste; eliminate it.
  • Tools: JIT, kanban, supplier partnerships, TQM, kaizen.
  • Needs stable demand and disciplined, reliable suppliers.

(e) Functions of a Materials Manager: The materials manager heads the materials department and coordinates all material-related functions. Key functions: (1) materials planning and control - forecasting demand, determining quantities and timing through tools like MRP and EOQ; (2) purchasing/procurement - sourcing, negotiation, order placement and vendor development; (3) inventory management - controlling stock levels of raw materials, WIP and finished goods using ABC, VED, FSN analysis and reorder points; (4) stores management - receipt, storage, issue, codification and warehouse management; (5) materials handling and transportation - moving materials economically inside and outside the plant; (6) quality assurance and inspection - ensuring incoming materials meet specifications; (7) disposal of scrap and surplus - realising value from obsolete and waste materials; and (8) coordination and reporting - linking production, finance, marketing and suppliers, and reporting performance to top management. The materials manager is thus both a cost controller and a coordinator at the heart of the firm.

Key points to remember:

  • Core functions: planning, purchasing, inventory control, stores, handling, disposal.
  • Also inspection, vendor development and coordination.
  • Materials manager = cost controller + coordinator.

Takeaway: Match the planning system and organisation to demand stability - that choice sets your inventory and cost levels.
