Unit 1: Management Control Systems — An Introduction

📖 Core Concepts
▶

  • Open vs Closed Loop: Open-loop has no feedback; closed-loop uses feedback to self-correct

📖 Goal Congruence

Goal Congruence is when individual goals align with organizational goals — the fundamental purpose of MCS. A well-designed control system ensures that managers acting in their own self-interest also serve the organization's interests. For example, if a sales manager's bonus depends on revenue (not profit), they may offer excessive discounts — breaking goal congruence.

📖 Ethics in MCS

Control systems must prevent ethical failures. Unethical behavior can be checked through codes of conduct, whistleblower policies, and cultural norms. Indian companies like Infosys and TCS have robust ethics frameworks integrated into their MCS — Infosys' Code of Conduct covers conflicts of interest, insider trading, and anti-corruption policies.

💡 Real-World Example

A manufacturing company uses action controls (SOPs), results controls (production targets with bonuses), and personnel controls (training, corporate culture) simultaneously to manage shop floor workers.

⚠ Exam Tip

Define MCS using both Anthony & Dearden (1981) and Anthony & Govindarajan (1994) — examiners often ask for the evolution of the concept.

Unit 3: Designing Management Control Systems

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Responsibility Centers
  • Cybernetic Paradigm: Set standard → Measure actual → Compare → Take corrective action
  • MIS: Integrates explicit and tacit knowledge for Total Knowledge Management
  • Critical Success Factors (CSFs): Limited areas where results, if satisfactory, ensure success
  • Behavioral Aspects: MCS must consider resistance to control, gaming, budget slack
  • Action Plans: Derived from CSFs for performance improvement
📖 Cybernetic Control Loop — Detailed

The cybernetic paradigm is the foundation of all control systems. The four-step process works as follows:

  1. Set Standard: Define what "good" looks like (e.g., production target of 1000 units/day)
  2. Measure Actual: Collect real performance data (e.g., actual output = 950 units)
  3. Compare: Find deviations (e.g., 50 units short = 5% unfavorable variance)
  4. Corrective Action: Investigate and fix (e.g., machine maintenance, overtime, training)
📖 Behavioral Aspects — Gaming and Budget Slack

Budget Slack: Managers deliberately understate revenues or overstate costs in budgets to make targets easier to achieve. For example, a sales manager budgeting ₹8 crore when they expect ₹10 crore — creating a ₹2 crore cushion.

Gaming: Managers manipulate metrics to appear better. Example: shipping products on December 31 to meet annual sales targets, even though the customer didn't need them until February.

💡 Real-World Example

A retail chain identifies CSFs as inventory turnover, customer satisfaction, and store productivity — all MCS metrics are derived from these. Reliance Retail tracks 5 CSFs: same-store sales growth, inventory days, customer NPS, employee productivity, and shrinkage rate.

Unit 5: Cost Centres

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Budgetary Control
  • Engineered Costs: Directly related to output — raw materials, direct labor (precisely measurable)
  • Discretionary Costs: Not directly related — R&D, advertising, training (management judgment)
  • Zero Base Budgeting (ZBB): Every budget justified from zero each period
  • Activity Based Costing (ABC): Allocate overheads based on cost-driving activities
  • Standard Costing: Set predetermined costs, compare with actual, analyze variances
  • Target Costing: Target cost = Market price - Desired profit (used by Japanese manufacturers)
📖 Standard Costing Variance Breakdown

Standard costing sets predetermined cost standards. When actual costs differ, variances are analyzed:

  • Material Variance: Price variance (actual vs standard price) + Usage variance (actual vs standard quantity)
  • Labor Variance: Rate variance (actual vs standard wage) + Efficiency variance (actual vs standard hours)
  • Overhead Variance: Spending variance + Efficiency variance + Volume variance
💡 Real-World Example

A hospital uses engineered costs for medical supplies (per patient) and discretionary costs for community health programs.

⚠ Exam Tip

ZBB vs traditional budgeting is a common comparison question — know pros and cons of each.

🧠 Memory Aid

EDZ = Engineered, Discretionary, ZBB

⭐ Summary
  • Engineered costs are measurable; discretionary costs rely on judgment
  • ZBB forces justification from zero; ABC links costs to activities

Unit 6: Profit Centres

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Performance Measurement
  • Profit = Revenue - Costs — measures manager's overall effectiveness
  • Advantages: Measures overall performance, encourages autonomy, develops general management skills
  • Challenges: Transfer pricing between divisions, allocation of common costs, short-term focus
  • Common Cost Allocation: Allocate corporate HQ costs to divisions using revenue %, headcount, or floor area
📖 Profit Centre — Controllability Principle

A profit centre should only be evaluated on items the manager can control. If a division manager cannot control corporate overhead, those costs should either be excluded from their P&L or clearly separated. This ensures fair evaluation and prevents dysfunctional behavior.

💡 Real-World Example

Godrej Consumer Products operates as a profit centre — each product division (home care, personal care) manages its own P&L.

⚠ Exam Tip

Profit centres work best when managers have authority over both inputs and outputs — explain when NOT to use them.

Unit 7: Investment Centres

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Balanced Scorecard
  • ROI = Profit / Investment × 100 — most common but may reject good projects
  • RI = Profit - (Investment × Required Rate) — considers cost of capital
  • EVA = NOPAT - (WACC × Capital) — true economic profit, most comprehensive
📖 ROI Limitation — Dysfunctional Behavior Example

Division A has ROI = 20%, company required rate = 12%. A new project offers 15% return.

  • Using ROI: Division rejects it (15% < 20%) — dysfunctional! The project earns above cost of capital.
  • Using RI: Division accepts it (15% > 12%) — adds value to the company.

This is why RI and EVA are superior to ROI for goal congruence.

💡 Real-World Example

A division has ₹50L profit on ₹200L investment. Required rate = 12%, WACC = 10%. ROI = 25%, RI = ₹26L, EVA = ₹25L.

⚠ Exam Tip

ROI, RI, and EVA calculations are guaranteed in exams. Always solve step by step with formulas written clearly.

▶

  • Tax Implications: MNCs use transfer pricing to shift profits across countries — must comply with regulations
  • Cost-Based Variants: Full cost, variable cost, cost-plus — each has different behavioral effects

📖 Transfer Pricing in India — IT Act Section 92

India's Income Tax Act Section 92-92F requires MNCs to follow the arm's length principle for international transactions between associated enterprises. The CBDT (Central Board of Direct Taxes) specifies 5 methods:

  1. Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) — market price for similar transactions
  2. Resale Price Method (RPM) — resale price minus margin
  3. Cost Plus Method (CPM) — cost plus appropriate markup
  4. Transactional Net Margin Method (TNMM) — net profit margin comparison
  5. Profit Split Method (PSM) — split combined profits based on contribution

Penalty for non-compliance: 100-300% of tax underreported. Indian companies must maintain transfer pricing documentation.

📖 Single vs Dual Pricing

Single Pricing: One price used for both divisions — simpler but may not serve both equally. Example: Market price of ₹500/unit is used for both buying and selling division.

Dual Pricing: Different prices for buying and selling divisions — more complex but fairer. Example: Selling division records ₹500 (market price), buying division pays ₹450 (cost-plus). Difference absorbed by corporate.

💡 Real-World Example

Tata Motors uses cost-plus 12% for Tata AutoComp components and market-based pricing for engines between plants. Reliance Industries uses negotiated transfer pricing between its refining and petrochemical divisions — Jio sources network equipment from Reliance Retail's procurement arm at negotiated rates.

📖 Variance Analysis — Detailed

Variance = Actual - Budget. A favorable variance means actual is better than budget (higher revenue or lower cost). An unfavorable variance means actual is worse. Key variances include:

  • Sales Variance: (Actual Sales - Budgeted Sales) — due to volume or price
  • Material Variance: Price variance + Usage variance
  • Labor Variance: Rate variance + Efficiency variance
  • Overhead Variance: Spending variance + Volume variance

SBI prepares annual budgets for credit growth, deposits, NPA provisioning. FY2024 budgeted 12% growth, achieved 15.2%. TCS uses rolling quarterly budgets — forecasts updated every quarter with actual performance data.

📖 BSC Cause-Effect Chain

The BSC is not just 4 separate perspectives — they are linked in a cause-effect chain:

Learning & Growth (train employees, upgrade IT) → improves Internal Processes (faster, better quality) → leads to better Customer outcomes (satisfaction, retention) → drives Financial results (revenue, profit, ROI).

Example: Infosys invested in digital skills training (Learning) → improved project delivery time (Process) → higher client satisfaction (Customer) → increased revenue per employee (Financial).

📖 Benchmarking Types
  • Internal Benchmarking: Compare departments within the same company (e.g., comparing productivity of Maruti's Gurgaon vs Manesar plant)
  • Competitive Benchmarking: Compare with direct competitors (e.g., SBI vs HDFC Bank on NPA ratios)
  • Functional Benchmarking: Compare similar functions across industries (e.g., logistics of Amazon vs DHL)
  • Generic Benchmarking: Compare broad processes (e.g., customer service of Taj Hotels vs Singapore Airlines)
💡 Real-World Example

Wipro's BSC improved client retention from 85% to 92% over three years by tracking all four perspectives. Bajaj Auto uses BSC to balance financial targets with dealer satisfaction, production efficiency, and workforce capability metrics.

Unit 11: Reward and Compensation

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Inventory Control
  • Incentives: Monetary (bonuses, stock options) and Non-monetary (recognition, promotions, autonomy)
  • Design Principles: Clear, Timely, Fair, Linked to performance, Aligned with goals
  • Behavioral Impact: Incentives shape behavior — be careful what you measure and reward
  • Goal Congruence: Rewards should align individual and organizational objectives
📖 Dysfunctional Behavior from Poor Incentives

Poorly designed incentives lead to unintended consequences:

  • Sales: Salespeople offer excessive discounts to hit revenue targets — eroding margins
  • Production: Overproducing to meet volume targets — creating excess inventory
  • Budgeting: Spending remaining budget at year-end to avoid cuts next year — waste
  • Quality: Skipping quality checks to meet speed targets — defects increase
💡 Real-World Example

Infosys redesigned incentives in 2020 to include ESG metrics alongside financial KPIs — improved employee engagement by 12%.

Unit 12: Techniques of Management Control

📖 Core Concepts
▶
Quality Control
  • TQM: Continuous improvement — customer-focused, employee involvement
  • JIT: Inventory arrives just when needed — reduces carrying costs
  • ABC: Allocate costs based on cost-driving activities
  • Benchmarking: Learning from best practices
📖 TQM Principles (Deming's 14 Points)

Total Quality Management is built on the philosophy of W. Edwards Deming. Key principles include:

  • Customer Focus: Quality is defined by the customer, not the producer
  • Continuous Improvement (Kaizen): Small, incremental improvements every day
  • Employee Empowerment: Quality is everyone's responsibility, not just QC department
  • Process-Centered: Fix the process, not blame the person
  • Data-Driven Decisions: Use statistical tools (Pareto charts, control charts, fishbone diagrams)
📖 ABC — Activity Based Costing

Traditional costing allocates overheads based on volume (machine hours, labor hours). ABC allocates based on activities that drive costs:

  1. Identify activities (e.g., machine setup, quality inspection, material handling)
  2. Assign costs to each activity
  3. Identify cost drivers for each activity
  4. Allocate costs to products based on activity usage

Example: A product requiring 50 setups but low volume would be undercosted by traditional methods but correctly costed by ABC.

Unit 14: Multinational Organizations

📖 Core Concepts
▶
MCS in Practice
  • Additional Challenges: Currency risk, cultural differences, varying regulations, political risk
  • Transfer Pricing in MNCs: Used for tax optimization — must comply with arm's length principle
  • Performance Measurement: Must consider exchange rate fluctuations — constant vs current rate methods
  • Centralization vs Localization: Balance between global consistency and local adaptation
📖 Currency Risk Management

MNCs face three types of currency risk:

  • Transaction Risk: Loss when converting payments (e.g., Indian company paying in USD when rupee depreciates)
  • Translation Risk: Loss when consolidating foreign subsidiary accounts
  • Economic Risk: Long-term impact on competitiveness due to exchange rate changes

Hedging Tools: Forward contracts, currency futures, options, and natural hedging (matching revenues and costs in same currency).

MCS Framework Overview — The Big Picture

📖 MCS Framework Components

A complete Management Control System has these interconnected components:

  1. Strategic Planning: Setting long-term direction (3-5 year plans, vision, mission)
  2. Responsibility Structure: Defining who controls what (cost, revenue, profit, investment centres)
  3. Budgeting: Translating strategy into financial plans (fixed, flexible, ZBB, rolling)
  4. Transfer Pricing: Setting prices for internal transactions (market, cost, negotiated)
  5. Performance Measurement: Tracking results (ROI, RI, EVA, BSC, KPIs)
  6. Reward Systems: Motivating desired behavior (bonuses, stock options, recognition)
  7. Control Techniques: Supporting tools (TQM, JIT, ABC, benchmarking)
🧠 Master Framework Mnemonic

S-R-B-T-P-R-C = Strategic planning, Responsibility structure, Budgeting, Transfer pricing, Performance measurement, Rewards, Control techniques

Key Formulas

📖 Formula Sheet
  • ROI = Profit / Investment × 100
  • RI = Profit - (Investment × Required Rate of Return)
  • EVA = NOPAT - (WACC × Capital)
  • Variance = Actual - Budget (Favorable if positive, Unfavorable if negative)
  • Profit = Revenue - Costs
💡 Quick Numerical Example

Division with ₹50L profit on ₹200L investment. Required rate = 12%, WACC = 10%. ROI = 50/200 × 100 = 25%. RI = 50 - (200 × 12%) = ₹26L. If NOPAT = ₹45L, EVA = 45 - (200 × 10%) = ₹25L.

Quick Revision Sheet

📖 One-Liners
  • MCS = Process of influencing members to implement strategies (Anthony & Govindarajan)
  • POSDCORB = Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing, Controlling, Reporting, Budgeting
  • ARP = Action, Results, Personnel — Merchant's 3 control types
  • CRPI = Cost → Revenue → Profit → Investment centres (increasing scope)
  • ROI = Profit/Investment; RI considers cost of capital; EVA = NOPAT - (WACC × Capital)
  • Transfer pricing = Market, Cost-based, Negotiated — must achieve goal congruence
  • BSC = Financial + Customer + Internal Process + Learning & Growth (Kaplan & Norton)
  • TQM = continuous quality; JIT = zero inventory; ABC = activity-based costs
  • Service orgs have no inventory buffer — quality hard to measure; need different metrics
  • MNCs face currency risk + cultural differences + regulations + political risk
  • Projects: PERT (probabilistic, 3 estimates) vs CPM (deterministic, 1 estimate)
  • SMEs use informal controls; Knowledge orgs manage intellectual capital
  • Cybernetic loop = Standard → Measure → Compare → Correct (SMAC mnemonic)
  • ZBB forces justification from zero; Rolling budgets continuously update
