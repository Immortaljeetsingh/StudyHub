Point 1: Definition of Management

  • Mary Parker Follett: "The art of getting things done through the efforts of others"
  • Harold Koontz: "Management is the art of getting things done through and with people in formally organized groups"
  • Peter Drucker: "Management is a multi-purpose organ that manages a business, manages managers, and manages workers and work"
  • Organized group efforts to achieve organizational objectives efficiently and effectively
  • Focuses on managing people, resources, and processes to create value
⚠ Exam Tip

Always distinguish between efficiency (doing things right — minimizing resources) and effectiveness (doing the right things — achieving goals). Management needs BOTH.

Point 2: Nature of Management

🔬 Management as Science
  • Systematic body of knowledge with principles
  • Testable and verifiable in practice
  • Cause-and-effect relationships (e.g., motivation → productivity)
  • Universal principles apply across organizations
🎨 Management as Art
  • Personal application of skills and intuition
  • Creative problem-solving in real situations
  • Practice makes perfect — experience matters
  • Each manager has a unique style
💡 Indian Example

Infosys: Narayana Murthy applied scientific management principles (process metrics, CMM Level 5 certification) while also using artistic leadership (building a culture of integrity). This dual nature made Infosys a global IT giant.

Professional Perspective: Management is increasingly recognized as a profession with codified knowledge, formal education (MBA programs), ethical codes (like medical or legal professions), and professional associations (AIMA in India).

Point 3: Characteristics of Management

Group ActivityUniversalContinuousIntegratedProcess-drivenObjective-drivenSkill-basedFunctionalDiscipline
  1. Group Activity: Integrates efforts through organized groups; no management without a group
  2. Universal: Applies to ALL organizations — business, government, NGOs, hospitals, schools
  3. Continuous: Dynamic, ever-changing process; never a one-time activity
  4. Integrated Effort: Harmonizes human + material + financial + informational resources
  5. Process: Involves planning, organizing, directing, and controlling (P-O-S-D-C)
  6. Objective-driven: Every managerial action aims toward specific organizational goals
  7. Roles & Skills: Requires technical, human, and conceptual skills at different levels
  8. Function: POSDCORB (Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing, Coordinating, Reporting, Budgeting)
  9. Discipline: Recognized body of knowledge taught in universities worldwide
🧠 Memory Aid

Gulick & Urwick (1937) coined this acronym for the 7 functions of management.

Fayol's 14 Principles of Management

  • 1. Division of Work — Specialization increases efficiency
  • 2. Authority & Responsibility — Right to give orders + accountability
  • 3. Discipline — Obedience and respect for agreements
  • 4. Unity of Command — One subordinate, one boss

  • 11. Equity — Fair treatment with kindness
  • 12. Stability of Tenure — Minimize turnover
  • 13. Initiative — Encourage new ideas
  • 14. Esprit de Corps — Team spirit and harmony

Point 4: Weber's Bureaucracy (1920s)

Max Weber — German sociologist who proposed bureaucracy as the most rational form of organization.

Formal RulesHierarchyImpersonalDivision of LaborMerit-based
  • Authority Hierarchy: Traditional → Charismatic → Legal-Rational (most modern)
  • Formal Rules: Written procedures ensure consistency and predictability
  • Impersonal Relationships: Decisions based on rules, not personal favoritism
  • Career Orientation: Employment based on qualifications, not connections
⚠ Exam Tip

Weber's bureaucracy is criticized for red tape, inflexibility, and resistance to change, but it remains the foundation of government organizations (IAS, IPS, Indian Railways) and large corporations.

Point 6: Hawthorne Studies (1924-1932)

Phase 1
Illumination Experiments

Productivity changed regardless of light levels — workers responded to being observed (Hawthorne Effect)

Phase 2
Relay Assembly Test

Small group with rest breaks and friendly supervision produced more output

Phase 3
Bank Wiring Room

Informal group norms and social pressure affected output more than financial incentives

Key Finding: Social and psychological factors (group dynamics, attention, feeling valued) influence productivity as much as or more than physical conditions and financial incentives. This birthed the Human Relations Movement.

💡 Indian Example

Godrej: The Godrej Group applies Hawthorne insights through employee welfare programs, open communication, and participative management. Their employee-centric culture has resulted in extremely low attrition rates compared to industry averages.

📐 Case Study: Maruti Suzuki — Blending Classical & Human Relations

Maruti Suzuki combines Taylor's scientific management (45-second car assembly, time-motion studies, incentive pay) with Hawthorne-inspired human relations (worker suggestion schemes, team-based rewards, canteen subsidies, annual family days). Result: India's #1 carmaker with 40%+ market share and among the highest productivity per worker in the global auto industry. The lesson? Neither classical nor behavioral approach alone is sufficient — the best companies blend both.

Point 1: Mintzberg's 10 Managerial Roles

Henry Mintzberg observed actual managers and identified 10 roles grouped into 3 categories:

👑 Interpersonal Roles (3)
  • Figurehead — Symbolic duties (signing documents, receiving guests, attending ceremonies)
  • Leader — Motivating, directing, developing subordinates
  • Liaison — Building external contacts and networks
👁 Informational Roles (3)
  • Monitor — Scanning environment for information
  • Disseminator — Sharing information with team
  • Spokesperson — Representing org to outsiders
💡 Decisional Roles (4)
  • Entrepreneur — Initiating change and innovation
  • Disturbance Handler — Managing crises and conflicts
  • Resource Allocator — Distributing budgets, people, time
  • Negotiator — Bargaining with stakeholders
💡 Indian Example

Sundar Pichai (Google/Alphabet CEO):
• Figurehead: Represents Google at global forums
• Leader: Guides 180,000+ employees worldwide
• Monitor: Tracks AI, cloud, and search trends
• Entrepreneur: Drove Google's AI-first strategy
• Negotiator: Handled EU antitrust cases

At Infosys, Narayana Murthy exemplified all 10 Mintzberg roles. As figurehead, he became the face of Indian IT globally. Point 5: Decision Making in Planning

Rational ModelBounded RationalityIntuitiveGroup Decision
  • Rational: Complete information, evaluate all alternatives, choose optimal (ideal but rare)
  • Bounded Rationality (Herbert Simon): Managers "satisfice" — find good-enough solutions due to limited info, time, cognitive capacity
  • Intuitive: Based on experience and gut feeling (useful in crisis situations)
  • Group Decision: Brainstorming, Delphi technique, nominal group technique

Herbert Simon (Nobel Prize 1978): Managers do NOT optimize; they satisfice because they face bounded rationality — limited information, limited time, and limited cognitive ability.

✅ Unit 4 Summary

  • Planning bridges present to future; it's the foundation of all other functions
  • 3 types: Strategic (top, long-term), Tactical (middle, medium), Operational (lower, short)
  • 6-step process: Objectives → Premises → Alternatives → Evaluate → Implement → Monitor
  • MBO: Participative goal-setting with SMART goals
  • Decision making: Rational, Bounded, Intuitive, Group

Unit 5: Organizing

📖 Unit 5: Organizing

Organizing creates the structure through which activities are coordinated and resources are deployed to achieve objectives.

▶
Organization Structure Types
▶
Centralization vs Decentralization

Point 1: Definition and Purpose

  • Creating a structure with defined lines of authority and responsibility
  • Allocation of resources, monitoring progress, coordination across departments
  • "Organizing is the process of arranging people and resources to work toward a goal" — Chester Barnard

Point 2: Principles of Organization

  • Unity of Command: One subordinate reports to one boss
  • Unity of Objective: All activities serve common goals
  • Scalar Chain: Clear line of authority from top to bottom
  • Span of Control: Reasonable number of subordinates per manager (5-8 optimal)
  • Balance: Authority must equal Responsibility
  • Division of Work: Specialization increases efficiency
  • Delegation: Authority flows down; accountability stays up
  • Coordination: Integrating activities across departments

Point 4: Delegation and Decentralization

⬇ Delegation
  • Manager assigns authority AND responsibility to subordinate
  • Accountability CANNOT be delegated — manager remains answerable
  • Three elements: Authority, Responsibility, Accountability
⬆ Decentralization
  • Systematic delegation to lower levels
  • Faster decisions, better local responsiveness
  • Develops managerial talent at lower levels
💡 Indian Example

Zomato: Deepinder Goyal delegates to vertical heads (delivery, dining, Hyperpure, Blinkit) with clear authority and KPIs. Each head has autonomy but is accountable for their unit's performance.

Controlling ensures that organizational activities conform to plans. It's the last function that feeds back into planning, completing the management cycle.

Point 1: Definition and Purpose

  • Systematic activities to measure actual performance against standards and correct deviations
  • "Controlling is measuring and correcting performance of subordinates to ensure that enterprise objectives and plans are being accomplished" — Koontz
  • Ensures plans are implemented; identifies problems early; provides feedback for future planning

Point 3: Types of Control

🛡 Feedforward Control

Preventive control BEFORE action begins. Example: Quality checks on raw materials, training before project launch, pre-employment screening.

⏱ Concurrent Control

Real-time monitoring DURING activities. Example: Manager watching production line, GPS tracking deliveries, live dashboards.

🔄 Feedback Control

Post-action evaluation AFTER completion. Example: Annual reports, customer surveys, project post-mortems, financial audits. Most widely used type.

📐 Case Study: Tata Steel — World-Class Controlling

Tata Steel's Jamshedpur plant uses a triple-layer control system. Feedforward: Raw material testing before entering the blast furnace (iron ore quality, coal grade). Concurrent: Real-time temperature monitoring in furnaces, AI-powered defect detection on the rolling mill. Feedback: Monthly quality audits, customer satisfaction surveys, Six Sigma project reviews. This system helped Tata Steel win the Deming Prize (2008) and maintain <0.5% defect rate in automotive steel — among the best in the world.

⚠ Exam Tip

A common exam question: "Why is controlling called a backward-looking function?" Answer: Because it measures PAST performance against standards. However, feedforward control is forward-looking — it prevents problems BEFORE they occur. Always mention this distinction!

✅ Unit 7 Summary

  • Control = Set Standards → Measure → Compare → Correct
  • 3 types: Feedforward (before), Concurrent (during), Feedback (after)
  • Techniques: Budgets, ratios, audits, reports, break-even
  • Good controls are: timely, forward-looking, realistic, economical, flexible
