Q1. (a) Define sales management and explain the evolution and modern scope of a sales manager. (10 marks)

Answer: Sales management is the planning, direction and control of personal selling activities, and today's sales manager is a strategist coordinating people, data and channels, not just a field supervisor.

According to the American Marketing Association, sales management is "the planning, direction, and control of the personal selling activities of a business unit". It includes recruiting, selecting, training, equipping, assigning, routing, supervising, paying, and motivating the sales force, summarised by the PRE-ASSEMP framework (Planning, Recruiting, Equipping, Assigning, Supervising, Selecting, Evaluating, Motivating, Paying). Sales management operates at three levels: top-level strategic planning, middle-level tactical coordination, and first-level operational supervision. Selling has evolved from the pre-industrial era, when a single person performed every function, through the industrial era of separate sales departments and order-takers, to the modern era of consultative, technology-enabled selling focused on solving customer problems. Today's sales manager is not merely a supervisor: the role covers strategic planning, forecasting, budgeting, territory design, CRM management, and coordination with marketing, finance, and supply chain. A good example is HUL, whose 80,000-plus sales personnel reach 8 million retail outlets across 600,000 villages, making its field force one of the largest distribution networks in India.

(b) Selling is product-focused and short-term: it converts existing products into orders. Marketing is customer-focused and long-term: it studies needs and builds demand. Selling is only one part of the broader marketing function; marketing creates the pull, selling converts it into revenue.

Key points to remember:

  • Sales management = planning, direction, control of personal selling.
  • PRE-ASSEMP covers all sales manager activities.
  • Evolution: pre-industrial → industrial → consultative, tech-enabled selling.
  • Selling is short-term and product-focused; marketing is long-term and customer-focused.

Takeaway: The modern sales manager is a strategist coordinating people, data and channels, not just a field supervisor.

Q2. (a) Describe the recruitment and selection process for a sales force. (10 marks)

Answer: Recruitment attracts a pool of applicants while selection chooses the best candidate through job analysis, sourcing, screening, tests, interviews, references and the offer.

Recruitment is the process of attracting a pool of qualified applicants for sales positions; selection is the process of choosing the most suitable candidate from that pool. A bad hire is expensive - turnover can cost 1.5 to 2 times the annual salary in rehiring and training. The process begins with job analysis to prepare the job description and job specification, which list the duties and the skills, traits, and personality required for selling. Next, the company sources applicants from internal and external sources, then screens applications and resumes to shortlist candidates. The shortlisted candidates appear for employment tests (aptitude, personality, and sales-skills tests) followed by interviews, often a panel interview with a sales manager and an HR manager. Reference checks verify past performance and character, a medical examination confirms fitness, and finally the offer letter is issued. Many Indian companies add a field trial or shadowing day to observe how the candidate handles real customers. ICICI Bank, for example, uses a structured multi-stage process combining aptitude tests, group discussions, and panel interviews to build its large direct-sales team, ensuring every new recruit has the right skills, motivation, and cultural fit.

(b) Internal sources are promotions and transfers of existing employees - they are cheaper, faster, and boost morale, but limit the pool and create vacancies. External sources - campus placements, employment agencies, advertisements, and referrals - bring fresh talent and wider choice, but cost more and take longer.

Key points to remember:

  • Recruitment attracts applicants; selection chooses the best candidate.
  • Process: job analysis → sourcing → screening → tests → interview → references → offer.
  • Turnover costs are 1.5-2x annual salary, so selection matters.
  • Internal sources are cheap and motivating; external sources bring fresh talent.

Takeaway: A structured multi-stage selection process pays off because a bad hire can cost 1.5 to 2 times the annual salary.

Q3. (a) Explain the importance of sales training and the ADDIE model of training design. (10 marks)

Answer: Sales training builds selling skills, product knowledge and customer relationships, and the ADDIE model - Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation - structures the entire training design.

Sales training develops selling skills, product knowledge, and customer relationship abilities in sales personnel. It covers orientation training for new hires, ongoing skill development for experienced representatives, and management training for those moving into supervisory roles. Training is important because well-trained sales forces outperform untrained ones by 20-30 per cent in revenue generation, reduce errors and complaints, and lower turnover by building confidence. The ADDIE model is the standard framework for designing training. Analysis identifies the skill gaps and training needs of the sales force. Design sets the learning objectives and outlines the content, methods, and duration. Development creates the actual materials - manuals, presentations, case studies, and role-play scripts. Implementation delivers the training through classroom sessions, on-the-job coaching, or e-learning. Evaluation measures results against objectives - through tests, supervisor feedback, and sales performance data - and feeds improvements back into the next cycle. Maruti Suzuki runs one of India's most famous dealer-training systems, using ADDIE-style modules to keep its showroom staff and service teams consistently skilled across thousands of dealerships nationwide.

(b) Training methods divide into on-the-job and off-the-job. On-the-job methods include coaching by the supervisor, job rotation, and field shadowing with senior salespeople - they are practical and low-cost. Off-the-job methods include classroom lectures, role plays, case studies, films, and e-learning - they are structured and allow mistakes without losing customers.

Key points to remember:

  • Training builds selling skills, product knowledge, and relationships.
  • Well-trained sales forces outperform untrained ones by 20-30%.
  • ADDIE = Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation.
  • On-the-job (coaching, rotation) vs off-the-job (classroom, role play, e-learning).

Takeaway: Well-trained sales forces outperform untrained ones by 20-30 per cent, so systematic training is an investment, not a cost.

Q4. (a) Explain the components and types of sales compensation plans. (10 marks)

Answer: Sales compensation combines direct financial pay with non-monetary rewards, and the three plan types - straight salary, straight commission and combination - trade off security against motivation.

Sales compensation is the total package of monetary and non-monetary rewards given to sales personnel for their contribution. The plan must balance three objectives: attracting talent through competitive pay, motivating performance through incentives, and controlling costs through affordability. Compensation has two components: direct financial pay (salary, commission, bonus, and allowances such as travel and conveyance) and indirect and non-monetary rewards (insurance, car or phone, recognition awards, and promotion opportunities). The three classic plan types are straight salary, straight commission, and combination plans. Straight salary gives a fixed amount at regular intervals - it provides security and easy administration and is used for consultative or long-cycle selling, but it gives little incentive for extra effort. Straight commission pays a percentage of sales or profit generated - it maximises motivation and attracts aggressive sellers, but income is uneven and insecure, and sellers may pressurise customers for short-term gains. The combination plan pays a base salary plus commission, bonus, or both - it balances security and motivation and is the most widely used. Indian FMCG companies typically pay field staff a base salary with monthly and quarterly incentives, while insurance and real estate agents often work on pure commission.

(b) Straight salary gives security and control but weak motivation; straight commission gives maximum motivation but uneven income and risk; the combination plan merges the two - a salary floor with an incentive upside - and is therefore the most popular and balanced choice for most industries.

Key points to remember:

  • Compensation must balance attract, motivate, and control costs.
  • Three plans: straight salary, straight commission, combination.
  • Combination plan is the most widely used.
  • Commission plans risk pressuring customers for short-term gains.

Takeaway: The combination plan is the most widely used because it merges the security of salary with the motivation of commission.

Q5. (a) Compare qualitative and quantitative methods of sales forecasting with examples. (10 marks)

Answer: Qualitative methods use judgement - executive jury, Delphi, sales force composite, surveys - while quantitative methods use historical data - time series, moving averages, exponential smoothing and regression.

Sales forecasting estimates future revenue and is the foundation of production, inventory, staffing, and budgeting decisions. Qualitative (judgment-based) methods are used when historical data is limited, such as for new products: the jury of executive opinion pools senior managers' estimates, as Tata Motors does for new car models; the Delphi method uses iterative expert surveys until consensus; the sales force composite aggregates territory-level estimates from frontline salespeople, used by HUL, ITC, and Maruti; and customer surveys directly ask buyers about purchase intentions, common in B2B selling at L&T and BHEL. Quantitative (data-based) methods use historical data: time series analysis identifies trend, seasonal, cyclical, and random patterns - Maruti uses ten years of sales data to predict monthly demand; the moving average smooths short-term fluctuations; exponential smoothing weights recent data more heavily, as Flipkart does for demand prediction; and regression analysis relates sales to variables such as GDP or advertising spend. Best practice combines both approaches: Amul forecasts demand for 50 lakh litres of milk daily using historical data, festival seasonality (ice-cream demand rises 30-40 per cent in summer), and real-time village cooperative data, enabling collection and distribution within 24 hours.

(b) Forecasting matters because every plan depends on it: accurate estimates prevent stockouts and lost sales, avoid overproduction and excess inventory, guide staffing and territory decisions, support budgeting and pricing, and give the organisation early warning of market shifts.

Key points to remember:

  • Qualitative methods: jury of executive opinion, Delphi, sales force composite, surveys.
  • Quantitative methods: time series, moving average, exponential smoothing, regression.
  • Best practice combines both approaches (Amul's daily milk forecast).
  • Forecasting drives production, inventory, staffing, and budgeting.

Takeaway: Combining both approaches gives the most reliable forecast, as Amul's daily 50-lakh-litre milk planning shows.

Q6. (a) Explain the seven steps of the personal selling process with examples. (10 marks)

Answer: Personal selling is face-to-face selling that follows seven systematic steps - prospecting, pre-approach, approach, presentation, objection handling, closing and follow-up.

Personal selling is direct, face-to-face communication with a prospect to make a sale, and it follows seven systematic steps. (1) Prospecting - identifying potential customers through referrals, cold calling, or trade shows; Byju's generates leads through free scholarship tests. (2) Pre-approach - researching the prospect before meeting, including company, needs, decision-makers, and budget. (3) Approach - making the first contact and a good impression; Eureka Forbes offers a free water-testing kit rather than a sales pitch. (4) Presentation - demonstrating features, advantages, and benefits (FAB); Tata Motors uses test drives. (5) Handling objections - addressing concerns about price, quality, or timing using techniques like feel-felt-found; this is the most critical skill for Indian salespeople. (6) Closing - asking for the order through assumptive, alternative, or urgency closes; HDFC Bank executives ask "Shall I start the documentation?" (7) Follow-up - ensuring satisfaction through delivery confirmation, installation, and periodic check-ins. The AIDAS model - Attention, Interest, Desire, Action, Satisfaction - describes the buyer's journey through these stages, and Eureka Forbes's 15,000-plus direct sellers, generating sales of over Rs 3,000 crore annually, exemplify the complete process in India.

(b) BATNA stands for Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement - the negotiator's best fallback if talks fail. A strong BATNA gives leverage and confidence; a weak one forces acceptance of poor terms. A Maruti dealer negotiating with a fleet buyer, for example, has the BATNA of selling those cars to individual customers at higher margins.

Key points to remember:

  • Seven steps: prospecting, pre-approach, approach, presentation, objections, closing, follow-up.
  • Objection handling and closing are the most critical selling skills.
  • AIDAS maps the buyer's journey: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action, Satisfaction.
  • BATNA = best fallback option; strong BATNA means negotiation leverage.

Takeaway: The AIDAS journey - Attention, Interest, Desire, Action, Satisfaction - shows that selling is a systematic process, not a one-shot pitch.
