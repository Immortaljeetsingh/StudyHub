Q1. “It is said that the overall aim for logistics is to achieve high customer satisfaction or perceived product value. This must be achieved with acceptable costs.” How would you find the best balance? Discuss.

Answer: The best balance of customer service and logistics cost is found through cost-service trade-off analysis - defining service standards per customer segment and picking the service level where total logistics cost is lowest or profit is highest.

Logistics exists to deliver the right product, in the right quantity, at the right place, in the right condition, at the right time, at the right cost. The tension is that customer satisfaction almost always costs money: faster delivery needs more trucks and warehouses, higher service levels need more safety stock, and better packaging costs more. The answer is not “maximum service at any cost” but the optimum balance - the point where the extra rupee spent on service brings at least a rupee of benefit in revenue or savings.

You find that balance through cost-service trade-off analysis. First, define service standards for each customer segment, because a premium segment may justify express delivery while a price-sensitive segment needs only economical delivery. Second, identify the relevant logistics cost buckets - transportation, warehousing, inventory, order processing, packaging and administration - and see how each responds to a change in service level. Third, compute total logistics cost at different service levels and pick the level where total cost is lowest for the required service, or where profitability is highest. For example, offering free next-day delivery for all orders may push transportation and inventory costs so high that profits vanish; a hybrid model - free standard delivery, paid express - finds the balance. Companies like Amazon India and Flipkart use this logic to set free-delivery thresholds and delivery SLAs by pin code.

Other balancing tools from the syllabus are: the cost-time trade-off (faster modes cost more), inventory-availability trade-off (more safety stock improves fill rate but raises holding cost), and the Total Cost of Ownership approach, which looks at the whole chain rather than a single cheapest option. Indian Railways lowering freight rates for containerised traffic and e-commerce firms locating warehouses in Tier-2 cities near customers are practical examples of balancing cost with service.

Key points to remember:

  • Goal = maximum service at acceptable cost, not minimum cost alone.
  • Use cost-service trade-off analysis to find the optimum service level.
  • Balance transportation, warehousing, inventory and order-processing costs.
  • Segment customers; premium service for premium segments.

Takeaway: Balance = the service level where the marginal rupee of service buys at least a rupee of benefit; cite the free-delivery-threshold example.

Q2. Discuss the factors contributing to the introduction of e-SCM. Also, describe challenges and advantages while adopting e-SCM.

Answer: e-SCM was driven by customer expectations, cheap internet, globalisation, cost pressure and e-commerce competition; it gives real-time visibility and speed but faces investment, integration, security, skills and change-resistance challenges.

e-SCM (electronic Supply Chain Management) is the application of internet-based technologies to plan, execute and coordinate supply chain activities electronically. Several factors pushed companies into e-SCM. Customer expectations rose - buyers now expect online order tracking, fast home delivery and easy returns. Internet and communication technology became cheap and universal, making real-time data sharing possible across partners. Globalisation scattered suppliers and markets across continents, so manual coordination became too slow. Cost pressure forced firms to cut inventory and lead times through better information sharing, and competition from online-first players (Amazon, Flipkart, BigBasket) forced traditional firms to go digital.

The advantages of e-SCM are powerful: real-time visibility of orders, inventory and shipments; lower transaction and paperwork costs; faster and more accurate information flow, which reduces the bullwhip effect; better collaboration and planning with suppliers and distributors; and improved customer service through tracking and faster response. Delhivery and Blue Dart, for example, give clients live shipment visibility through web dashboards and APIs.

The challenges are equally real: high investment in software, integration and infrastructure; integration difficulty because legacy systems of different partners do not talk to each other; data security and privacy risks when sensitive commercial data crosses company boundaries; need for skilled manpower and process re-engineering; and resistance to change from employees and small suppliers who are not digitally ready. Adoption works best in phases - start with e-procurement and order tracking, then extend to collaborative planning with key partners.

Key points to remember:

  • e-SCM = internet technology across the whole supply chain.
  • Drivers: customer expectations, cheap internet, globalisation, cost pressure, e-commerce competition.
  • Advantages: visibility, speed, lower costs, less bullwhip, better service.
  • Challenges: cost, integration, security, skills, change resistance.

Takeaway: Answer in three moves - drivers, advantages, challenges - and close with a phased adoption plan.

Q3. What were the reasons for the fall of management accounting? Explain activity based costing and mention the benefits it had over the management accounting.

Answer: Traditional management accounting failed because volume-based drivers distorted costs in automated, high-variety plants; Activity Based Costing fixes this by tracing costs through activities, cost pools and cost drivers to give accurate product costs.

Traditional management accounting assigned overheads to products using simple volume-based drivers such as direct labour hours or machine hours. It failed for several reasons. As production became automated, direct labour shrank while overheads grew, so labour hours no longer explained costs. Product variety increased, and complex, low-volume products consumed far more support resources (engineering changes, small orders, special handling) than simple high-volume ones, yet were charged too little. Cost distortions led managers to make wrong pricing, product-mix and outsourcing decisions. In short, the old system reported costs that did not reflect how resources were actually consumed - the “fall” of management accounting as a reliable decision tool.

Activity Based Costing (ABC) fixes this by tracing costs through activities. The steps are:

  • Identify key activities - e.g., order processing, machine setup, quality inspection, delivery.
  • Assign overhead costs to each activity (cost pools).
  • Choose a cost driver for each activity - e.g., number of orders, number of setups, number of inspections.
  • Compute a rate per driver: activity cost ÷ total driver volume.
  • Charge each product with the activities it actually consumes.
Worked example: A factory has inspection overhead of Rs 4,00,000 and inspects 2,000 lots a year, so the rate is Rs 4,00,000 ÷ 2,000 = Rs 200 per inspection. Product A uses 400 inspections = Rs 80,000; product B uses 1,600 inspections = Rs 3,20,000. High-volume product B is now correctly charged four times the inspection cost of A - something volume-based costing would have missed.

Benefits over traditional costing: accurate product costs, visibility of which products/customers are actually profitable, identification of non-value-added activities to eliminate, better pricing and product-mix decisions, and fairer cost control. Indian firms in engineering, FMCG and logistics use ABC to price complex, low-volume jobs correctly.

Key points to remember:

  • Old costing failed because volume-based drivers distorted costs for complex products.
  • ABC: activities → cost pools → cost drivers → accurate product costs.
  • Benefits: true profitability, better pricing, elimination of waste.
  • Numerical skill: rate = activity cost ÷ driver volume.

Takeaway: Explain why volume-based costing failed, then run the ABC steps with the worked inspection-rate example.

Q4. Discuss in detail about the supply chain performance measurement system. Highlight the similarities/dissimilarities in any two of these measures.

Answer: A supply chain performance measurement system tracks cost, time, quality, flexibility and asset measures across the whole chain; on-time delivery and perfect order rate are both customer-facing percentages, but OTD covers timing alone while the perfect order rate covers full, on-time, accurate and undamaged delivery.

A supply chain performance measurement system (SCPMS) is the set of metrics a firm uses to judge how well its supply chain is achieving its goals of cost, speed, quality, flexibility and service. Measurement matters because you cannot manage what you do not measure. A good system covers the whole chain - suppliers, internal operations and customers - and both financial and non-financial measures.

Typical measures are:

  • Cost measures: total logistics cost as a % of sales, cost per order, inventory carrying cost.
  • Time/response measures: order cycle time, lead time, on-time delivery, cash-to-cash cycle.
  • Quality measures: perfect order rate, damage rate, return rate.
  • Flexibility measures: ability to handle rush orders, change volumes and product mix.
  • Asset measures: inventory turnover, warehouse and fleet utilisation.

Frameworks in the syllabus include the SCOR model (Plan, Source, Make, Deliver, Return), balanced scorecard for supply chains, and benchmarking against best-in-class firms.

Similarities between any two measures: take On-time delivery (OTD) and Perfect order rate. Both are customer-facing service measures, both are expressed as percentages, both directly affect customer satisfaction and repeat business, both improve when planning and coordination improve, and both are reported by the same customer service teams. Dissimilarities: OTD measures only the timing dimension - whether the order arrived when promised - while the perfect order rate is a composite measure that checks delivery in full, on time, with correct documentation and undamaged condition, so it is stricter and always equal to or lower than OTD. A firm can have 95% OTD but only 80% perfect orders because of wrong items or damage. Thus OTD is a narrow, partial view; perfect order rate is a holistic view of service quality.

Key points to remember:

  • Measure the chain end to end: cost, time, quality, flexibility, assets.
  • SCOR model and balanced scorecard give structure to measurement.
  • OTD = timing only; perfect order = full + on-time + accurate + undamaged.
  • Good measurement feeds benchmarking and continuous improvement.

Takeaway: Compare OTD vs perfect order rate as narrow timing vs holistic service - that pair makes the similarities/dissimilarities part easy.

Q5. Is it true that subjective criteria in plant location models focus on long-run cost effects? If yes, why?

Answer: Yes - subjective criteria like labour climate, infrastructure quality and government policy shape long-run costs over the plant’s life, so factor rating combines weighted subjective criteria with the load-distance method’s transport cost side.

Yes, this is largely true. Plant location is a one-time, long-term, high-investment strategic decision - once a plant is built, it is very difficult and expensive to move. Location models use two kinds of criteria. Quantitative criteria (transportation cost, land cost, labour wage, taxes) can be measured directly. Subjective criteria cannot be measured in rupees easily but have long-run cost consequences: labour relations and union climate (strikes cost crores), availability of skilled manpower (affects training and quality costs), government policies and incentives (state subsidies, ease of doing business), infrastructure quality (power, roads, ports), proximity to markets and suppliers, community attitude and even climate and environmental rules.

The reason they focus on long-run cost effects is that these factors operate over the life of the plant. A cheap piece of land in a location with unreliable power and poor roads may look good initially but costs far more over 20 years in power outages, delayed shipments and lost customers. State incentives that appear in the short run may be withdrawn; labour climates and infrastructure, on the other hand, shape every year’s operating cost. This is why techniques like the factor rating method weight subjective factors and combine them with quantitative ones - each location is scored on weighted criteria like C-L-I-G-T (Customers, Labour, Infrastructure, Government, Tax). The long-run cost view is also why the syllabus pairs subjective factor rating with the load-distance method, which handles the quantifiable transport-cost side of the same decision.

Worked example (load-distance method): A firm must choose between sites X and Y to serve three demand points. Loads and distances from each site:

Point P1 (load 200): X = 10 km, Y = 5 km; Point P2 (load 300): X = 8 km, Y = 12 km; Point P3 (load 150): X = 4 km, Y = 6 km.

Site X: (200×10) + (300×8) + (150×4) = 2000 + 2400 + 600 = 5,000 load-km.

Site Y: (200×5) + (300×12) + (150×6) = 1000 + 3600 + 900 = 5,500 load-km.

Lower score wins, so Site X is better by transport distance cost.

Key points to remember:

  • Location is a long-term decision; subjective factors shape lifetime costs.
  • Factor rating scores weighted qualitative + quantitative criteria.
  • Load-distance = sum of (load × distance); choose the lowest.
  • Never decide on land price alone - consider the full life-cycle.

Takeaway: Split the answer: factor rating for subjective long-run criteria plus load-distance for transport cost - lowest load-km wins.

Q6. How can bulk freight carriers take advantage of technological innovations to streamline their supply chain?

Answer: Bulk freight carriers streamline their supply chains through tracking and telematics, RFID, data analytics and AI, terminal automation, digital freight platforms and intermodalism - raising asset utilisation and cutting empty running.

Bulk freight carriers - rail, coastal shipping, pipelines and large road fleets moving coal, cement, steel, fertiliser, grains and crude - handle huge volumes at low margins, so small efficiency gains create large savings. Technology helps them in several ways.

Tracking and telematics: GPS and IoT sensors on wagons, trucks and ships give real-time location and condition (temperature, pressure, damage), so dispatchers reroute around congestion and customers get live ETAs. Indian Railways’ GPS-enabled freight trains and FREIGHT operations information system (FOIS) cut wagon turnaround time. Automatic identification with RFID and barcodes speeds loading and unloading, and reduces documentation errors at every transshipment point.

Data analytics and AI: demand forecasting models match wagons to expected cargo, predictive maintenance predicts when wheelsets or engines will fail so maintenance is planned instead of reactive, and route optimisation software plans the cheapest, fastest paths. Automation and robotics at bulk terminals - automated stacking, conveyor loading, drone stockpile surveys in mines - cut dwell time and labour cost. Digital platforms and electronic documentation (e-way bills, digital freight exchanges like FreightBazaar and Vahak in India) match empty return legs with cargo, attack the empty-run problem, and replace paper documents with faster electronic ones.

Finally, intermodalism combined with technology lets bulk cargo move seamlessly between road, rail and sea in containers or wagons, with a single digital record following the load. The pay-offs are higher asset utilisation, lower empty running, shorter cycle times, better fuel efficiency and higher customer trust through visibility - exactly what a low-margin bulk carrier needs.

Key points to remember:

  • Track everything: GPS, IoT, RFID for visibility and faster turnaround.
  • Forecast and optimise with AI and analytics; maintain predictively.
  • Digital freight platforms cut empty running and paperwork.
  • Intermodal + digital records = seamless bulk movement.

Takeaway: Group the technologies as visibility (GPS/RFID), intelligence (AI and analytics), automation and platforms - one example each.

Q7. Short notes (any three of five)

Answer: Any three of the five short notes can be attempted - all five are given below so you can pick the ones you know best.

(a) Accurate Response (AR): Accurate Response is a supply chain planning approach that combines demand forecasting with mathematical optimisation to decide how much to produce, where, and in what product mix, so that capacity is used to maximise profit under uncertainty. Instead of building large safety stocks, the firm uses optimisation to balance risk: which orders to accept, how much of each product to make, and what delivery promises to give. Fashion and seasonal industries use it because demand is unpredictable and capacity limited. Benefits are better service with lower inventory; the limitation is that it needs good forecasts and powerful software. Indian apparel exporters use AR-style planning to commit to orders early while managing fabric and capacity constraints profitably.

Key points to remember:

  • AR = forecasting + optimisation of capacity and product mix.
  • Maximises profit under demand uncertainty.
  • Best for seasonal, unpredictable demand.

(b) e-SCM: e-SCM is the use of internet technologies - e-procurement, e-marketplaces, web-based order tracking, collaborative platforms - across the supply chain. It replaces paper and phone-based coordination with real-time digital information sharing between suppliers, manufacturers, distributors and customers. Advantages: lower transaction costs, speed, transparency, reduced bullwhip effect, and better collaboration. Challenges: integration of legacy systems, data security, investment cost and partner readiness. Examples in India include Flipkart’s and Amazon’s seller platforms, e-procurement portals of government departments (GeM), and logistics APIs of Delhivery. e-SCM turns the supply chain from a chain of separate firms into one connected network.

Key points to remember:

  • e-SCM = internet-based information sharing across partners.
  • Benefits: speed, low cost, visibility, less bullwhip.
  • Challenges: integration, security, partner readiness.

(c) Benchmarking in SCM: Benchmarking is comparing your supply chain processes, costs and performance with the best firms in the industry or world, to find gaps and learn improvements. Types: internal (against your own best unit), competitive (against direct rivals), functional (against best performers of a similar function in any industry) and generic (against world-class processes anywhere). The process is: identify what to benchmark, choose partners, collect and compare data, set targets, implement improvements, and review. Indian firms benchmark logistics cost as % of sales, order cycle time and fill rate against global leaders like Amazon or Procter & Gamble. Benchmarking is not copying - it is learning the practices behind the numbers and adapting them to your conditions.

Key points to remember:

  • Benchmarking = compare with best-in-class to close performance gaps.
  • Four types: internal, competitive, functional, generic.
  • Always follow up with implementation and review.

(d) Intermodalism in SCM: Intermodalism means moving a shipment in the same loading unit (container, swap body, trailer) using two or more transport modes - road, rail, sea, air - without handling the cargo itself at mode change. The goods stay in the container; only the carrier changes. Benefits: reduced handling cost and damage, faster transfers, door-to-door service, and economies of scale of each mode - rail for long hauls, road for first and last mile, sea for international movement. India’s Dedicated Freight Corridors, container trains from ports like Nhava Sheva to inland container depots (ICDs) in Delhi and Bengaluru, and the Gati Shakti initiative promote intermodalism. The key enablers are standardised containers, integrated terminals, single documentation and digital tracking. It is the backbone of modern global and Indian logistics.

Key points to remember:

  • Intermodal = same container, multiple modes, no cargo rehandling.
  • Benefits: lower cost and damage, faster transit, door-to-door.
  • Needs standard containers, integrated terminals, single docs.

(e) Hau Lee’s uncertainty framework: Hau Lee classified supply chains by two uncertainties: demand uncertainty (stable vs volatile) and supply uncertainty (stable vs evolving). The matrix gives four strategies: Efficient supply chains (stable demand + stable supply) - compete on cost, like grocery staples. Risk-hedging supply chains (stable demand + evolving supply) - pool resources and share risks, like oil companies. Responsive supply chains (volatile demand + stable supply) - build flexibility and speed, like fashion retail. Agile supply chains (volatile demand + evolving supply) - combine responsiveness with risk hedging, like semiconductor makers. The framework tells managers to design their chain to match the uncertainty they face instead of copying one generic model - high-volume stable products need cost focus, while new products need agility.

Key points to remember:

  • Two dimensions: demand uncertainty and supply uncertainty.
  • Four strategies: efficient, risk-hedging, responsive, agile.
  • Match chain design to your uncertainty profile.

Takeaway: Pick notes you can draw as you write - e-SCM (flow diagram) and intermodalism (mode chain) are the safest choices.
