Q1. Define a contract under Section 2(h) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 and explain the essentials of a valid contract under Section 10.

Answer: Under Section 2(h), a contract is an agreement enforceable by law. Every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract — enforceability by law is the distinguishing feature. Section 10 lays down the essentials of a valid contract: (1) Free consent — the parties must agree upon the same thing in the same sense, and consent must not be caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake, the five vitiating elements covered by Sections 15 to 22; (2) Competency of parties — under Section 11, parties must be major (18 years or above), of sound mind, and not disqualified by law from contracting; (3) Lawful consideration — under Section 2(d), consideration is the price of the promise; it need not be adequate but must be lawful; (4) Lawful object — the purpose of the agreement must not be illegal or immoral; and (5) The agreement must not be expressly declared void by law. The offer (Section 2(a)) must be communicated and certain, while acceptance (Section 2(b)) must be absolute and unqualified. Where performance becomes impossible after the contract is made, the doctrine of frustration under Section 56 discharges both parties — for example, where the house contracted to be sold is destroyed by fire before the sale.

Key points:

  • Under Section 2(h), a contract is an agreement enforceable by law.
  • Where performance becomes impossible after the contract is made, the doctrine of frustration under Section 56 discharges both parties — for example, where the house contracted to be sold is destroyed by fire before the sale.
  • Every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract — enforceability by law is the distinguishing feature.

Takeaway: Where performance becomes impossible after the contract is made, the doctrine of frustration under Section 56 discharges both parties — for example, where the house contracted to be sold is destroyed by fire before the sale.

Q2. Discuss the different types of partners under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 and explain the position of a minor in a partnership firm.

Answer: Under Section 4 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Its five essentials are: minimum two persons (maximum fifty under the Companies Act, 2013), an agreement, sharing of profits, a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all, and mutual agency. The Act recognises six types of partners: active or ostensible partners, who participate in management, have unlimited liability and must give public notice on retirement; sleeping or dormant partners, who only invest capital and need no notice; nominal partners, who lend only their name and are liable to third parties; partners in profits only, who share profits but not losses; sub-partners, who share the profits of a partner with no liability to the firm; and partners by estoppel, who are held out as partners and are liable to third parties. A minor cannot become a full partner but may be admitted to the benefits of partnership — he is entitled to a share of the profits and access to accounts, and his liability is limited to his share in the firm. On attaining majority he has six months to decide whether to become a partner; if he gives no notice, he is deemed to have become a partner.

Key points:

  • Under Section 4 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all.
  • Its five essentials are: minimum two persons (maximum fifty under the Companies Act, 2013), an agreement, sharing of profits, a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all, and mutual agency.
  • On attaining majority he has six months to decide whether to become a partner; if he gives no notice, he is deemed to have become a partner.

Takeaway: On attaining majority he has six months to decide whether to become a partner; if he gives no notice, he is deemed to have become a partner.

Q3. Explain the main features and objectives of FEMA, 1999. How does it differ from FERA, 1973?

Answer: The Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999 replaced the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA), 1973, marking a shift from the regulation of foreign exchange to its management. FEMA has 7 chapters and 49 sections, with 6 Rules made by the Central Government and 23 Regulations framed by the RBI. Its objective is to facilitate external trade and payments and to promote the orderly development and maintenance of the foreign exchange market in India. FEMA differs from FERA on four counts: FERA was restrictive while FEMA is liberal; FERA aimed at conservation of foreign exchange while FEMA aims at its management; contraventions under FERA were criminal offences, while under FEMA they are civil offences; and FERA presumed a person guilty until proven innocent, whereas FEMA presumes innocence. FEMA classifies transactions into capital account transactions, which alter assets or liabilities, such as FDI, FPI and external borrowing, and current account transactions, such as exports, imports and remittances, which are generally free. A person resident in India is one who stays 182 days or more in the preceding financial year or is registered or incorporated in India. The Enforcement Directorate investigates contraventions of FEMA.

Key points:

  • The Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999 replaced the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA), 1973, marking a shift from the regulation of foreign exchange to its management.
  • Its objective is to facilitate external trade and payments and to promote the orderly development and maintenance of the foreign exchange market in India.
  • FEMA has 7 chapters and 49 sections, with 6 Rules made by the Central Government and 23 Regulations framed by the RBI.

Takeaway: The Enforcement Directorate investigates contraventions of FEMA.

Q4. Explain the provisions of Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013 relating to Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR).

Answer: Under Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013, a company becomes eligible for CSR obligations if, in any financial year, its net worth is Rs. 500 crore or more, or its turnover is Rs. 1000 crore or more, or its net profit is Rs. 5 crore or more. Such companies must constitute a CSR committee of at least three directors, including one independent director, and must spend at least two per cent of the average net profits of the preceding three years on CSR activities. The activities are specified in Schedule VII of the Act and include education, healthcare, environment protection, rural development, slum area development, gender equality and women empowerment, contributions to PM funds, disaster management, protection of national heritage and welfare of the armed forces. Companies must disclose CSR spending and the reasons for non-spending in the Board's report. Global frameworks such as the UN Global Compact, ISO 26000 and GRI Standards guide responsible business practice. Reliance Industries is an Indian example, spending over Rs. 1000 crore annually on CSR through the Reliance Foundation on education, healthcare and rural development.
