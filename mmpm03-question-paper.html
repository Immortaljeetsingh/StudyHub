Q1(a). Explain the following terms with an example : Trademark/Logo, Skimming Pricing Strategy, Brand Associations, Brand Asset Valuator

Answer: These four terms - trademark, skimming pricing, brand associations and the Brand Asset Valuator - cover the legal, pricing, memory and measurement sides of branding.

Trademark/Logo - a legally protected distinctive sign, symbol or word that identifies a seller's goods or services and distinguishes them from competitors. Registration under the Trade Marks Act 1999 gives exclusive rights. Example: the Tata 'T' logo instantly signals trust, or the Amul girl identifies Amul's dairy products.

Skimming Pricing Strategy - setting a high initial price to recover costs from price-insensitive buyers, then lowering it gradually. Example: Apple launched the iPhone in India at a premium price and reduced it over time as competition from OnePlus and Samsung arrived.

Brand Associations - the mental links, images and meanings consumers attach to a brand in memory. Example: Maggi is associated with 2-minute noodles, quick cooking and childhood nostalgia, which is why its 2015 relaunch succeeded quickly.

Brand Asset Valuator (BAV) - a model developed by Young & Rubicam that measures brand health on four pillars: differentiation, relevance, esteem and knowledge. It shows whether a brand is new and growing (high differentiation, low knowledge) or mature and declining (low differentiation, high knowledge).

Key points to remember
  • A trademark is a legal asset; a logo is its visual expression.
  • Skimming = price high first, cut later; opposite of penetration pricing.
  • Associations live in consumer memory; BAV tracks brand health on 4 pillars.

Takeaway: Each term tackles a different side of branding - legal protection, pricing, memory and measurement.

Q1(b). Define what constitutes a new product and explain how the responsibility for New Product Development is assigned at the corporate level

Answer: A new product is anything new to the firm - six categories from inventions to cost-reduced versions - and NPD is owned at corporate level by committees, departments, venture teams, product managers or R&D.

A new product is any offering that is new to the firm, whether it is new to the world or simply a new addition to the company's range. New products include six categories: new-to-the-world inventions, new product lines (a company entering a category for the first time), additions to existing lines, improvements or revisions of existing products, repositioning of existing products, and cost-reduced versions. For the consumer, a product is 'new' if it is perceived as different from what was available earlier, while for the firm it is new if it has not been manufactured or marketed by it before. This broad definition matters because firms treat all six categories as innovation requiring investment and planning.

The responsibility for New Product Development (NPD) at corporate level is assigned through dedicated structures because NPD cuts across marketing, R&D, production and finance. Companies commonly use: a new product committee of senior executives that approves and funds projects; a new product department that coordinates the whole process; venture teams or skunk works with cross-functional members given freedom and a budget; product managers who handle incremental additions to their lines; and R&D departments that drive technology-based ideas. Corporate-level assignment ensures top-management commitment, adequate resources and a company-wide view of strategy, so that a new product idea is not blocked by the narrow interests of any single department.

Key points to remember
  • New product = new to the world OR new to the firm; six categories.
  • Corporate structures: committee, department, venture team, product manager, R&D.
  • Corporate-level assignment gives resources and cross-functional coordination.

Takeaway: Corporate-level ownership of NPD ensures resources, top-management commitment and cross-functional coordination.

Q2(a). Discuss the possible situations where generation of new product ideas occurs both internal as well as external to the firm

Answer: New product ideas arise from internal sources - R&D, employees, sales force, records - and external sources - customers, competitors, distributors, suppliers and crowdsourcing.

Internal sources of new product ideas arise inside the firm. R&D laboratories create ideas through research; 3M's Post-it notes began with a failed adhesive in the company's lab. Employees and the sales force suggest improvements because they meet customers daily; Marico encourages employee ideas for new variants of Parachute and Saffola. Company records, complaint data and the marketing department's own analysis of sales and competition also generate ideas, and top management may set strategic directions that trigger new projects.

External sources come from outside. Customers are the largest source - studies show many successful innovations come from user needs; HUL's Fair & Lovely and Dove products grew out of consumer research on skin concerns. Competitors' launches force response, as when telecom operators copied Reliance Jio's bundled data plans. Distributors, retailers, suppliers, consultants, advertising agencies, trade shows, and even government research institutes supply ideas. Modern firms also use crowdsourcing and social media listening, like Dabur running online contests for product suggestions.

Situations where generation typically occurs: when a firm launches a growth strategy, when a category matures and needs new variants, when technology changes (smartphone era), when competitors enter, and when consumer habits shift, such as the post-2020 demand for hygiene products.

Key points to remember
  • Internal: R&D, employees, sales force, company records.
  • External: customers, competitors, distributors, suppliers, crowdsourcing.
  • Ideas are generated continuously; screening comes next.

Takeaway: Idea generation must be continuous because later NPD stages can only work with a rich input flow.

Q2(b). What options of different appeals can a marketer use to reinforce a strong and positive perception for building a strong brand image ?

Answer: Marketers can use rational, emotional, fear, humour, celebrity and comparative appeals to reinforce a strong, positive brand perception.

A brand appeal is the emotional or rational hook used in communication to build a positive brand perception. The main options are:

  • Rational appeal: focuses on facts, features, performance and value. Example: V-Guard's ads stress durability and after-sales service of its voltage stabilizers.
  • Emotional appeal: touches feelings such as love, joy, pride, security and belonging. Example: Cadbury's 'Kuch Meetha Ho Jaaye' ads link the brand to celebration and family.
  • Fear appeal: warns of negative consequences to motivate action, common in insurance and health campaigns like Life Insurance Corporation's family-security ads.
  • Humor appeal: makes the brand memorable and likable, as used by Fevicol, whose funny ads are recalled for decades.
  • Celebrity appeal: transfers the celebrity's credibility and popularity to the brand, like Amitabh Bachchan endorsing Cadbury or Ranveer Singh for Fogg.
  • Comparative appeal: positions the brand against rivals, used by soft-drink and detergent brands.

Appeals reinforce a strong, positive perception when they are consistent over time, match the target segment, and are delivered repeatedly through advertising, packaging, sponsorship and digital media, so that the image gradually gets fixed in consumer memory.

Key points to remember
  • Appeals: rational, emotional, fear, humor, celebrity, comparative.
  • Consistency and repetition build the image over time.
  • The appeal must match the target segment's values.

Takeaway: Consistency, repetition and fit with the target segment turn an appeal into a lasting brand image.

Q3(a). List the brand building blocks and explain how important these blocks are in the consumer decision-making process

Answer: Keller's six brand building blocks - salience, performance, imagery, judgments, feelings and resonance - are arranged in a pyramid that carries consumers from awareness to loyalty.

Keller's Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE) model lists six brand building blocks arranged in a pyramid: brand salience, brand performance, brand imagery, brand judgments, brand feelings, and brand resonance. Salience answers 'who are you?' and covers awareness and recall; performance covers how the product works; imagery covers the user profile and personality; judgments cover quality and credibility; feelings cover emotions like warmth and excitement; and resonance is the final stage where consumers feel deep attachment and loyalty.

These blocks matter in consumer decision-making because they operate at every step. Salience decides whether the brand enters the consumer's consideration set at all - a brand that is not recalled is never evaluated. Performance and imagery are used in evaluating alternatives, so a brand like Titan wins because consumers judge its quality and status imagery higher than unknown watch brands. Judgments and feelings shorten the evaluation and purchase stage: trust built through consistent delivery reduces perceived risk, as with Tata or Amul. Finally, resonance drives repeat purchase, advocacy and immunity to competitors' offers, which is why Royal Enfield and Apple owners buy again and recommend the brand. In short, each block removes friction from the buying decision, moving the consumer from awareness to loyalty.

Key points to remember
  • Six blocks in order: salience, performance, imagery, judgments, feelings, resonance.
  • Blocks map to the decision process: awareness → evaluation → purchase → loyalty.
  • Resonance is the highest level - deep attachment and repeat buying.

Takeaway: Each building block removes friction from the buying decision, culminating in brand resonance.

Q3(b). What is brand hierarchy ? Comment on the different levels of brand hierarchy accessible for a marketer

Answer: Brand hierarchy arranges all of a firm's brands from the corporate brand down through family and individual brands to modifiers.

Brand hierarchy is the structured display of all brands and brand elements a firm markets, arranged from the top corporate brand down to the individual product and its modifiers. It shows the nesting relationship among brands, and it helps the firm decide how much branding emphasis to give each level. The typical hierarchy has four levels:

  • Corporate (company) brand: the top level, like Tata or Reliance, which lends overall trust and identity to everything below it.
  • Family brand: a brand used across a group of related products, like Tata Motors or Amul, which transfers recognition to multiple lines.
  • Individual brand: the brand of one specific product, like the Tata Nexon or Amul Kool, which allows distinct positioning within a family.
  • Modifier: the bottom level describing a version or variant, like Nexon EV, or Amul Kool Milk Shake; it differentiates size, flavour, technology or target segment.

A marketer can choose which levels to emphasize and how many to use. Emphasizing the corporate level builds umbrella equity (Tata's trust covers cars, salt and hotels); emphasizing individual brands allows each product to hold a distinct image and take risks, as HUL does with Lux, Lifebuoy and Dove; and combining levels through sub-brands or endorsed brands, like Cadbury Dairy Milk, lets a new product borrow equity while building its own. The hierarchy also guides brand extension, co-branding and the branding of new products introduced into the market.

Key points to remember
  • Levels: corporate brand → family brand → individual brand → modifier.
  • Corporate emphasis = umbrella equity; individual emphasis = distinct images.
  • Sub-brands and endorsed brands mix levels for extension.

Takeaway: Choosing which hierarchy levels to emphasise decides whether equity builds as umbrella trust or as distinct product images.

Q4. Write short notes on any three of the following

Answer: Short notes: services differentiate on people, process and physical evidence; display matrices map market gaps; NPD ideas flow from internal and external sources; branding commodities adds trust and premium pricing; composite measures index total brand strength.

(a) Services Differentiation - Services are intangible, so firms differentiate on people, process and physical evidence. Example: Taj Hotels differentiate through personalized hospitality; a bank differentiates through branch ambience, staff courtesy and fast queues. Differentiation builds competitive advantage where the core service itself is similar.

(b) Utility of Display Matrices - Display matrices map products or brands on two or more dimensions, like price and quality, so marketers can spot gaps and positioning problems. Example: plotting Indian telecom brands on coverage-versus-price shows where a new brand could position itself. They help spot gaps and position new products.

(c) Generation of New Product Ideas - Ideas come from internal sources (R&D, employees, sales force) and external sources (customers, competitors, distributors, suppliers, crowdsourcing). Example: HUL's consumer research on skin care generated its fairness and moisturizer ranges. Generation is the first stage of the NPD process and must be continuous.

(d) Advantages of Branding the Commodities - Branding commodities adds recognition, consistent quality assurance, premium pricing and customer loyalty. Example: Tata Salt, Daawat rice and Mother Dairy milk proved that even salt, rice and milk can be branded profitably in India; branding reduces price-based competition and builds trust.

(e) Composite Measure of Brand Equity - A single index that combines several indicators of brand strength, such as awareness, preference, loyalty, perceived quality and associations, often weighted together. Example: the Brand Equity Index used by agencies or the BAV pillars; a composite measure gives one number to track a brand's health over time and compare brands.

Key points to remember
  • The question asks for any three - choose the three you know best.
  • Each note needs a definition, 2-3 points and one Indian example.
  • Composite measures combine awareness, preference, loyalty and quality.

Takeaway: Each short note needs a definition, two or three points and one Indian example to score full marks.

Q5(a). Discuss the important criteria used to screen new product ideas. How would you use product profile rating on a new product idea ?

Answer: Screening evaluates ideas on strategic fit, market potential, financial viability, technical feasibility and resources, using weighted product-profile ratings against a cut-off score.

Screening is the second stage of NPD, where the many ideas generated are evaluated quickly and weak ones are dropped before heavy investment. The important screening criteria are: strategic fit with company mission and objectives; market potential in terms of size, growth and demand; financial viability covering expected sales, costs, profits and payback; technical feasibility of producing the product with available technology; legal and environmental acceptability; and availability of resources, skills and distribution. A product that fails any critical criterion is rejected at screening, which saves the company from expensive failures later.

Product profile rating is a formal screening tool. Steps: (1) list the important criteria, such as market size, growth, competition, technical feasibility, margin and strategic fit; (2) assign a weight to each criterion according to its importance, with weights totalling 100; (3) rate the new product idea on each criterion on a scale, say 1 to 10; (4) multiply each rating by its weight and add the scores to get a total; (5) compare the total against a cut-off score or the profiles of existing successful products. Illustration: if market size is weighted 30 and the idea scores 8, it contributes 240 points; competition weighted 25 scores 4, contributing 100; adding all contributions gives the total profile score, and the idea is approved only if it crosses the company cut-off, say 700 out of 1000.

Key points to remember
  • Criteria: strategic fit, market, finance, technology, resources.
  • Profile rating = weighted score: sum of (weight x rating).
  • Screening drops weak ideas early; it is fast and cheap.

Takeaway: Weighted profile rating turns subjective judgement into a comparable score against a company cut-off.

Q5(b). Comment on the need for brand valuation and the methods available for a marketer

Answer: Brand valuation estimates the financial worth of a brand as an intangible asset, using cost-based, market-based or income-based methods.

Brand valuation is the process of estimating the financial worth of a brand as an intangible asset. It is needed for several reasons: for mergers, acquisitions and joint ventures, where the brand is a large part of the purchase price (Tata paid heavily for the Jaguar Land Rover brands); for licensing and franchising, where royalty rates are fixed as a percentage of brand value; for balance-sheet reporting under intangible-asset accounting standards; for raising finance where lenders accept brand value as security; and for internal marketing decisions, so that spending on the brand is treated as investment rather than cost, and its return can be tracked.

The methods available fall into three groups. Cost-based methods value the brand by what it would cost to create or replace, such as historical cost or reproduction cost. Market-based methods compare the brand with similar brands actually sold, which is difficult because brand sales are rare. Income-based methods, the most accepted, capitalise the future earnings the brand generates: the price-premium method compares the brand's price with a generic product's; the royalty-relief method estimates the royalties the firm would pay if it did not own the brand; and discounted-cash-flow methods are used by Interbrand and Brand Finance, which project brand-driven earnings and discount them to present value. Each year Interbrand and Brand Finance publish global and Indian brand value rankings, and Indian brands such as Tata, Reliance and Airtel regularly feature, showing that brand value is a measurable, tradable asset.

Key points to remember
  • Valuation needed for M&A, licensing, balance sheet, finance, decisions.
  • Methods: cost-based, market-based, income-based (best).
  • Income methods: price premium, royalty relief, DCF.

Takeaway: Income-based methods are the most accepted because they capitalise the brand's future earnings.
