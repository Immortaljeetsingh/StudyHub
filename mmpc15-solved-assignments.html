Solved Assignment Questions & Answers - MMPC-15

Q1. Discuss the steps involved in the research process.

Answer: The research process follows a systematic sequence that converts a research problem into dependable findings. It begins with identifying and defining the research problem, which states the question or issue that needs investigation. Next, a literature review surveys existing published work to understand what is already known and to identify research gaps. Based on this, hypotheses are formulated: the null hypothesis (H₀) states that there is no effect or no difference, while the alternative hypothesis (H₁) states that an effect exists. The researcher then selects a research design, the master plan that specifies the methods and procedures for collecting and analysing data. Data is collected using appropriate techniques, then processed through editing, coding, classification, and tabulation before statistical analysis is performed. Findings are interpreted in light of the hypotheses, and conclusions are drawn. Finally, the complete study is written up and presented in a research report. Throughout the process, the researcher must avoid Type I errors (rejecting H₀ when it is true) and Type II errors (failing to reject H₀ when H₁ is true).

Key points:

  • The research process follows a systematic sequence that converts a research problem into dependable findings.
  • It begins with identifying and defining the research problem, which states the question or issue that needs investigation.
  • Throughout the process, the researcher must avoid Type I errors (rejecting H₀ when it is true) and Type II errors (failing to reject H₀ when H₁ is true).

Takeaway: Throughout the process, the researcher must avoid Type I errors (rejecting H₀ when it is true) and Type II errors (failing to reject H₀ when H₁ is true).

Q2. Explain exploratory, descriptive, and experimental research designs with examples.

Answer: Research designs are classified into three broad types. Exploratory research is used when the research problem is unclear or not fully understood; it helps generate ideas and hypotheses through literature searches, expert surveys, and pilot studies. Descriptive research describes the characteristics of a population or phenomenon; it answers questions such as how many or how much, for example, “60 per cent of customers prefer online shopping,” and typically uses surveys and case studies. Experimental research establishes cause-and-effect relationships by manipulating an independent variable and measuring its effect on a dependent variable, as in laboratory or field experiments, for example testing whether training improves productivity. A good design must satisfy validity requirements: internal validity is the degree of confidence that the independent variable caused the observed change in the dependent variable; external validity is the degree to which results can be generalised to other settings and populations; and construct validity asks whether we are measuring what we think we are measuring. Choosing the right design saves time and cost and ensures that the conclusions drawn from the study are credible and actionable for managers.

Key points:

  • Research designs are classified into three broad types.
  • Descriptive research describes the characteristics of a population or phenomenon; it answers questions such as how many or how much, for example, “60 per cent of customers prefer online shopping,” and typically uses surveys and case studies.
  • Exploratory research is used when the research problem is unclear or not fully understood; it helps generate ideas and hypotheses through literature searches, expert surveys, and pilot studies.

Takeaway: Choosing the right design saves time and cost and ensures that the conclusions drawn from the study are credible and actionable for managers.

Q3. Distinguish between probability and non-probability sampling designs.

Answer: Sampling is the process of selecting a subset of a population to study, rather than conducting a census of every element. Probability sampling gives every unit of the population a known, non-zero chance of being selected. It includes simple random sampling, where every unit has an equal chance of selection, as in a lottery; stratified sampling, where the population is divided into homogeneous strata and random samples are drawn from each; cluster sampling, where groups are selected randomly and all units within the chosen groups are studied, which suits geographically dispersed populations; and systematic sampling, where every nth unit is selected from a list. Non-probability sampling does not give all units a known chance of selection. It includes convenience sampling, which takes whoever is available and is useful for pilot studies; judgment sampling, where the researcher selects units based on expertise; quota sampling, which fixes the number of respondents from each category and is common in market research; and snowball sampling, which uses referrals from initial respondents to reach rare or hidden populations. Probability designs permit estimation of sampling error, the difference between a sample statistic and the population parameter.

Key points:

  • Sampling is the process of selecting a subset of a population to study, rather than conducting a census of every element.
  • Probability sampling gives every unit of the population a known, non-zero chance of being selected.
  • Probability designs permit estimation of sampling error, the difference between a sample statistic and the population parameter.

Takeaway: Probability designs permit estimation of sampling error, the difference between a sample statistic and the population parameter.

Q4. What are the major ethical issues in research, and how can a researcher avoid them?

Answer: Research ethics are the principles that keep a study honest and protect the people involved in it. The core values include honesty, which means reporting data truthfully without fabrication; integrity, which is consistency between words and actions; objectivity, which avoids bias in design, analysis, and interpretation; beneficence, which maximises benefits and minimises harm to participants; and confidentiality, which protects participant identity and data. Two procedural safeguards are essential: informed consent, which ensures participants know exactly what they are agreeing to before joining the study. The three major ethical violations to avoid are fabrication, which is making up data that does not exist; falsification, which is manipulating research materials, equipment, or processes; and plagiarism, which is using others’ work without proper attribution. A researcher can avoid these issues by maintaining transparent records, citing all sources correctly, pre-testing instruments to prevent data distortions, and seeking ethical review before data collection. Ethical lapses damage the credibility of the research, harm participants, and erode public trust in management research, so ethics must be upheld at every stage of the research process.

Key points:

  • Research ethics are the principles that keep a study honest and protect the people involved in it.
  • The core values include honesty, which means reporting data truthfully without fabrication; integrity, which is consistency between words and actions; objectivity, which avoids bias in design, analysis, and interpretation; beneficence, which maximises benefits and minimises harm to participants; and confidentiality, which protects participant identity and data.
  • Ethical lapses damage the credibility of the research, harm participants, and erode public trust in management research, so ethics must be upheld at every stage of the research process.
