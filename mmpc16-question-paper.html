Solved Answers - Model Answers for Every Question

Answer to Q1: Why Firms Go International and the Barriers They Face

Answer: Firms cross borders for four "seeking" motives — market, resource, efficiency and strategic asset — and then meet tariff, non-tariff, cultural, administrative, currency and political barriers that shape their entry strategy.

Key points:

  • Market seeking — reach new customers, follow clients abroad or escape a saturated home market; Dr Reddy’s entered the US and European generic-drug markets for higher margins
  • Resource seeking — access raw materials, cheap labour or natural resources; ONGC Videsh invested in oil fields in Russia, Vietnam and Mozambique for energy security
  • Efficiency seeking — cut costs through scale economies and factor-cost arbitrage; Infosys and TCS use global delivery centres to serve clients across time zones at lower cost
  • Strategic asset seeking — acquire brands, technology, patents or distribution; Tata bought Corus, JLR and Tetley; Bharat Forge acquired Carl Dan Peddinghaus for forging technology
  • Barriers: tariff (import duties, anti-dumping) and non-tariff (quotas, licensing, standards); cultural (language, religion, tastes); administrative (bureaucracy, corruption); currency risk; political risk (instability, expropriation)

Takeaway: MRES = Market, Resource, Efficiency, Strategic asset seeking — match the motive with the right entry mode and assess country risk before committing resources.

Answer to Q2: Globalization, Its Drivers and India's Journey

Answer: Globalization is the growing integration of world economies through freer trade, investment, technology and culture — driven by technology, liberalization, regional blocs, MNCs, finance and political change — and India’s 1991 LPG reforms are its most dramatic domestic chapter.

Key points:

  • Three waves: 1870–1914 (steamships, telegraph, colonialism); 1945–1980 (Bretton Woods, GATT, Cold-War blocs); 1980 to present (internet, container shipping, emerging markets)
  • Drivers: technology (ICT, containerization), trade liberalization (WTO and FTAs cut average tariffs from ~40% in 1947 to ~4% by 2020), regional integration (EU, ASEAN, USMCA), MNC activity (intra-firm trade ≈ 33% of world trade), financial liberalization, political change (end of Cold War, China 1978, India 1991)
  • India’s journey: pre-1991 License Raj and import substitution → LPG reforms → FDI up from ~$100 mn (1991) to ~$84 bn (2022); IT exports ~$194 bn; globalized IT, pharma and auto-components sectors, though a trade deficit and protectionist pressures remain
  • Glocalization = "think globally, act locally": McDonald’s serves McAloo Tikki in India, Teriyaki Burger in Japan, McArabia in the Middle East — one global brand, locally adapted menus

Takeaway: Drivers = Technology, Trade liberalization, Regional integration, MNCs, Finance, Political change; India is proof that 1991-style openness, not isolation, globalizes an economy.

Answer to Q3: PESTEL Analysis and Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions

Answer: PESTEL scans the six macro-environment forces — Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal — before entering a market, while Hofstede’s six cultural dimensions explain how management style must adapt to a country’s values.

Key points:

  • PESTEL applied to India: stable democracy but state-level policy variation (Political); fifth-largest GDP with 7%+ growth, fiscal-deficit concerns (Economic); 1.4 billion people, young median age, 300 million+ middle class (Social); UPI and Aadhaar digital revolution (Technological); renewable-energy targets but pollution challenges (Environmental); GST-based tax system with strong IP courts (Legal)
  • Hofstede’s six dimensions: power distance, individualism vs collectivism, masculinity vs femininity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term vs short-term orientation, indulgence vs restraint
  • India scores high on power distance (77) → hierarchy accepted; Japan high on uncertainty avoidance (92) → detailed contracts matter; China (87) and Japan (88) are long-term oriented → patient planning
  • Ignoring socio-cultural factors is costly — KFC faced heavy early resistance in India partly because local tastes and values were not anticipated

Takeaway: Use PESTEL to scan a market and Hofstede to manage within it — culture explains why the same strategy fails in one country and succeeds in another.

Answer to Q4: Dunning's OLI Framework and Stages of Economic Integration

Answer: FDI happens when Ownership, Location and Internalization advantages exist together — Tata’s JLR acquisition fits all three — and regional integration deepens through six stages from PTA to political union (PFCCEP).

Key points:

  • Ownership (O) = firm-specific assets: technology, brands, patents, management skills
  • Location (L) = country-specific factors: market size, resources, labour cost, policy incentives
  • Internalization (I) = keeping advantages in-house vs licensing, to protect IP and avoid transaction costs; O alone → export; O+I without L → licensing; all three → wholly-owned subsidiary
  • Tata–JLR case (2008, $2.3 bn): financial and managerial strength (O), UK premium brands and European market access (L), full acquisition protected brand equity (I); JLR revenue later grew from ~$10 bn to ~$28 bn
  • Integration stages (PFCCEP): Preferential Trade Area (India–SAARC) → Free Trade Area (USMCA) → Customs Union (MERCOSUR) → Common Market (EU pre-Maastricht) → Economic Union (EU + euro) → Political Union

Takeaway: OLI decides the mode (export, license or invest); PFCCEP decides the market’s depth — the EU at 27 members is the deepest bloc so far.

Answer to Q5: WTO Agreements, Principles and the Dispute Settlement Mechanism

Answer: The WTO (1995, successor to GATT) runs a rules-based trade system on three pillars — GATT, GATS, TRIPS — with MFN and National Treatment as its core principles, and a binding dispute settlement mechanism that makes its rulings stick.

Key points:

  • Three pillars (GGT): GATT (goods), GATS (services), TRIPS (intellectual property), plus agreements on agriculture, anti-dumping and subsidies
  • Key principles: Most Favoured Nation (equal treatment of all members), National Treatment (foreign goods treated like domestic after customs), free trade through negotiation, fair competition, predictability via bound tariffs, special treatment for developing countries
  • TRIPS: minimum 20-year patents, 50-year copyright, but flexibilities like compulsory licensing — India used it in 2012 for the cancer drug Nexavar and pushed a TRIPS waiver during COVID-19
  • Dispute settlement: consultation → panel → Appellate Body → implementation; rulings are binding with authorized retaliation for non-compliance; India has been complainant in 24 cases and respondent in 32 (won its steel case vs US 2014, lost the solar case 2016)
  • Trade creation = new efficient trade because a bloc shifts production to a lower-cost member; trade diversion = trade shifted from an efficient outsider to a less efficient member — creation is good, diversion is not

Takeaway: GGT + MFN + National Treatment is the framework; the binding dispute process is the WTO’s teeth — trade creation is welfare, trade diversion is a cost.

Answer to Q6: International Entry Modes and the EPRG Framework

Answer: Entry modes form a ladder of rising risk, control and commitment — from exporting to wholly-owned subsidiaries (ELJW) — while the EPRG framework classifies the firm’s marketing orientation as Ethnocentric, Polycentric, Region-centric or Geocentric.

Key points:

  • Exporting — low risk, low control, ideal for testing a market; Indian IT firms began by exporting software to the US
  • Licensing and franchising — sell IP or a business system for a fee; low investment, limited control; Godrej licensed FMCG brands in Africa
  • Management contracts and turnkey projects — export expertise; common in hotels and hospitals
  • Joint ventures — shared ownership and risk with a local partner; Tata Starbucks (50:50) and Maruti Suzuki (1982)
  • Wholly-owned subsidiaries (greenfield or acquisition) — full control at highest risk; Tata Steel–Corus ($12 bn, 2007), Tata–JLR
  • Choice depends on country attractiveness, country risk, FDI policy, timing (first vs late mover) and internal capabilities
  • EPRG: Ethnocentric (home approach everywhere — Patanjali); Polycentric (each country separate — Apollo Hospitals in the Gulf); Region-centric (regional approach — Unilever in South Asia); Geocentric (global integration + local responsiveness — Apple)

Takeaway: Entry modes = Export → License → JV → WOS (commitment buys control); EPRG = how global your mindset is — geocentric firms balance global scale with local taste.

June 2023 Real Paper8 questions · attempt any five · 20 marks each

Q1. 20

"Distinguish between domestic and international business and highlight the importance of international business."

Answer: Domestic business operates inside one country with one currency, one legal system and one culture, while international business crosses borders into multiple currencies, laws, cultures and environments — and it matters because it is the engine of firm growth and national development.

Key points:

  • Domestic: single currency, uniform laws/tax, known culture · International: multiple currencies, legal systems, languages, cultures
  • IB covers goods, services, capital, technology and knowledge moving across borders
  • Importance for firms: larger markets and scale economies, resource access, risk diversification, learning and technology
  • Importance for nations: exports and FDI create jobs, transfer technology, raise productivity — India’s IT services and pharma exports are direct evidence
  • Why it matters for India: 1991 LPG reforms opened the economy; FDI rose from ~$100 mn (1991) to ~$84 bn (2022)

Takeaway: Domestic is one ballgame, international is many — the four seeking motives (MRES) explain why firms leave home.

Q2. 20

"Briefly examine the key agreements of World Trade Organization."

Answer: The WTO (1995, successor to GATT) runs world trade on three pillar agreements — GATT for goods, GATS for services, TRIPS for intellectual property — supported by agreements on agriculture, anti-dumping and technical standards.

Key points:

  • GATT (goods): MFN, National Treatment, bound tariffs; built through 8 negotiating rounds (Doha is the 9th, unfinished)
  • GATS (services): covers four modes of supply — cross-border (IT services), consumption abroad (medical tourism), commercial presence (bank branches), movement of persons (engineers)
  • TRIPS: minimum patent term 20 years, copyright 50 years, with flexibilities such as compulsory licensing — India’s 2012 Nexavar licence
  • Agriculture Agreement: disciplines subsidies and market access, though rich-country farm subsidies remain contentious
  • Anti-dumping & subsidies agreements: remedies against unfair pricing; SPS and TBT stop health/technical standards becoming disguised protection

Takeaway: GGT = GATT (goods) + GATS (services) + TRIPS (IP) — every agreement runs on MFN and National Treatment.

Q3. 20

"What are the modes of entry into international markets? Discuss the factors influencing entry modes."

Answer: Firms enter foreign markets through a ladder — exporting, licensing/franchising, management contracts/turnkey, joint ventures and wholly-owned subsidiaries — choosing the rung by balancing risk, control, commitment and country factors.

Key points:

  • Exporting: low risk and commitment, limited control — the natural first step (Indian IT firms started by exporting software)
  • Licensing/franchising: sell IP or a business system for fees — low investment, limited control (Godrej licensed brands in Africa)
  • Management contracts/turnkey: export expertise without equity — hotels, hospitals, EPC projects
  • Joint ventures: share ownership and risk with a local partner — Maruti Suzuki (1982), Tata Starbucks (50:50)
  • Wholly-owned subsidiary (greenfield or acquisition): full control, highest risk — Tata Steel–Corus ($12 bn), Tata Motors–JLR
  • Influencing factors: company objectives and resources, market attractiveness, country risk (political, currency), government FDI policy, local partner availability, timing (first vs late mover)

Takeaway: Higher commitment buys higher control — match the rung to your resources, the market’s attractiveness and its political/currency risk.

Q4. 20

"Discuss in detail stages of alliance formation."

Answer: Alliance formation moves through a five-stage sequence — strategy formulation, partner selection, negotiation, operation and evaluation — where trust and fit matter as much as the contract.

Key points:

  • Stage 1 — Strategy formulation: decide whether an alliance fits the corporate strategy (vs acquisition or going alone)
  • Stage 2 — Partner assessment: screen for complementary capabilities, compatible goals, trustworthiness and culture fit
  • Stage 3 — Negotiation: agree scope, contributions, governance, equity, IP and exit clauses
  • Stage 4 — Operation: joint governance, communication systems, conflict resolution (airline alliances such as Star Alliance)
  • Stage 5 — Evaluation and evolution: review performance; renew, expand or terminate the alliance
  • Success factors: compatible objectives, mutual trust, balanced contribution, clear leadership and quick dispute handling

Takeaway: An alliance is a courtship, not just a contract — partner selection and trust decide success more than the legal document.

Q5. 20

"Discuss the selection of channels of distribution in international marketing. Explain the steps for successful exporting."

Answer: International distribution channels link the exporter to the foreign consumer through direct or intermediary routes, and successful exporting follows a disciplined sequence from market research to after-sales support.

Key points:

  • Channel options: direct (own sales force, e-commerce), indirect via home intermediaries (export houses), or foreign-based intermediaries (agents, distributors, wholesalers, retailers)
  • Channel decisions: length, intensity (intensive/selective/exclusive), control, cost and coverage of the target segment
  • Steps for successful exporting: market research & product adaptation → legal/regulatory compliance & documentation → pricing with INCO terms → finance and payment (letters of credit) → logistics (freight, insurance, customs) → after-sales service
  • Government support: DGFT, FIEO, export incentives and SEZs help Indian exporters
  • Digitization: Amazon Global Selling and e-commerce channels are now direct export routes for Indian MSMEs

Takeaway: The channel decides how the product reaches the buyer; exporting is a process — documentation and payment terms are half the battle.

Q6. 20

"What is the relationship between marketing management and logistics management? Discuss in detail."

Answer: Marketing creates demand while logistics fulfils it — the two are interdependent halves of one supply chain: marketing’s 4Ps meet logistics’ place, time and cost, and customer service is the bridge between them.

Key points:

  • Marketing’s place, price and promotion create demand; logistics delivers the right product, at the right place, time, quantity and cost
  • Product decisions depend on logistics: packaging, perishability and transportability
  • Price must recover logistics cost — logistics is often 10–30% of product cost
  • Customer service is logistics’ output: on-time, in-full delivery drives satisfaction and repeat purchase
  • Promotion makes promises logistics must keep — e-commerce and quick-commerce (10-minute delivery) prove the link
  • Integrate the two: demand forecasting ↔ inventory ↔ warehousing ↔ transport ↔ order processing

Takeaway: Marketing wins the order, logistics keeps the customer — plan them as one system, not two departments.

Q7. 20

"(a) What are the factors involved in expatriate selection and describe the advantages and disadvantages in using expatriates. · (b) Briefly discuss expatriate training and development."

Answer: Expatriate selection weighs technical skill alongside cultural adaptability, family situation and language; expatriates bring control and know-how but cost more and risk culture shock, which training is designed to prevent.

Key points:

  • Selection factors: technical competence, managerial ability, cultural empathy/adaptability, family and spouse support, language, motivation, health and company loyalty
  • Advantages of expatriates: company knowledge and control, technology and culture transfer, global manager development, filling local skill gaps
  • Disadvantages: high cost (salary, housing, schooling, allowances), culture shock and early assignment failure (15–40%), family adjustment problems
  • Training areas: cultural awareness, language, practical relocation briefing and role expectations
  • Training types: pre-departure, in-country and on-the-job — briefings, simulation, cultural immersion, mentoring
  • Repatriation planning: returning expatriates need re-entry support so their learning is retained

Takeaway: Hire the expat for the job, but deploy the training for the adjustment — culture shock fails more assignments than technical incompetence.

Q8. 20

"Write short notes on the following: (a) Effects of globalization · (b) The Leontiff Paradox · (c) Environmental scanning · (d) E-Commerce."

Answer: (a) Globalization expands trade, investment and technology flows but also deepens inequality and environmental pressure; (b) the Leontief Paradox found the capital-rich US exporting labour-intensive goods, contradicting factor-proportions theory; (c) environmental scanning monitors the PESTEL macro-environment to spot opportunities and threats; (d) e-commerce is buying and selling over digital networks in B2B, B2C, C2C and B2G forms.

Key points:

  • (a) Effects: trade and FDI growth, technology diffusion, cultural exchange vs homogenization, job offshoring debates, rising inequality concerns, environmental costs, and India’s IT-services gains
  • (b) Leontief (1953, US data): expected capital-intensive exports, found labour-intensive ones; explanations: US labour is highly skilled (human capital), technology advantages, demand patterns
  • (c) Environmental scanning: continuous monitoring of Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal forces plus industry competition, feeding SWOT analysis
  • (d) E-commerce: business models — B2B, B2C (Flipkart, Amazon India), C2C (OLX), B2G; benefits — global reach, low cost, 24×7; enabled by UPI payments and digital logistics

Takeaway: Short-note formula: one definition + two effects + one example — Leontief shows even famous theories can be empirically challenged.

December 2023 Real Paper8 questions · attempt any five · 20 marks each

Q1. 20

"Discuss the evolving paradigm of globalization in the context of geo-economic and geo-strategic developments in the world economy."

Answer: Globalization has evolved in waves shaped by geo-strategic events (Cold War, 9/11, China’s rise) and geo-economic forces (trade blocs, supply chains, digital economy) — and today it is fragmenting into regionalization, protectionism and strategic decoupling.

Key points:

  • Wave 1 (1870–1914): steamships, telegraph, colonialism · Wave 2 (1945–1980): Bretton Woods, GATT, Cold-War blocs
  • Wave 3 (1980–2008): ICT, WTO, China and emerging markets — hyper-globalization
  • Post-2008: slower trade growth after the financial crisis; protectionism, US–China trade war, Brexit
  • Geo-economics: supply-chain reconfiguration, regional blocs (RCEP, CPTPP), digital and data flows
  • Geo-strategy: sanctions, strategic technology controls (semiconductors, 5G), energy security, friend-shoring
  • India’s position: FDI reforms, Make in India, PLI schemes, new trade agreements and digital public infrastructure

Takeaway: Globalization is not a straight line — expect regional blocs and strategic decoupling alongside continued open trade.

Q2. 20

"Discuss various types of trade blocs. What are the benefits of regional trade blocs? Explain."

Answer: Trade blocs integrate countries in deepening stages — PTA, FTA, customs union, common market, economic union and political union (PFCCEP) — and members benefit from freer trade, scale economies, investment and bargaining power.

Key points:

  • Types: Preferential Trade Area (India–SAARC) → Free Trade Area (USMCA) → Customs Union (MERCOSUR) → Common Market (EU pre-Maastricht) → Economic Union (EU + euro) → Political Union
  • Benefits: trade creation, larger markets and economies of scale, more FDI and technology, competition raising efficiency, freer movement of labour and capital, stronger collective bargaining
  • Costs: trade diversion, loss of national sovereignty, unequal gains within the bloc
  • India’s engagement: SAFTA, India–ASEAN dialogue, India–Mauritius CECPA, IPEF participation

Takeaway: Blocs trade sovereignty for market size — PFCCEP is the ladder of integration depth, with the EU at the deepest rung.

Q3. 20

"Discuss different types of organizational structures and highlight the characteristics of global functional structure."

Answer: MNCs organize by function, product, geography, matrix or hybrid forms; the global functional structure groups the whole world’s activities under one function at headquarters — efficient, but weakly responsive to local markets.

Key points:

  • Types: functional, product/divisional, geographic (area), matrix (dual authority), hybrid and network structures
  • Global functional structure: world-wide functional departments (marketing, production, finance) with strong HQ authority
  • Characteristics: centralization, expertise concentration, no duplication across countries
  • Advantages: economies of scale, deep specialist skills, simple authority lines
  • Disadvantages: poor local responsiveness, cross-functional coordination gaps, HQ overload — best for narrow, standardized product lines

Takeaway: Structure follows strategy — functional suits standardized lines, geographic suits diversity, matrix suits dual pressures.

Q4. 20

"Discuss the key drivers and challenges in international marketing."

Answer: Firms internationalize marketing for market growth, scale economies, competition and global customers — and then face culture, regulation, logistics, currency and consumer-behaviour challenges that force glocalization.

Key points:

  • Drivers: saturated home markets, growth and market-seeking, economies of scale and cost, global competition, technology and communication, global customers and brands
  • Challenges: cultural and language differences, legal and regulatory diversity, political and currency risk, distribution and logistics complexity, pricing and payment issues, consumer-behaviour differences, local competition
  • Response: glocalization — standardize the core (brand, technology) and adapt the periphery (taste, price, channels)

Takeaway: The same drivers push firms out and the same challenges greet them — local adaptation is the entry fee, not an option.

Q5. 20

"Briefly discuss the terms of payment of international trade."

Answer: International payments move along a risk ladder — cash in advance, letter of credit, documentary collection and open account — where the letter of credit is the standard risk-sharing instrument.

Key points:

  • Cash in advance: safest for the exporter, riskiest for the buyer; used in new relationships
  • Letter of credit (LC): a bank guarantees payment against documents (bill of lading, invoice, insurance) — confirmed LC adds a second bank’s guarantee
  • Documentary collection: the bank acts as intermediary with drafts (D/P documents against payment, D/A against acceptance)
  • Open account: buyer pays after receipt — safest for the buyer, riskiest for the exporter; only for established relationships
  • Countertrade: barter, buyback and offset, used with hard-currency-poor countries; INCOTERMS and documents frame the whole transaction

Takeaway: Whoever holds the money instrument controls the risk — the LC turns an unknown buyer into a bank’s credit.

Q6. 20

"Why is management of supply chain vital for international business management? Discuss."

Answer: SCM coordinates sourcing, production, inventory, warehousing, transport and information across borders — and in international business it is vital because it decides cost, speed, reliability and resilience.

Key points:

  • Longer, riskier chains: customs, distances, currencies and multiple transport modes
  • Cost: logistics is often 10–30% of product cost — chain efficiency is competitive price
  • Customer service: on-time, in-full delivery wins and keeps orders
  • Inventory and lead times: global sourcing lengthens lead times → safety-stock and EOQ trade-offs
  • Information technology: ERP, RFID and tracking make the chain visible and controllable
  • Resilience: COVID-19, the Suez blockage and geopolitics showed that chains must be diversified — resilience is the new efficiency

Takeaway: In international business you compete on your supply chain as much as your product — resilience now matters as much as cost.

Q7. 20

"(a) Briefly discuss expatriate performance appraisal. · (b) What are the objectives of international compensation and approaches used for international compensation?"

Answer: Expatriate appraisal needs host-country and HQ input because distance distorts judgment; international compensation aims to attract, retain and motivate global talent fairly, using home-based, host-based or balance-sheet approaches.

Key points:

  • (a) Appraisal problems: different environments distort results, HQ vs host-evaluator divergence, communication distance, cultural differences in feedback
  • (a) Solutions: combine host manager and HQ appraisal, adjust targets for country conditions, measure both job and cultural performance
  • (b) Objectives: attract and retain global talent, fairness and equity, motivate performance, control cost, ease transfers and repatriation
  • (b) Approaches: home-country based, host-country based, balance-sheet (maintain home purchasing power plus premium), localization and hybrid/negotiated
  • Components: base pay, allowances (housing, COLA, hardship, education), benefits, tax equalization, incentives

Takeaway: Appraisal needs two eyes (host + HQ); compensation needs one principle (equity) and one budget (cost).

Q8. 20

"Write short notes on the following: (a) Challenges faced in international business · (b) Distinction between GATT and WTO · (c) Global financial system · (d) Major reasons for firms going international."

Answer: (a) IB faces cultural, legal, political, currency, logistics and ethical challenges; (b) GATT was a provisional 1947 agreement on goods while the WTO is a permanent 1995 organization covering goods, services and IP with binding dispute settlement; (c) the global financial system is the web of institutions, markets and instruments moving capital across borders; (d) firms go international for market, resource, efficiency and strategic-asset motives (MRES).

Key points:

  • (a) Challenges: cultural and language differences, legal diversity, political and currency risk, logistics complexity, intense competition, ethical dilemmas in host countries
  • (b) GATT vs WTO: GATT = rules for goods, no permanent institution, weak dispute handling · WTO = permanent organization, goods + services + IP, binding dispute settlement, ~164 members
  • (c) Global financial system: institutions (IMF for stability, World Bank for development, BIS, central banks like RBI), markets (FX, money, capital), and instruments for payments, credit and risk
  • (d) MRES: Market seeking (new customers), Resource seeking (raw materials/labour), Efficiency seeking (cost arbitrage), Strategic asset seeking (brands, technology); plus home-market saturation and competition

Takeaway: Four notes, four one-liners: challenges (PESTEL+), GATT→WTO (institutional upgrade), global finance (IMF + FX), reasons (MRES).

Q1. 20

"(a) Discuss the importance of international business. · (b) Briefly discuss the evolving paradigm of globalisation."

Answer: (a) IB gives firms larger markets, resources, scale, learning and risk spread, and gives nations jobs, FDI, technology and development; (b) globalization’s paradigm has shifted from trade-only to investment-led, digital, regionally fragmented and geopolitically managed integration.

Key points:

  • (a) Benefits: market expansion, resource access, economies of scale, technology transfer, risk diversification, competition-driven efficiency, employment and FDI inflow
  • (b) Evolving paradigm: three waves (1870–1914, 1945–1980, 1980 onwards); India’s 1991 LPG reforms as the turning point
  • (b) Post-2008: deglobalization and reshoring debates, digital trade growth, regionalization (RCEP, EU), strategic decoupling in technology
  • (b) India today: IT exports ~$194 bn, globalized pharma and auto-components, rising FDI, trade agreements and digital public infrastructure

Takeaway: IB is the growth engine for firms and nations; globalization is a moving target — prepare for regional and digital integration.

Q2. 20

"Examine the impact of technological, ecological and legal environment on international business."

Answer: Technology enables and disrupts IB, ecology constrains it with climate rules and green supply chains, and law frames contracts, IP and FDI — together they decide where and how firms operate.

Key points:

  • Technological: ICT and e-commerce shrink distance, container shipping and tracking cut logistics cost, automation reshapes production; technology transfer and the digital divide matter
  • Ecological: carbon taxes and green regulations, demand for sustainable products, climate disasters disrupt chains, sustainability reporting; India’s solar and green-hydrogen push
  • Legal: differences in commercial law and contract enforcement, IP protection, FDI regulations, labour and environmental standards, arbitration for disputes
  • Integration: scan all three through PESTEL before entry — the environment shapes entry mode, location and operations

Takeaway: Technology enables, ecology constrains, law frames — all three must be scanned before any entry decision.

Q3. 20

"Explain the importance of international trade theories and discuss the Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) theories."

Answer: Trade theories explain why and what countries trade (comparative advantage, factor endowments, product cycles), while FDI theories — market imperfections, internalization and Dunning’s OLI — explain why firms invest abroad rather than export.

Key points:

  • Importance of trade theories: explain trade patterns, guide tariff and FTA policy, predict gains from trade, shape firm strategy
  • Classic theories: mercantilism, absolute advantage (Smith), comparative advantage (Ricardo), Heckscher–Ohlin factor proportions, Leontief paradox, product life cycle (Vernon), new trade theory, Porter’s diamond
  • FDI theories: monopolistic advantage (Hymer), market imperfections, internalization theory, Dunning’s eclectic OLI paradigm
  • OLI in practice: Ownership + Location + Internalization advantages together decide FDI — Tata–JLR is the textbook Indian case

Takeaway: Theories map trade between nations (comparative advantage) and investment between firms (OLI) — learn the one-line logic of each.

Q4. 20

"(a) Discuss various types of organisational structures. · (b) Highlight the characteristics of global functional structure and its advantages and disadvantages."

Answer: (a) MNC structures span functional, product-divisional, geographic, matrix and hybrid forms; (b) the global functional structure centralizes each function worldwide under HQ — efficient and simple, but weak on local responsiveness.

Key points:

  • (a) Types: functional, product division, geographic/area, matrix (dual reporting), hybrid (mix) and network structures; choice follows strategy, size and product diversity
  • (b) Characteristics: world-wide functional departments, HQ-centred authority, centralized decisions
  • (b) Advantages: economies of scale, no duplication, pooled expertise, clear authority lines
  • (b) Disadvantages: poor local adaptation, cross-functional coordination gaps, HQ overload, unsuitable for diverse product lines

Takeaway: Structure follows strategy — functional suits narrow lines, geographic suits diversity, matrix suits dual pressures.

Q5. 20

"What do you understand by global financial system? Discuss the foreign exchange market in detail."

Answer: The global financial system is the web of institutions, markets and instruments that moves capital across borders, and the foreign exchange market is where currencies are traded — its exchange rates decide the competitiveness of every trade and investment.

Key points:

  • Global financial system: IMF (balance-of-payments support and stability), World Bank group (development), BIS, central banks (RBI), commercial banks and sovereign funds; functions: payments, savings, credit, risk and stability
  • FX market: the world’s largest market (~$7.5 trillion daily), decentralized OTC trading of currency pairs
  • Participants: banks, firms (exporters/importers), investors, central banks, speculators and arbitrageurs
  • Exchange rates: spot vs forward; floating, fixed and managed floats — India runs a managed float with RBI intervention
  • Determinants: inflation, interest rates, trade balance, capital flows, expectations and speculation
  • FX risk: transaction, translation and economic exposure — hedged with forwards, futures and options

Takeaway: Global finance is the plumbing of the world economy and the FX market is its heart — exchange rates decide who wins in trade.

Q6. 20

"Discuss international logistics management and explain in detail the relationship between marketing management and logistics management."

Answer: International logistics plans, implements and controls the flow of goods, services and information across borders, and it is the delivery half of marketing — marketing creates the demand that logistics must fulfil.

Key points:

  • International logistics: multimodal transport, customs clearance, INCOTERMS, warehousing and distribution centres, inventory across long lead times, IT visibility
  • Relationship: product (packaging, transportability), price (logistics cost 10–30%), promotion (delivery promises), place (the channel itself)
  • Customer service = logistics output: on-time, in-full delivery decides repeat purchase and e-commerce ratings
  • Integrated planning: demand forecasting ↔ inventory ↔ warehousing ↔ transport ↔ order processing across countries

Takeaway: Marketing sells the promise, logistics delivers it — worldwide coordination makes or breaks customer service.

Q7. 20

"Write short notes on any four of the following: (a) International compensation · (b) Strategic alliances · (c) Benefits of trade blocs · (d) International joint ventures · (e) International consumer behaviour."

Answer: (a) International compensation designs fair, cost-effective reward packages for global staff; (b) strategic alliances are cooperative arrangements that create value through scale, learning and market access; (c) trade blocs deliver trade creation, bigger markets and investment; (d) joint ventures share ownership and risk with local partners; (e) international consumer behaviour studies how culture and social factors shape buying.

Key points:

  • (a) Compensation: balance-sheet, host-based and home-based approaches; components — base pay, allowances (housing, COLA, hardship), tax equalization, incentives
  • (b) Alliances: types — non-equity (licensing, R&D, marketing pacts), equity alliances, joint ventures; value — scale economies, capability access, learning, risk sharing (Star Alliance, Tata–Airbus)
  • (c) Trade blocs: trade creation, larger markets and scale, more FDI and technology, mobility of labour and capital, stronger bargaining — with trade diversion as the cost
  • (d) JVs: motives — local knowledge, regulatory compliance, risk sharing; Indian examples Maruti Suzuki (1982), Tata Starbucks; failure factors — culture clash, unequal contribution
  • (e) Consumer behaviour: cultural, social, personal and psychological influences; standardization vs adaptation; Indian consumers are value-conscious and increasingly digital

Takeaway: Short-note formula: definition + types/motives + one Indian example — these five topics recur across sessions.

December 2024 Real Paper7 questions · attempt any five · 20 marks each

Q1. 20

"Explain the benefits of international business. Why do firms go international? Discuss in brief."

Answer: IB rewards firms with market growth, resources, scale, learning and risk spread, and the four seeking motives — market, resource, efficiency and strategic asset (MRES) — explain why firms make the move.

Key points:

  • Benefits: bigger markets, resource access, economies of scale, technology and knowledge, risk diversification, competitiveness, jobs and development in home and host countries
  • Why firms go: market seeking (new customers, following clients), resource seeking (materials, labour), efficiency seeking (cost arbitrage), strategic asset seeking (brands, technology)
  • Additional push: home-market saturation, competition at home, government incentives, following competitors abroad
  • Indian evidence: IT services and pharma exporters, Tata’s acquisitions, ONGC Videsh’s overseas oil assets

Takeaway: MRES covers the "why"; the benefits are the "what’s in it" — growth, resources, scale and learning.

Q2. 20

"Describe the elements of international business environment and discuss how a country’s political environment influences the operations of business both domestically and internationally."

Answer: The IB environment has internal, industry and macro (PESTEL) layers, and the political environment is the parent environment — it decides entry, ownership, operations and even exit for every business.

Key points:

  • Elements: macro (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal), industry/task (competitors, suppliers, customers), and internal environment
  • Political factors: government type and stability, policies (FDI caps, industrial policy), licensing and price controls, corruption, political risk (expropriation, sanctions), tariffs and trade barriers
  • Domestic influence: laws and policies shape the business climate at home — e.g., India’s GST, labour codes and digital-payments policy
  • International influence: entry-mode choice (JV to satisfy local ownership rules), profitability (taxes, incentives), operations (labour and environmental law), risk assessment
  • Examples: India’s FDI liberalization; US–China technology controls; sanctions reshaping trade routes

Takeaway: Politics can grant entry and expropriate assets — assess the political environment first, at home and abroad.

Q3. 20

"Discuss the key agreements of WTO."

Answer: The WTO’s key agreements are GATT (goods), GATS (services) and TRIPS (intellectual property), supported by agreements on agriculture, anti-dumping and technical standards — all built on MFN and National Treatment (see the June 2023 structure diagram above).

Key points:

  • GATT: trade in goods, MFN, National Treatment, bound tariffs, negotiated in rounds
  • GATS: four modes of service supply — cross-border (IT), consumption abroad (medical tourism), commercial presence (branches), movement of persons (professionals)
  • TRIPS: patents 20 years, copyright 50 years, flexibilities like compulsory licensing (India’s Nexavar case, COVID-19 waiver push)
  • Other agreements: agriculture, anti-dumping, subsidies, SPS and TBT; the Trade Facilitation Agreement (2017) cuts customs red tape
  • Dispute settlement: consultation → panel → Appellate Body → binding implementation — the WTO’s enforcement engine

Takeaway: Asked twice in four sessions — GGT + principles + dispute settlement is the highest-probability WTO answer.

Q4. 20

"Explain the concept of strategic alliances in detail. What are the types of strategic alliances and what are the sources of value creation in an alliance? Discuss."

Answer: A strategic alliance is a cooperative arrangement between independent firms that share resources to pursue common goals — spanning non-equity contracts, equity stakes and joint ventures — and its value comes from scale, access, learning and risk sharing.

Key points:

  • Concept: cooperation between independent firms retaining their identities, for mutual strategic benefit
  • Types: non-equity (licensing, franchising, R&D and marketing agreements, consortia), equity alliances (minority stakes), and joint ventures (new entity)
  • Sources of value creation: economies of scale and scope, access to new markets and capabilities, learning and knowledge transfer, cost and risk sharing, speed to market, legitimacy in regulated sectors
  • Risks: partner opportunism, culture clash, knowledge leakage
  • Examples: Star Alliance (airlines), Maruti–Suzuki, Tata–Airbus, pharma co-development deals

Takeaway: Alliances create value through combination — shared scale, shared risk, shared learning — but they leak knowledge to the partner too.

Q5. 20

"(a) What are the key drivers of international marketing and the challenges in international marketing? Examine briefly. · (b) Distinguish between international and domestic marketing."

Answer: (a) Growth, scale, competition and global customers drive international marketing, which then meets cultural, legal, currency and logistics challenges; (b) international marketing multiplies the uncontrollables — more markets, currencies, cultures and laws to coordinate.

Key points:

  • (a) Drivers: saturated home markets, growth opportunities, economies of scale, global competition, technology, global customer segments
  • (a) Challenges: culture and language, legal and regulatory diversity, currency and payment risk, distribution and logistics, consumer-behaviour differences, local competitors
  • (b) Domestic marketing: one market, one currency, one culture, one set of regulations, one controllable environment
  • (b) International marketing: multiple environments, added uncontrollables (PESTEL), 4Ps must be adapted per market, global brand vs local positioning coordination

Takeaway: Same principles, different ballparks — the extra uncontrollables (currency, culture, law) are what make marketing "international".

Q6. 20

"Define international human resource management and its scope and explain in detail the role and nature of international human resource management."

Answer: IHRM is the management of human resources across national borders — staffing, developing and rewarding people in multiple countries — adding expatriation, culture, law and strategy to domestic HRM.

Key points:

  • Definition: procurement, development, compensation and relations of people in an international, multi-country context
  • Scope: HQ staff, host-country nationals (HCNs), parent-country nationals/expatriates (PCNs), third-country nationals (TCNs); staffing policies from ethnocentric to geocentric (EPRG)
  • Role: aligning people with MNC strategy, global talent management, expatriate management (selection, training, appraisal, compensation, repatriation), cultural adaptation, compliance with multiple labour laws
  • Nature: more complex and costly than domestic HRM (distance, culture, law), higher risk of failure, and sensitive to the firm’s global vs local strategy

Takeaway: IHRM = domestic HRM + expatriation + culture + multiple legal systems — staffing policy (EPRG) is its strategic heart.

Q7. 20

"Write short notes on any four of the following: (a) Supply chain modelling · (b) Balance of Payments (BoP) · (c) Hybrid Structure · (d) General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS) · (e) Theory of comparative cost advantage."

Answer: (a) Supply chain modelling represents chain flows to optimize cost and service; (b) BoP is the systematic double-entry record of a country’s transactions with the world; (c) hybrid structure mixes functional, product and geographic forms; (d) GATS governs trade in services through four modes of supply; (e) comparative advantage says countries gain by specializing where their opportunity cost is lower.

Key points:

  • (a) Modelling: material, information and money flows; tools — EOQ, network design, linear programming, simulation; targets — minimize cost, maximize service, tame the bullwhip effect
  • (b) BoP: current account (goods, services, income, transfers) + capital and financial account (FDI, FPI, loans, reserves); must balance by double-entry; surplus adds reserves, deficit drains them — India runs a trade deficit offset by IT services and remittances
  • (c) Hybrid: combines two or more structures to balance global efficiency with local responsiveness; used when pure matrix is too complex — global product divisions with regional marketing units
  • (d) GATS: four modes — cross-border (Mode 1, IT exports), consumption abroad (Mode 2, tourism), commercial presence (Mode 3, bank branches), movement of persons (Mode 4, professionals); MFN and National Treatment apply
  • (e) Comparative advantage (Ricardo): specialize where opportunity cost is lowest — even a country better at everything gains from trade; India’s textiles vs Switzerland’s watches

Takeaway: Five notes, five frameworks: model (optimize), BoP (record), hybrid (balance), GATS (modes), comparative advantage (opportunity cost).

Q2. 20

"Discuss the drivers of globalization and India’s globalization experience since 1991."

Answer: Technology, trade liberalization, regional integration, MNC activity, financial liberalization and political change drive globalization, and India’s post-1991 LPG journey turned it from a closed to an open economy.

Key points:

Q3. 20

"Explain Dunning’s OLI eclectic paradigm with suitable examples."

Answer: FDI happens when Ownership, Location and Internalization advantages coexist — Tata’s JLR acquisition illustrates all three conditions.

Key points:

  • O = firm assets (technology, brand, management); L = country factors (market, cost, policy); I = keeping advantages in-house
  • O only → export; O+I → licensing; O+L+I → wholly-owned subsidiary (Tata–JLR 2008, $2.3 bn)

Takeaway: OLI = the mode-choice equation of FDI.

Q4. 20

"Discuss the stages of regional economic integration with examples."

Answer: Integration deepens through six stages — PTA, FTA, customs union, common market, economic union and political union (PFCCEP).

Key points:

  • PTA (India–SAARC) → FTA (USMCA) → Customs Union (MERCOSUR) → Common Market → Economic Union (EU + euro) → Political Union
  • Trade creation vs trade diversion decide whether integration is efficient

Takeaway: PFCCEP is the ladder — each stage adds one more degree of joint decision-making.

Q5. 20

"Write short notes on: (a) Barriers to international business · (b) Hofstede’s cultural dimensions · (c) EPRG framework · (d) Expatriate management."

Answer: (a) Tariff, non-tariff, cultural, administrative, currency and political barriers; (b) six Hofstede dimensions explain cross-cultural management; (c) EPRG classifies marketing orientation from ethnocentric to geocentric; (d) expatriate management covers selection, training, appraisal, compensation and repatriation.

Key points:

  • (a) Tariffs, quotas, standards, language/culture, bureaucracy, exchange-rate risk, political risk
  • (b) Power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation, indulgence — India high on power distance (77)
  • (c) Ethnocentric (Patanjali), Polycentric (Apollo Gulf), Region-centric (Unilever South Asia), Geocentric (Apple)
  • (d) Selection factors, training types, appraisal with host+HQ input, balance-sheet compensation, repatriation support

Takeaway: Four notes = four recurring exam topics — one definition plus one example each.
