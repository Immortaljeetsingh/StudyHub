Unit 1: Operations Management — An Introduction

▶
Project Management Introduction
▶
PERT and CPM Explained
📖 Core Concepts

Operations Management: Design, operation, and improvement of systems that create and deliver products/services.

Input-Transformation-Output Model: Inputs (materials, labor, capital) > Transformation Process > Outputs (goods, services).

Manufacturing vs Services: Tangible vs Intangible products.

Service Characteristics (I-I-H-P):

  • Intangibility: Cannot be touched
  • Inseparability: Produced and consumed simultaneously
  • Heterogeneity: Quality varies
  • Perishability: Cannot be stored

Competitive Priorities (C-Q-F-D): Cost, Quality, Flexibility, Delivery.

📖 Key Definitions

Operations Management: The management of systems or processes that create goods and/or provide services.

Value Added: The difference between the cost of inputs and the value of outputs.

Core Competencies: Unique strengths that provide competitive advantage.

📊 Input-Transformation-Output Model
Inputs (Materials, Labor, Capital)
↓
Transformation Process
↓
Outputs (Goods & Services)
💡 Real-World Example

A restaurant: Inputs (raw food, chefs, kitchen) > Transformation (cooking, plating) > Output (meal served). Service is inseparable (cooked and served simultaneously) and perishable (unsold food is wasted).

⚠ Exam Tip

I-I-H-P (service characteristics) and C-Q-F-D (competitive priorities) are guaranteed short-answer questions.

🧠 Memory Aid

I-I-H-P = Intangibility, Inseparability, Heterogeneity, Perishability (service characteristics). C-Q-F-D = Cost, Quality, Flexibility, Delivery (competitive priorities).

✅ Unit Summary

  • OM = design + operation + improvement of production/service systems
  • Input-Transformation-Output is the core framework
  • Services have 4 unique characteristics: I-I-H-P
  • 4 competitive priorities: C-Q-F-D

Unit 3: Sustainable Operations

📖 Core Concepts

Triple Bottom Line (TBL): People, Planet, Profit (3 Ps).

Green Manufacturing: Reducing environmental impact in production.

Circular Economy: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle (eliminate waste concept).

Lean Operations: Eliminate all forms of waste.

7 Wastes (TIMWOOD):

  • Transport — unnecessary movement of materials
  • Inventory — excess stock tying up capital
  • Motion — unnecessary movement of people
  • Waiting — idle time between process steps
  • Overproduction — making more than needed
  • Over-processing — doing more than required
  • Defects — rework and scrap

Carbon Footprint: Total greenhouse gas emissions caused by an organization.

📖 Key Definitions

Triple Bottom Line: Measuring success by social, environmental, and financial impact.

Circular Economy: An economic system aimed at eliminating waste through continual use of resources.

Lean: A philosophy that focuses on minimizing waste within a manufacturing system.

💡 Real-World Example

Patagonia uses recycled materials (planet), pays fair wages (people), and remains profitable (profit) — demonstrating TBL in action.

Unit 4: Process and Capacity Analysis

▶
Earned Value Management
▶
Risk Management
📖 Core Concepts

Process Types:

  • Job Shop: Low volume, high variety (custom furniture)
  • Batch: Medium volume, medium variety (bakery)
  • Mass/Assembly Line: High volume, low variety (cars)
  • Continuous: Very high volume, no variety (cement, chemicals)

Capacity: Maximum output rate of a system.

Capacity Planning: Long-term (years), Medium-term (months), Short-term (weeks).

Bottleneck: The process step with the lowest capacity — limits entire system.

Theory of Constraints (TOC): Identify > Exploit > Subordinate > Elevate > Repeat.

Break-Even Analysis: BEP = Fixed Cost / (Price - Variable Cost per unit).

📖 Key Definitions

Bottleneck: The resource with the lowest capacity in the system; it limits overall throughput.

Theory of Constraints: A methodology for identifying and managing bottlenecks.

Break-Even Point: The level of output at which total revenue equals total cost.

Process TypeVolumeVarietyExample
Job ShopLowHighCustom furniture
BatchMediumMediumBakery
Mass/LineHighLowCar assembly
ContinuousVery HighNoneCement, chemicals
💡 Real-World Example

In a pizza shop: dough prep (20/hr) > baking (12/hr) > packaging (25/hr). Baking is the bottleneck at 12/hr. TOC says: exploit (maximize oven use), then subordinate (slow down prep to match baking).

🧠 Memory Aid

TOC = I-E-S-E-R = Identify, Exploit, Subordinate, Elevate, Repeat.

💰 Key Formula: BEP = FC / (P - VC)

📊 Capacity Planning Strategies

Design Capacity: Maximum theoretical output under ideal conditions.

Effective Capacity: Maximum output after accounting for real-world constraints (breaks, maintenance, changeovers).

Capacity Utilization = Actual Output / Design Capacity x 100.

Three Strategies for Capacity Expansion:

  • Lead Strategy: Add capacity before demand arrives (aggressive, risk of overcapacity) — used by e-commerce firms during festival season
  • Lag Strategy: Add capacity only after demand materializes (conservative, risk of lost sales) — traditional Indian manufacturers like Tata Steel follow this
  • Match Strategy: Add capacity incrementally in response to demand signals (balanced approach) — used by Maruti Suzuki for new model launches

Capacity Cushion = 100% - Utilization Rate. Most firms maintain 10-20% cushion for demand surges.

📊 Theory of Constraints — Focusing Steps
Step 1: IDENTIFY — Find the system bottleneck
↓
Step 2: EXPLOIT — Maximize bottleneck output (no idle time)
↓
Step 3: SUBORDINATE — Align all other resources to bottleneck pace
↓
Step 4: ELEVATE — Invest to increase bottleneck capacity
↓
Step 5: REPEAT — Go back to Step 1 (new bottleneck emerges)
💡 Indian Example — Capacity Planning

Amul (Gujarat Cooperative): During festival seasons (Diwali, Holi), Amul increases milk procurement and runs plants at 95%+ utilization. They use a lead strategy — expanding chilling capacity in rural Gujarat ahead of peak demand. Their Surat plant added a new processing line in 2023 specifically for the Navratri sweet-making season, increasing capacity by 40%.

✅ Unit Summary

  • 4 process types: Job Shop, Batch, Mass, Continuous
  • Bottleneck = lowest capacity step
  • TOC: Identify → Exploit → Subordinate → Elevate → Repeat
  • BEP = FC / (P - VC)
  • Design vs Effective Capacity — always calculate utilization
  • Lead, Lag, Match = 3 capacity expansion strategies

Unit 7: Demand Forecasting

▶
Gantt Charts
▶
WBS Work Breakdown Structure
📖 Core Concepts

Qualitative Methods: Delphi method, Market Survey, Expert Opinion (subjective, judgment-based).

Quantitative Methods:

  • Time Series: Moving Average, Exponential Smoothing, Trend Analysis
  • Causal: Regression, Correlation

Exponential Smoothing: F(t+1) = alpha x D(t) + (1-alpha) x F(t). alpha = smoothing constant (0 to 1); higher alpha = more responsive to recent data.

Forecast Error Measures:

  • MAD = Mean Absolute Deviation
  • MSE = Mean Squared Error
  • MAPE = Mean Absolute Percentage Error

Forecast Error = Actual - Forecast.

📖 Key Definitions

Exponential Smoothing: A weighted moving average where recent data gets more weight.

MAD: Average of absolute forecast errors.

MAPE: Average of absolute percentage errors (best for comparing across datasets).

💡 Real-World Example

A retailer uses exponential smoothing (alpha = 0.3). If actual = 100 and forecast = 90, new forecast = 0.3(100) + 0.7(90) = 93.

📐 Numerical Walkthrough — 3-Month Moving Average

Given: Jan=120, Feb=135, Mar=150, Apr=145, May=?

3-Month Moving Average for May:

  • MA(May) = (Feb + Mar + Apr) / 3 = (135 + 150 + 145) / 3 = 143.3

Exponential Smoothing (alpha = 0.4, F(Apr) = 140):

  • F(May) = 0.4 x 145 + 0.6 x 140 = 58 + 84 = 142

Error Calculation (if Actual May = 148):

  • Forecast Error = 148 - 143.3 = 4.7 (moving avg)
  • Forecast Error = 148 - 142 = 6.0 (exponential smoothing)

💡 Indian Example — Forecasting

ITC Foods (Bingo, Sunfeast): ITC uses a combination of time-series forecasting and market intelligence from its e-Choupal network (over 35,000 internet kiosks in rural India) to predict demand for packaged food products. During monsoon disruptions, they switch to qualitative methods — surveying distributors across 15 states. Their forecasting accuracy improved by 22% after implementing SAP Integrated Business Planning in 2022.

🧠 Memory Aid

alpha-D + (1-alpha)-F = Exponential smoothing formula. Higher alpha = more reactive. Lower alpha = more stable. "Higher Alpha = Higher Reactivity" (easy to remember).

💰 Key Formulas: F(t+1) = aD(t) + (1-a)F(t) | MAD = Σ|A-F|/n | MAPE = Σ|Error/A|/n × 100

✅ Unit Summary

  • Qualitative: Delphi, surveys, expert opinion
  • Quantitative: Time series (moving avg, exp smoothing) + Causal (regression)
  • Exponential smoothing: recent data weighted more
  • MAD, MSE, MAPE = error measures
  • MAPE is best for comparing across different datasets
  • Holt's for trend, Winters' for trend + seasonality

Unit 8: Inventory Management

▶
WBS Work Breakdown Structure
▶
Stakeholder Management
📖 Core Concepts

Inventory Types: Raw Materials, WIP (Work in Progress), Finished Goods, MRO (Maintenance, Repair, Operations).

ABC Analysis: A items (80% value, 20% items), B items (15% value, 30% items), C items (5% value, 50% items).

EOQ (Economic Order Quantity) = sqrt(2DS/H). D = Annual demand, S = Ordering cost per order, H = Holding cost per unit per year.

Reorder Point (ROP) = d x L + Safety Stock. d = daily demand, L = lead time.

Safety Stock = Z x sigma x sqrt(L). Z = service level factor, sigma = std dev of demand.

Just-in-Time (JIT): Produce only what's needed, when needed, in the quantity needed.

Kanban: Pull system using visual signals (cards).

📖 Key Definitions

EOQ: The order quantity that minimizes total inventory costs (ordering + holding).

Safety Stock: Extra inventory held to buffer against demand/supply variability.

JIT: An inventory strategy that increases efficiency by receiving goods only as needed.

Class% Items% ValueControl
A20%80%Tight, frequent review
B30%15%Moderate control
C50%5%Simple, infrequent
💡 Real-World Example

Amazon uses ABC analysis: A items (high-value electronics) in easily accessible locations. C items (cheap accessories) in back storage.

📐 EOQ Numerical Walkthrough

Given: Annual Demand (D) = 12,000 units, Ordering Cost (S) = Rs 500/order, Holding Cost (H) = Rs 10/unit/year

  • EOQ = sqrt(2 x 12000 x 500 / 10) = sqrt(1,200,000) = 1,095 units
  • Number of Orders/Year = D / EOQ = 12,000 / 1,095 = 10.96 orders
  • Order Cycle = 365 / 10.96 = 33.3 days
  • Total Cost = (D/EOQ x S) + (EOQ/2 x H) = (10.96 x 500) + (547.5 x 10) = 5,480 + 5,475 = Rs 10,955/year

Safety Stock Example: Daily demand = 100 units, Lead time = 5 days, sigma = 15, Service level = 95% (Z = 1.65)

  • Safety Stock = 1.65 x 15 x sqrt(5) = 1.65 x 15 x 2.236 = 55.3 units
  • ROP = (100 x 5) + 55.3 = 555.3 units

Suppliers deliver parts multiple times a day using milk-run logistics (dedicated trucks on fixed routes). This reduced inventory carrying costs by 60% compared to the 1990s. Kanban signals trigger replenishment from nearby supplier parks.

Their cold chain operates on JIT principles: milk collected in the morning reaches processing plants by evening, and packaged products reach distributors by next morning. Inventory turns are among the fastest in FMCG — fresh products reach retail within 24-48 hours of production.

Flipkart's fulfillment centers use algorithmic slotting that adjusts daily based on demand patterns during sales events like Big Billion Days.

🧠 Memory Aid

EOQ = sqrt(2DS/H). A = 80/20 rule = ABC Analysis. JIT = "Right part, right quantity, right time." Kanban = visual pull signal.

💰 Key Formulas: EOQ = sqrt(2DS/H) | ROP = d×L + SS | SS = Z×sigma×sqrt(L) | Total Inventory Cost = (D/Q x S) + (Q/2 x H)

✅ Unit Summary

  • ABC: A=80/20, B=15/30, C=5/50 (Pareto principle)

  • ROP = when to reorder; Safety Stock = buffer against variability

Unit 9: Aggregate Planning

▶

📖 Core Concepts

Aggregate Plan: Medium-term production plan (3-18 months) that determines output levels.

Strategies:

  • Chase Strategy: Match demand by adjusting workforce (hire/fire as needed)
  • Level Strategy: Constant production rate, use inventory/backlog to absorb demand fluctuations
  • Mixed Strategy: Combination of chase and level

Costs Considered: Regular time, Overtime, Hiring, Firing, Inventory holding, Backorder.

📖 Key Definitions

Aggregate Planning: A process to develop a preliminary, approximate schedule of the overall operations.

Chase Strategy: Adjusting capacity to match demand (variable workforce).

Level Strategy: Maintaining constant capacity regardless of demand.

StrategyWorkforceInventoryBest For
ChaseVariableLowDemand fluctuations
LevelConstantHigh/LowStable demand
MixedSemi-variableModerateMost situations
💡 Real-World Example

An ice cream factory uses chase strategy (more workers in summer, fewer in winter). A paper mill uses level strategy (constant production, build inventory in low-demand periods).

📊 Aggregate Planning Cost Comparison

Scenario: Demand = 1000 units/month (normal), 1500 units/month (peak, 3 months), 700 units/month (low, 3 months)

StrategyApproachAdditional CostRisk
ChaseHire/fire workers each monthHiring Rs 5,000/person, Firing Rs 8,000/personMorale issues, training costs

Key Decision Factors:

  • High hiring/firing costs → prefer Level strategy
  • High inventory costs → prefer Chase strategy
  • Highly seasonal demand with stable base → prefer Mixed strategy
  • Skilled labor shortage → Level (keep workforce stable)
💡 Indian Example — Aggregate Planning

Cadbury India (Mondelez): Uses a mixed strategy. Their Pune plant runs at a level production rate year-round for core products (Dairy Milk). During festival season (Oct-Dec), they add overtime shifts and subcontract packaging to meet the 3x demand surge. Post-festival, they reduce shifts but don't fire workers — maintaining a skilled permanent workforce.

Hindustan Unilever (HUL): For seasonal products like Kwality Wall's ice cream, HUL uses chase strategy — temporary workers and extended shifts during summer (March-June), reduced operations in winter. For detergents (Surf Excel), they use level strategy with promotional demand managed through advance inventory builds.

Indian Railways (Coach Manufacturing): Uses a level strategy — Rail Coach Factory (Kapurthala) and Integral Coach Factory (Chennai) produce at a constant rate. Finished coaches are stored in marshalling yards and deployed as needed across the network. Demand variation is absorbed through scheduling, not production changes.

🧠 Memory Aid

C-L-M = Chase, Level, Mixed strategies. "Chase = Change workforce. Level = Change inventory. Mixed = Both."

✅ Unit Summary

  • Chase = adjust workforce to match demand (hire/fire)
  • Level = constant production, use inventory/backlog to absorb variation
  • Mixed = combination of chase and level (most practical)
  • Consider: hiring/firing costs, inventory costs, skill requirements, demand patterns
  • Indian examples: Cadbury (mixed), HUL (chase for seasonal, level for staples), Railways (level)

Unit 11: Scheduling and Sequencing

▶
Quality Management
▶
Resource Leveling
📖 Core Concepts

FCFS: First Come First Served — simple, fair.

SPT: Shortest Processing Time — minimizes average flow time.

EDD: Earliest Due Date — minimizes maximum lateness.

CR: Critical Ratio = (Due Date - Today) / Processing Time.

Johnson's Rule: For 2-machine flow shop sequencing.

Flow Time: Total time a job spends in the system. Makespan: Total time to complete all jobs.

📖 Key Definitions

SPT Rule: Process the job with the shortest processing time first.

Johnson's Rule: An algorithm for optimally sequencing jobs through two machines.

Flow Time: Time from job arrival to completion.

💡 Real-World Example

A mechanic with 3 cars: Oil change (30 min), Brake repair (2 hrs), Tire rotation (45 min). SPT sequence: Oil (30) > Tire (45) > Brake (120). Minimizes average waiting time.

📐 Scheduling Rule Comparison — Worked Example

Given 4 jobs:

JobProcessing TimeDue Date
A68
B212
C46
D816

FCFS Sequence (A-B-C-D):

  • Flow times: A=6, B=8, C=12, D=20. Avg Flow = 46/4 = 11.5
  • Lateness: A=-2, B=-4, C=6, D=4. Max Lateness = 6

SPT Sequence (B-C-A-D):

  • Flow times: B=2, C=6, A=12, D=20. Avg Flow = 40/4 = 10.0 (minimum!)
  • Lateness: B=-10, C=0, A=4, D=4. Max Lateness = 4

EDD Sequence (C-A-B-D):

  • Flow times: C=4, A=10, B=12, D=20. Avg Flow = 46/4 = 11.5
  • Lateness: C=-2, A=2, B=0, D=4. Max Lateness = 4 (minimum!)

Conclusion: SPT minimizes average flow time (best overall). EDD minimizes maximum lateness (best for meeting deadlines).

💡 Indian Example — Scheduling

Infosys (Bangalore DC): Uses SPT-like prioritization for IT service tickets. Quick-fix issues (password resets, access requests) are processed first, while complex projects (system upgrades) are scheduled later. This minimizes average ticket resolution time across their 200,000+ employee base.

Indian Railways (Rake Scheduling): Uses a combination of EDD and priority rules. Express trains (Rajdhani, Shatabdi) get priority over freight trains. The scheduling algorithm at Indian Railways' control offices considers due dates (timetable adherence), train priority, and track availability — a real-world application of multi-machine scheduling.

IRCTC Catering (Pantry Cars): Uses FCFS for serving passengers on trains, but internally uses SPT for kitchen operations — preparing quick items (tea, snacks) before complex meals (thali) to minimize average waiting time for passengers.

🧠 Memory Aid

SPT = Shortest First = Min Flow Time. EDD = Earliest Due = Min Lateness. "Shortest is Sweetest (for flow). Earliest is Easiest (for lateness)."

✅ Unit Summary

  • FCFS = fair; SPT = min flow time; EDD = min lateness
  • Johnson's Rule for 2-machine sequencing (split by shortest time)
  • CR = (Due - Today) / Processing Time (dynamic priority rule)
  • Gantt charts visualize schedules
  • Indian examples: Infosys (SPT for tickets), Railways (priority scheduling)

SQC: Monitor and control process quality using statistics.

Six Sigma: A methodology that aims for near-perfect quality (3.4 DPMO).

DMAIC: The structured problem-solving methodology of Six Sigma.

Control Chart: A statistical tool to monitor process variation over time.

Cp: Process capability ratio (potential capability). Cpk: Process capability index (actual, accounts for centering).

A hospital tracks patient wait times using X-bar charts. If a point falls outside UCL, the process is out of control and needs investigation.

📊 Control Chart Interpretation

When is a process "Out of Control"?

  • Any point beyond UCL or LCL (3-sigma limits)
  • 7 consecutive points on one side of the center line (run rule)
  • 7 consecutive points trending up or down
  • 2 out of 3 consecutive points beyond 2-sigma limits
  • 4 out of 5 consecutive points beyond 1-sigma limits

Types of Variation:

  • Common Cause: Random, inherent in the process (in control, expected)
  • Special Cause: Assignable, unexpected — indicates process change (out of control)

📐 Process Capability Example

Given: USL = 10.5 mm, LSL = 9.5 mm, Process Mean (mu) = 10.1 mm, Process Std Dev (sigma) = 0.12 mm

  • Cp = (10.5 - 9.5) / (6 x 0.12) = 1.0 / 0.72 = 1.39 (process can fit within specs)
  • Cpk = min[(10.5 - 10.1) / (3 x 0.12), (10.1 - 9.5) / (3 x 0.12)]
  • Cpk = min[0.4/0.36, 0.6/0.36] = min[1.11, 1.67] = 1.11
  • Interpretation: Cp > 1.33 is good; Cpk = 1.11 means process is capable but slightly off-center (mean shifted toward LSL)
  • Rule of Thumb: Cpk > 1.33 = capable, Cpk > 1.67 = excellent, Cpk < 1.0 = needs improvement

Unit 14: Maintenance Management

▶
Six Sigma Basics
▶
MS Project Tutorial
📖 Core Concepts

Maintenance Types: Breakdown (reactive), Preventive (scheduled), Predictive (condition-based), TPM (operators maintain own equipment).

OEE = Availability x Performance x Quality.

Availability = Uptime / Planned Production Time. Performance = Actual Output / Max Possible. Quality = Good Units / Total Units.

MTBF = Mean Time Between Failures (reliability). MTTR = Mean Time To Repair (maintainability).

Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR).

📖 Key Definitions

TPM: A system where operators are responsible for maintaining their own equipment.

OEE: A measure of how well a manufacturing operation is utilized.

MTBF: Average time between equipment failures.

MTTR: Average time to repair equipment after failure.

💡 Real-World Example

A factory's OEE = 90% Availability x 95% Performance x 99% Quality = 84.6%. World-class OEE is 85%+.

📐 OEE and Availability Numerical

OEE Example: A CNC machine operates for 480 min/shift. Setup takes 30 min, breakdowns lose 20 min. It produces 400 parts (rated speed: 1 part/min). 15 parts are defective.

  • Availability = (480 - 30 - 20) / 480 = 430/480 = 89.6%
  • Performance = 400 / 430 = 93.0%
  • Quality = (400 - 15) / 400 = 385/400 = 96.3%
  • OEE = 89.6% x 93.0% x 96.3% = 80.1% (below world-class 85%)

Availability with MTBF/MTTR: If MTBF = 200 hrs, MTTR = 4 hrs

  • Availability = 200 / (200 + 4) = 200/204 = 98.04%

⚠️ Common Exam Mistakes to Avoid
  • Cp vs Cpk: Don't confuse them! Cp = potential (spread), Cpk = actual (centering). Cpk is always less than or equal to Cp.
  • EOQ units: Ensure D, S, H are all in the same time period (annual vs monthly).
  • Exponential smoothing: Don't confuse alpha (weight on actual) with (1-alpha) (weight on forecast).
  • Chase vs Level: Chase changes workforce, Level changes inventory. Don't mix them up.
  • DMAIC vs DMADV: DMAIC = existing process, DMADV = new process/design. "I" = Improve existing, "D" = Design new.
  • Service characteristics: Inseparability (not simultaneity) — the official term matters in exams.
  • Process types: Job Shop ≠ Job Shop layout. Job Shop process = low volume, high variety. Process layout = grouping by function.
  • Control charts: UCL/LCL are NOT specification limits. They are process control limits (statistical).
