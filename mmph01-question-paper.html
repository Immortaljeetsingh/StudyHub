Solved Answers - MMPH-01 December 2023 Question Paper

  • Q1. Describe and evaluate classical and neo-classical approaches to understand organisations.

    Answer: Classical theory (Taylor, Fayol, Weber) treats the organisation as an efficient machine of rules and structure, while neo-classical theory (Mayo's Hawthorne studies) adds the human and social side.

    The classical approach (roughly 1900-1930) treats the organisation as a machine and focuses on efficiency, structure, and formal rules. It has three streams: Scientific Management (F.W. Taylor) with time-and-motion studies and the "one best way" of doing work; Administrative Theory (Henri Fayol) with his 14 principles such as division of work and unity of command; and Bureaucracy (Max Weber) with hierarchy, written rules, and impersonal decision-making. Its strengths are clarity of authority, specialisation, and discipline. Its weakness is that it ignores the human side - workers are treated as "economic men" whose feelings, groups, and emotions do not matter. The neo-classical approach (1930s onward) emerged from the Human Relations Movement. Elton Mayo's Hawthorne Experiments (1924-1932) proved that social factors - recognition, informal groups, and morale - affect productivity more than lighting or rest breaks, giving rise to the Hawthorne Effect. Neo-classical writers like Chester Barnard and Herbert Simon added psychology and decision-making to management thought. Its strength is that it rediscovered the human being at work; its weakness is that it overemphasises social factors and underestimates structure, power, and technology. Evaluation: for a modern answer, remember that both are partial - classical gives the skeleton (structure and efficiency) and neo-classical gives the flesh (people and relationships), so good organisations blend both. Maruti Suzuki's assembly lines show classical precision, while its work circles and recognition schemes reflect the neo-classical spirit.

    Key points to remember: Classical = Taylor, Fayol, Weber (efficiency, structure, rules); Neo-classical = Mayo's Hawthorne experiments (human relations); Classical ignores people, neo-classical ignores structure; blend both in real organisations.

    Takeaway: Blend classical structure with neo-classical people-sensitivity - each approach alone is only half the picture.

  • Q2. Identify the characteristics of decentralized autonomous organisations. Discuss with examples to justify your answer.

    Answer: A decentralized autonomous organisation pushes decision authority down to unit level, giving flatter structures, faster local decisions, and empowered, accountable managers.

    A decentralized autonomous organisation pushes decision-making authority down to lower levels and gives units independence to run themselves. Its main characteristics are: decision-making authority at unit or divisional level rather than headquarters; flatter structures with fewer layers of middle management; profit-centre autonomy, where each unit manages its own budgets, targets, and resources; local responsiveness, so decisions match local markets and conditions; empowered managers who are accountable for their own results; faster decision-making because nothing waits for head-office approval; and headquarters acting as a support or steering body rather than a controller. Decentralization works best when the environment is dynamic, the organisation is large and diversified, and decisions must be taken quickly at the point of action. The Godrej Group illustrates this: each business - home care, appliances, agrovet - operates semi-autonomously with its own board and CEO. Indian IT firms like Infosys give project teams the authority to plan and deliver client work independently, which is why they can respond to global clients rapidly. Zomato's vertical heads run delivery, dining, and Blinkit as separate units with their own KPIs. At the other extreme, Indian Railways is highly centralized, with major decisions flowing from Rail Bhavan. The justification for decentralization is simple: as companies grow and markets change, no single person at the top can know everything - autonomy at the edges creates speed, accountability, and leadership development.

    Key points to remember: Decentralized = authority pushed down, flatter structure, unit autonomy; gives speed and accountability; Godrej, Infosys, Zomato as examples; Indian Railways as the centralized contrast.

    Takeaway: Decentralization suits large, diversified firms in dynamic markets - authority should follow information.

  • Q3. Compare and describe different contemporary approaches to job design.

    Answer: Modern job design moves from the old engineering fit-the-worker approach to motivational, mechanistic, ergonomic and team-based designs like rotation, enlargement, enrichment and autonomous work teams.

    Job design is the way tasks, duties, and responsibilities are arranged into a job, and modern approaches go far beyond the old "fit the worker to the job" idea. The engineering approach, from Taylor's scientific management, simplifies jobs for maximum efficiency but makes work boring. The motivational approach, based on Herzberg and Hackman & Oldham, makes jobs interesting by adding responsibility and growth. Hackman and Oldham's Job Characteristics Model gives five core dimensions - skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and feedback (STTAF) - which create meaningfulness, responsibility, and knowledge of results. The mechanistic approach optimises behaviour through standardisation (used in routine mass production), while the biological and perceptual-motor approaches design jobs around ergonomics and human capacities, reducing fatigue and errors. Contemporary designs go further: job rotation moves employees laterally between tasks to reduce boredom; job enlargement adds more tasks of the same level (horizontal loading); job enrichment adds planning and control responsibility (vertical loading); and autonomous work teams let a group plan, execute, and evaluate its own work. For example, an IT company that lets engineers pick their projects, pair-program, and self-review is using enrichment plus teamwork, while a call centre using scripts and call quotas is closer to the mechanistic approach. The best design matches the job to both the person and the strategy - boring jobs for an innovative workforce fail as surely as overloaded ones.

    Key points to remember: Engineering vs motivational vs mechanistic vs ergonomic approaches; Hackman-Oldham STTAF dimensions; rotation (lateral), enlargement (horizontal), enrichment (vertical), autonomous teams; match design to people and strategy.

    Takeaway: Design the job to fit both the person and the strategy - the STTAF dimensions make work meaningful.

  • Q4. Evaluate the reasons for the emerging trends that occur in work organisation. Cite suitable examples.

    Answer: Technology, globalization, worker expectations, COVID-19, demographics and economics are reshaping work organisation into hybrid, gig, virtual and project-based forms.

    Work organisation is changing faster than ever, and there are clear reasons for each trend. Technology is the biggest driver: automation, AI, and cloud tools now allow work to happen anywhere, giving rise to remote and hybrid working - for example, a study of an IT firm found 43% of its 3,000 employees wanted to work from home even after offices reopened. Globalization makes organisations compete across time zones, so virtual teams and follow-the-sun work schedules are common in Indian software companies serving US and European clients. Changing worker expectations push organisations toward flexibility: gig and platform work (Zomato delivery partners, Swiggy riders, freelance developers) grew because people want income and flexibility without a permanent contract. Post-COVID learning: the pandemic proved that many jobs can be done remotely, so organisations now build digital-first processes, video-based training, and online performance reviews. Demographic and social shifts add diversity and inclusion goals, wellness programmes, and mental-health support - companies like Infosys now have mindfulness and sabbatical policies. Economic pressures push organisations toward flatter structures, outsourcing, and project-based teams that can be formed and disbanded quickly. Evaluation: these trends are not random - each is a rational response to technology, markets, or people. But they carry risks: gig workers lack job security, hybrid work can weaken culture and cohesion, and constant change causes burnout. Wise organisations adopt the trend but manage its side-effects.

    Key points to remember: Drivers = technology, globalization, worker expectations, COVID-19, demographics, economics; examples = hybrid work, gig economy, virtual teams; adopt trends but manage risks like insecurity and weak culture.

    Takeaway: Each trend is a rational response to technology, markets or people - adopt it but manage side-effects like insecurity and weak culture.

  • Q5. Describe and discuss the role of HR and identify the strategies to improve organisational effectiveness. Cite examples.

    Answer: HR attracts, develops, motivates and retains talent, and organisational effectiveness comes from aligning people's capability and motivation with the company's strategy.

    Organisational effectiveness means how well an organisation achieves its goals while using resources wisely. HR plays a central role because people create the products, serve the customers, and make the decisions. The role of HR has four parts: attracting talent (recruitment and selection), developing talent (training, career planning, mentoring), motivating talent (compensation, rewards, recognition), and retaining talent (engagement, welfare, healthy culture). HR also acts as the custodian of performance systems such as appraisal and feedback that tell employees what good looks like. Strategies to improve effectiveness include: hiring right the first time through competency-based selection, so capability gaps never start; building skills through continuous training tied to business needs; empowering employees by delegating authority and encouraging initiative, which speeds decisions; linking rewards to performance through incentives and appraisals, so effort follows strategy; fostering engagement through communication, recognition, and fair treatment, which reduces turnover; and planning succession so leadership pipelines never run dry. Examples: the Tata Group's Tata Business Excellence Model measures effectiveness at every level and its training academies develop managers systematically; Infosys uses campus hiring, innovation awards, and leadership programs to keep its human capital world-class. In short, an organisation is effective when its HR systems align people's capabilities and motivation with the company's strategy - and that alignment is a management job, not a formality.

    Key points to remember: HR roles = attract, develop, motivate, retain; strategies = competency hiring, training, empowerment, performance-linked rewards, engagement, succession; Tata and Infosys as examples; alignment of people with strategy = effectiveness.

    Takeaway: HR systems that align people's capability and motivation with strategy are the engine of organisational effectiveness.

  • Q6. Describe, compare and discuss any two theoretical frameworks to understand organizations. Justify your answer.

    Answer: The systems approach sees the organisation as an open input-transformation-output system with feedback, while contingency holds that structure must fit the environment, technology and size.

    Two powerful frameworks are the Systems Approach and the Contingency Approach. The systems approach, developed by Katz and Kahn (1966), views the organisation as an open system that takes inputs (raw materials, people, capital, information), transforms them through internal processes, and exports outputs (products, services) to the environment, with feedback looping back into the next cycle. Its key concepts are subsystems, synergy, entropy, and steady state - for example, a cement plant takes limestone and energy, converts them through kilns, sells cement, and uses sales data to adjust production. Its strength is that it shows how every department depends on others and on the environment; its weakness is that it is abstract and does not tell managers exactly what to do. The contingency approach holds that there is no one best way to organise: structure, leadership, and systems must fit the environment, technology, and size. Burns and Stalker showed that mechanistic structures suit stable environments while organic structures suit dynamic ones; Lawrence and Lorsch found that successful firms in turbulent industries were more differentiated and integrated. Its strength is realism and practical guidance; its weakness is that it offers principles but no precise prescriptions. Comparing them: systems describes how organisations work as wholes, contingency tells managers how to adapt; systems is descriptive, contingency is prescriptive. I justify preferring the contingency framework because managers need situational answers - a garment exporter and a software firm cannot be run with identical structures, as the systems view alone would suggest.

    Key points to remember: Systems = open system of inputs, transformation, outputs, feedback (Katz & Kahn); Contingency = no one best way, fit environment and technology (Burns & Stalker, Lawrence & Lorsch); systems describes, contingency prescribes; contingency wins for practical managers.

    Takeaway: Systems describes how organisations work as wholes; contingency prescribes how to adapt them - which is why contingency wins for practical managers.

  • Q7. Discuss any two perspectives to design an organization and their merits and demerits.

    Answer: The functional design groups by specialisation for depth but breeds silos, while the matrix uses dual chains of command for flexibility at the cost of conflict and stress.

    Two classic perspectives on organisation design are the functional (bureaucratic) design and the matrix design. In a functional design, people are grouped by specialisation - marketing, finance, production, HR - under a single chain of command. Its merits are specialisation and expertise, clear authority, simple career paths, and economies of scale: a company like Larsen & Toubro organises by function and gains deep technical excellence. Its demerits are slow decision-making across departments, poor coordination, and the "silo" problem where each function optimises itself rather than the whole. The matrix design overlaps two chains of command: functional managers and project managers, as used in ISRO, DRDO, and large IT projects. Its merits are flexibility, fast response to complex projects, efficient use of scarce specialists, and strong communication - engineers report to both their discipline head and their project leader. Its demerits are dual-boss confusion, conflict over authority, higher coordination costs, and stress on employees who must balance two masters. Comparing them: functional design suits stable, single-product organisations that value depth, while matrix design suits complex, project-based work that needs speed and coordination. There is no universal winner - design must follow strategy. A small manufacturer should stay functional; an engineering services firm handling multiple client projects will need the matrix, accepting its coordination costs in exchange for responsiveness.

    Key points to remember: Functional = specialisation, clear authority, but silos; Matrix = dual chains of command, flexibility, but conflict and stress; ISRO and L&T as examples; design must match strategy and task complexity.

    Takeaway: Design must follow strategy - functional for stable single-product firms, matrix for complex project work.

  • Q8. Describe formal and informal organisations. Discuss centralization and decentralization of organisational structures with their merits and demerits citing examples.

    Answer: A formal organisation is the official structure while an informal one is the social network; centralization concentrates authority at the top and decentralization disperses it down the line.

    A formal organisation is the official structure - the organogram, authority levels, rules, job descriptions, and reporting lines deliberately created by management. An informal organisation is the network of social relationships, friendships, groups, and the grapevine that grows naturally among employees, first noticed in the Hawthorne studies. Both exist in every company: the formal structure tells you who reports to whom, while the informal one tells you who actually influences decisions. Centralization means decision-making authority is concentrated at the top. Its merits are uniform policies, tight control, and quick decisions in a crisis; its demerits are overloaded top managers, slow responses to local issues, and demotivated lower staff. Indian Railways is a classic centralized example - major policy and even operational decisions flow from Rail Bhavan. Decentralization disperses authority to lower levels. Its merits are faster decisions, better local responsiveness, development of managers, and higher motivation; its demerits are inconsistency between units, loss of top-level control, and duplication of effort. The Godrej Group decentralizes by giving each business unit its own board and CEO. Balance is the key: complete centralization kills initiative, complete decentralization loses coordination. Modern practice decentralizes operational decisions and centralizes strategic ones - for example, Zomato lets vertical heads run their businesses (decentralized) while the group keeps brand and capital decisions (centralized). Both structures are legitimate; what matters is matching authority to the information and speed each decision requires.

    Key points to remember: Formal = official structure, informal = social networks and grapevine; centralized = control but slow and overloaded; decentralized = fast and motivated but inconsistent; Indian Railways vs Godrej; balance operations vs strategy.

    Takeaway: Decentralize operations, centralize strategy, and let both formal and informal structures coexist.
