Solved Assignment Questions & Answers

Q1. What is supply chain management? Explain the SCOR model.

Answer: Logistics is the management of the flow of goods from origin to consumption, while a supply chain is the network of organisations involved in producing and delivering products from raw material to the end customer. Supply chain management coordinates these activities to maximise customer value and achieve sustainable competitive advantage; logistics is a subset of SCM. The evolution ran from Physical Distribution Management (1960s) to Integrated Logistics (1980s) to SCM (2000s) to digital supply chains driven by AI, IoT, and blockchain. Three flows move through the chain: product flow (supplier to customer), information flow (bidirectional), and financial flow (customer to supplier). The SCOR model (Supply Chain Operations Reference) describes five processes, remembered as P-S-M-D-R: Plan (demand and supply planning), Source (supplier selection and procurement), Make (production and manufacturing), Deliver (order management, warehousing, and transportation), and Return (returns, repairs, and recycling). PDM components are T-W-I-O-P: transportation, warehousing, inventory, order processing, and packaging. Amazon's supply chain, with suppliers, fulfilment centres, carrier shipping, and real-time tracking, illustrates the complete framework.

Key points:

  • Logistics is the management of the flow of goods from origin to consumption, while a supply chain is the network of organisations involved in producing and delivering products from raw material to the end customer.
  • Supply chain management coordinates these activities to maximise customer value and achieve sustainable competitive advantage; logistics is a subset of SCM.
  • The SCOR model (Supply Chain Operations Reference) describes five processes, remembered as P-S-M-D-R: Plan (demand and supply planning), Source (supplier selection and procurement), Make (production and manufacturing), Deliver (order management, warehousing, and transportation), and Return (returns, repairs, and recycling).

Takeaway: The SCOR model (Supply Chain Operations Reference) describes five processes, remembered as P-S-M-D-R: Plan (demand and supply planning), Source (supplier selection and procurement), Make (production and manufacturing), Deliver (order management, warehousing, and transportation), and Return (returns, repairs, and recycling).

Q2. What is the bullwhip effect? Explain its causes and mitigation strategies.

Answer: The bullwhip effect is the phenomenon in which demand variability amplifies as it moves upstream in the supply chain: a stable customer demand becomes slight variation at the retailer, more variation at the distributor, and massive variation at the manufacturer. Its causes are remembered as D-O-P-R: demand forecasting errors (each level adds safety margin to forecasts), order batching (placing large orders infrequently), price fluctuations (promotions cause forward buying), and rationing and gaming (over-ordering during shortages). The mitigation strategies are I-V-E: information sharing (point-of-sale data shared upstream), VMI (vendor managed inventory), and EDLP (every day low pricing). The COVID-19 pandemic produced an extreme bullwhip effect: consumers hoarded toilet paper, retailers ordered two to five times normal, and distributors ordered even more, causing massive upstream demand spikes. Key supply chain metrics include on-time delivery (on-time orders divided by total orders), fill rate, inventory turnover (COGS divided by average inventory), and cash-to-cash cycle. Indian benchmarks: Flipkart achieves 95% on-time delivery outside sale periods and Delhivery processes 2.5 million packages daily with 99.5% tracking accuracy.

Key points:

  • The bullwhip effect is the phenomenon in which demand variability amplifies as it moves upstream in the supply chain: a stable customer demand becomes slight variation at the retailer, more variation at the distributor, and massive variation at the manufacturer.
  • Indian benchmarks: Flipkart achieves 95% on-time delivery outside sale periods and Delhivery processes 2.5 million packages daily with 99.5% tracking accuracy.
  • The mitigation strategies are I-V-E: information sharing (point-of-sale data shared upstream), VMI (vendor managed inventory), and EDLP (every day low pricing).

Q3. Compare push and pull supply chain strategies with suitable examples.

Answer: A push system produces based on forecasts, making products to stock; MRP systems and traditional manufacturing are examples. It carries high inventory, offers low flexibility, and risks overproduction, but benefits from economies of scale. A pull system produces based on actual demand, making products to order; JIT and Kanban systems, as in the Toyota Production System, are examples. It carries minimal inventory, offers high flexibility, and risks stockouts. The leagile strategy combines lean (upstream) and agile (downstream) approaches, using a decoupling point where forecast-driven production meets demand-driven production. Dell is the classic push-pull example: components are pushed based on forecasts, but final assembly is pulled by customer orders, with the decoupling point at assembly. In India, Flipkart uses a hybrid model: fast-moving items are pushed to regional warehouses based on forecasts, while slow-moving or high-value items are pulled from central fulfilment centres when orders arrive; during Big Billion Days it pre-positions inventory at over 1,400 fulfilment hubs. Choose push for stable, predictable demand and pull for volatile, customised demand; most modern chains are hybrid.

Key points:

  • A push system produces based on forecasts, making products to stock; MRP systems and traditional manufacturing are examples.
  • A pull system produces based on actual demand, making products to order; JIT and Kanban systems, as in the Toyota Production System, are examples.
  • It carries high inventory, offers low flexibility, and risks overproduction, but benefits from economies of scale.

Takeaway: Choose push for stable, predictable demand and pull for volatile, customised demand; most modern chains are hybrid.

Q4. Explain the five transportation modes and the concept of intermodal transportation.

Answer: The five transportation modes are remembered as R-R-A-W-P: road, rail, air, water, and pipeline. Road offers medium speed and cost with high flexibility and is best for door-to-door delivery. Rail is slow and cheap with high capacity but low flexibility, suited to bulk and long-distance movement. Air is fast and expensive with low capacity, used for high-value, time-sensitive goods. Water is slow and cheapest with the highest capacity and lowest flexibility, ideal for bulk international trade. Pipeline is continuous, cost-effective, but limited to oil, gas, and chemicals with no flexibility. Mode selection depends on cost, speed, reliability, flexibility, and capability. Intermodal transportation combines two or more modes, for example road to a port or rail hub, rail or ship for the long haul, and road again for last-mile delivery; Amazon ships by rail or air from warehouses to regional hubs and then by truck to customers. Indian examples: the Dedicated Freight Corridors cut Delhi-Mumbai freight transit from 14 days to 14 hours, CONCOR operates 84 inland container depots handling 3.5 million TEUs annually, and Delhivery uses air for same-day metro delivery, rail for Tier-2 bulk shipments, and road for Tier-3 and Tier-4 last-mile delivery.

Key points:

  • The five transportation modes are remembered as R-R-A-W-P: road, rail, air, water, and pipeline.
  • Intermodal transportation combines two or more modes, for example road to a port or rail hub, rail or ship for the long haul, and road again for last-mile delivery; Amazon ships by rail or air from warehouses to regional hubs and then by truck to customers.
  • Road offers medium speed and cost with high flexibility and is best for door-to-door delivery.
