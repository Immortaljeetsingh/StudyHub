Q1. Explain the marketing mix (4Ps) and the 4As framework with Indian examples.

Answer: The marketing mix, or the 4Ps, describes the controllable variables a firm combines to satisfy its target customers: Product (features, quality, branding and packaging), Price (pricing strategy, discounts and credit terms), Place (distribution channels, coverage and logistics) and Promotion (advertising, personal selling and sales promotion). Kotler defines marketing as the art and science of choosing target markets and getting, keeping and growing customers through creating, delivering and communicating superior customer value. For emerging markets like India, Jagdish Sheth's 4As framework is often more relevant: Attraction (does the product attract the customer?), Affordability (can the customer pay for it?), Accessibility (is it available where the customer shops?) and Awareness (does the customer know it exists?). Hindustan Unilever demonstrates all four: Surf Excel's emotional advertising attracts mothers, Project Shakti's 136,000 women entrepreneurs make products accessible in 600,000+ villages, single-use sachets make them affordable, and long-running campaigns build awareness. Amul similarly combines affordable pricing with cooperative cold-chain distribution and topical advertising. Indian managers should blend the 4Ps with the 4As to convert product features into customer value.

Key points:

  • The marketing mix, or the 4Ps, describes the controllable variables a firm combines to satisfy its target customers: Product (features, quality, branding and packaging), Price (pricing strategy, discounts and credit terms), Place (distribution channels, coverage and logistics) and Promotion (advertising, personal selling and sales promotion).
  • For emerging markets like India, Jagdish Sheth's 4As framework is often more relevant: Attraction (does the product attract the customer?), Affordability (can the customer pay for it?), Accessibility (is it available where the customer shops?) and Awareness (does the customer know it exists?).
  • Kotler defines marketing as the art and science of choosing target markets and getting, keeping and growing customers through creating, delivering and communicating superior customer value.

Takeaway: Indian managers should blend the 4Ps with the 4As to convert product features into customer value.

Q2. Discuss the PESTEL factors with reference to Reliance Jio's 2016 launch.

Answer: The macro environment comprises six forces analysed through PESTEL: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal. Reliance Jio's 2016 launch is a masterclass in reading all six factors correctly. Politically, the Digital India programme created a favourable policy environment for telecom expansion. Economically, smartphone penetration was rising rapidly, but data tariffs of about Rs.250 per GB kept most Indians offline — a gap Jio attacked with free data and voice. Socially, a young population was hungry for digital content such as videos and music. Technologically, 4G infrastructure was ready but underutilised, allowing Jio to leapfrog older networks. Environmentally, digital services reduce paper and transport needs, aligning with sustainability goals. Legally, TRAI's pro-consumer regulations encouraged new entrants and fair competition. Because Jio scanned every factor and acted on the interconnections — political support, rising affordability and digital appetite reinforcing one another — it gained 100 million subscribers in 170 days and transformed Indian telecom. The lesson for marketers is that PESTEL is not a checklist to memorise but a framework for spotting opportunities where several forces reinforce each other.

Key points:

  • The macro environment comprises six forces analysed through PESTEL: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal.

  • Politically, the Digital India programme created a favourable policy environment for telecom expansion.

Q3. Explain the STP framework. How has Maruti Suzuki applied segmentation, targeting and positioning in India?

Answer: STP is the foundation of marketing strategy. Segmentation divides the market into distinct groups sharing characteristics; targeting selects the segments the firm will serve; positioning creates a clear, distinctive place for the brand in customers' minds. The four segmentation bases are geographic (region, urban-rural), demographic (age, income, gender), psychographic (lifestyle, values) and behavioral (usage rate, loyalty, benefits sought), and segments must satisfy the MASA criteria — measurable, accessible, substantial and actionable. Maruti Suzuki applies the framework across the Indian car market. It segments on geography (urban versus rural), income (Rs.3-5 lakh, Rs.5-10 lakh and above Rs.10 lakh) and behavior (first-time buyers versus upgraders). It then follows a differentiated targeting strategy: Alto for the budget segment, Swift for the youth, Baleno for the premium hatchback buyer and Brezza for SUV seekers. Each model is positioned distinctly — Alto on fuel efficiency ("Kitna Deti Hai?"), Swift on youthful energy and Baleno as the new face of premium. The result is that each Maruti model occupies a separate mental slot, allowing the company to serve many segments without cannibalising itself.

Key points:

  • STP is the foundation of marketing strategy.
  • Maruti Suzuki applies the framework across the Indian car market.
  • Segmentation divides the market into distinct groups sharing characteristics; targeting selects the segments the firm will serve; positioning creates a clear, distinctive place for the brand in customers' minds.

Takeaway: The result is that each Maruti model occupies a separate mental slot, allowing the company to serve many segments without cannibalising itself.

Q4. Describe the consumer decision-making process. What role did Cash on Delivery play in Flipkart's success?

Answer: The consumer decision-making process has five stages: need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision and post-purchase behavior. A stimulus, internal or external, triggers need recognition; the consumer then searches memory and external sources, compares alternatives on key attributes, decides to buy, and finally experiences satisfaction or cognitive dissonance. The depth of the process depends on involvement: extensive problem solving for high-involvement purchases such as a house or an MBA admission, limited problem solving for moderate items like a smartphone, and habitual decision making for routine groceries. Flipkart's success shows how understanding these stages pays off. Indian consumers had high perceived risk about online payments — a psychological barrier at the purchase stage. Flipkart responded with Cash on Delivery, which quickly accounted for about 60% of early orders, removing the payment risk. It also addressed the cultural preference for "touch and feel" with a 30-day return policy, and used referral bonuses to harness word-of-mouth during the information-search stage. By redesigning the decision journey around Indian behavioral realities, Flipkart built trust and became the country's leading e-commerce platform.

Key points:

  • The consumer decision-making process has five stages: need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision and post-purchase behavior.
  • The depth of the process depends on involvement: extensive problem solving for high-involvement purchases such as a house or an MBA admission, limited problem solving for moderate items like a smartphone, and habitual decision making for routine groceries.
  • A stimulus, internal or external, triggers need recognition; the consumer then searches memory and external sources, compares alternatives on key attributes, decides to buy, and finally experiences satisfaction or cognitive dissonance.
