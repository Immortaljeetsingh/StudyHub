    (b) List the main components of current assets and explain why cash is called the 'lubricant of business'. (5)

  5. (a) Discuss the motives for holding cash and explain the Baumol model of cash management. (10)

Q1. Working capital: gross vs net, permanent vs temporary

Answer: Working capital is the capital locked in short-term assets that convert to cash within one operating cycle, and it is studied as gross vs net and permanent vs temporary.

Working capital is the capital invested in short-term assets that can be converted into cash within one year (one operating cycle), such as inventory, receivables and cash. Gross working capital is the total value of current assets, while net working capital is current assets minus current liabilities — a positive figure means the firm can pay its short-term obligations from its own current resources.

Permanent (fixed) working capital is the minimum level of current assets needed to keep the business running at all times, even in the slack season; it grows with the scale of the business and is financed from long-term sources. Temporary (variable) working capital is the extra current assets needed only during peak seasons, festivals or special operations, and it fluctuates with production and sales.

Example: a textile mill needs Rs. 50 lakh of current assets all year (permanent) but Rs. 75 lakh during the wedding season (temporary Rs. 25 lakh).

Components of current assets: cash, bank balances, marketable securities, inventory (raw material, WIP, finished goods), and accounts receivable.

Key points: Gross WC = total current assets; Net WC = current assets − current liabilities; permanent WC = minimum always needed; temporary WC = seasonal extra; too little WC risks insolvency, too much idle money hurts profitability.

Takeaway: Write 'gross = total current assets, net = current assets minus current liabilities, permanent = minimum always needed, temporary = seasonal extra', name the five components, and close with the insolvency-versus-idle-money risk.

Q2. Working capital cycle, cash conversion cycle + numerical

Answer: The operating cycle is the Cash → Inventory → Receivables → Cash journey, and the cash conversion cycle (CCC = ICP + RCP − PDP) measures how many days that cash stays locked up.

The working capital (operating) cycle is the time between paying cash for raw material and collecting cash from customers — the journey Cash → Inventory → Receivables → Cash (CIRO). Shorter cycles need less working capital.

The Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) = Inventory Conversion Period + Receivables Collection Period − Payables Deferral Period. It tells us how many days cash is locked up in operations.

Numerical (part b): CCC = 60 + 45 − 30 = 75 days.
Working capital required = (Annual cost of goods sold × CCC) ÷ 365
= (18,00,000 × 75) ÷ 365 = 1,35,00,000 ÷ 365 ≈ Rs. 36,986.

So the firm needs about Rs. 37,000 of working capital to fund one operating cycle; it shrinks if suppliers give longer credit or customers pay faster. The CCC is significant because it directly tells the finance manager how many days of funding are needed and where to focus: reducing debtor days or stock days releases cash, while pushing out payables lowers the amount of bank finance required.

Key points: CCC = ICP + RCP − PDP; WC = cost of goods × CCC/365; longer cycle = more capital locked; the mnemonic is CIRO — Cash to Inventory to Receivables to Cash.

Takeaway: Apply CCC = 60 + 45 − 30 = 75 days, then working capital = COGS × CCC / 365 ≈ Rs 36,986, and explain that shorter cycles need less capital.

Q3. Determinants of working capital + liquidity-profitability trade-off

Answer: Working capital needs are set by nature, size, cycle length, seasonality, credit policy, growth and inflation — and managing it is a constant trade-off between liquidity and profitability.

Working capital needs depend on many factors. Nature of business: a trading firm needs more WC than a service firm because it carries inventory. Size of business: bigger scale, more WC. Operating cycle length: longer cycle, more WC. Seasonality: festive-season firms need temporary WC. Credit policy: liberal credit to customers and tough terms from suppliers raise WC needs. Growth and inflation: expanding firms and rising prices need more funds. Other factors: production policy, dividend policy, taxes, and the firm's ability to access bank finance (cash credit, overdraft).

The liquidity-profitability trade-off is the core tension of WC management: holding more cash and inventory (high liquidity) keeps the firm safe but earns little, while cutting current assets (high profitability) risks running out of cash and losing suppliers' or customers' trust. The optimum is the level that keeps costs of both liquidity and shortage minimum.

Example: a supermarket keeping 90 days of stock has high liquidity but loses interest; one keeping 7 days of stock earns more but may face stock-outs on promotion days.

Key points: determinants = nature, size, cycle, seasonality, credit policy, growth, inflation; too little WC = risk of failure, too much = low return; management aims for the balanced middle.

Takeaway: List the determinants with an example each, then state the trade-off plainly: too little liquidity risks failure, too much earns nothing, so aim for the minimum-cost middle.

Q4. Receivables management + cash discount numerical

Answer: Receivables management balances the profit from liberal credit against the cost of funds locked in debtors, and rests on credit policy, the 5 C's of credit and graded collection effort.

Receivables management balances the profit from liberal credit against the cost of funds locked in debtors. It has three parts: credit policy (who gets credit and on what terms), credit analysis — the 5 C's of credit (Character, Capacity, Capital, Collateral, Conditions), and collection effort (polite reminders to legal action).

Numerical (part b): Savings = credit sales × days reduced ÷ 365 × interest rate = 36,00,000 × 15/365 × 15% = 1,47,945 × 0.15 ≈ Rs. 22,192.
Cost of discount (assume 60% customers avail) = 36,00,000 × 0.60 × 2% = Rs. 43,200.
Since cost (43,200) exceeds savings (22,192), the discount is not worthwhile at this rate; the firm should offer a smaller discount or extend the payment window.

Collection efforts should be prompt and graded: reminders, phone calls, personal visits, and finally stopping supplies or taking legal recourse.

Key points: 5 C's = Character, Capacity, Capital, Collateral, Conditions; compare cost of discount with interest saved before offering one; ageing schedules help chase old debtors.

Takeaway: Quote the 5 C's, then compare the discount cost (Rs 43,200) with the interest saved (Rs 22,192) — at this rate the discount is not worthwhile.

Q5. Motives for cash + Baumol model numerical

Answer: Firms hold cash for transaction, precautionary and speculative motives, and the Baumol model finds the optimum balance where transaction cost meets opportunity cost.

Keynes identified three motives for holding cash: the transaction motive (paying suppliers, salaries, taxes), the precautionary motive (buffer for emergencies) and the speculative motive (grab bargain opportunities). The Baumol model treats cash like inventory: the optimum balance is where transaction costs and opportunity (interest) costs balance.

Numerical (part b): Baumol optimum cash = √(2 × Annual cash requirement × Cost per transaction ÷ Interest rate)
= √(2 × 12,00,000 × 150 ÷ 0.10). Let us compute step by step:

  • 2 × 12,00,000 × 150 = 36,00,00,000
  • 36,00,00,000 ÷ 0.10 = 36,00,00,00,000
  • √36,00,00,00,000 = Rs. 60,000

So the company should convert securities to cash in lots of Rs. 60,000, making 12,00,000 ÷ 60,000 = 20 transfers a year. The model shows the central trade-off: smaller lots reduce interest forgone but increase transaction costs, while larger lots do the reverse. When cash flows are uncertain, managers add the Miller-Orr model, which sets upper and lower control limits instead of a single optimum.

Key points: motives = transaction, precautionary, speculative; Baumol optimum = √(2 × C × T / i); the model assumes steady, predictable cash use — add the Miller-Orr model when flows are uncertain.

Takeaway: Name the three motives, then apply Baumol: √(2 × 12,00,000 × 150 / 0.10) = Rs 60,000 — 20 transfers a year — and note Miller-Orr as the uncertain-cash-flow extension.

Q6. Inventory management, EOQ model + numerical

Answer: Inventory management aims to hold just enough stock, and the EOQ model picks the order size at which ordering cost equals carrying cost.

Inventory is often the biggest current asset, so inventory management aims to hold just enough stock — not too much (blocked funds, storage and obsolescence costs) and not too little (stock-outs and lost sales). The EOQ (Economic Order Quantity) is the order size that minimises total of ordering cost and carrying cost. It assumes constant, known demand; no lead-time uncertainty; and constant order and carrying costs.

Numerical (part b): EOQ = √(2 × Annual demand × Ordering cost ÷ Carrying cost per unit)
Carrying cost per unit = 20% of Rs. 10 = Rs. 2
EOQ = √(2 × 10,000 × 50 ÷ 2) = √(10,00,000 ÷ 2) = √5,00,000 ≈ 707 units.

So the firm should order about 707 units at a time, which works out to roughly 14 orders per year (10,000 ÷ 707). At this order size, annual ordering cost (14 × 50 = Rs. 700) equals carrying cost, giving the minimum total cost.

Related tools in practice: ABC analysis (classify stock by value), reorder point, safety stock and Just-in-Time.

Key points: EOQ = √(2 × D × O / C); at EOQ, ordering cost = carrying cost; holding too much inventory locks working capital, too little causes stock-outs.

Takeaway: Work EOQ = √(2 × 10,000 × 50 / 2) ≈ 707 units — about 14 orders a year — and say at EOQ ordering cost equals carrying cost; add ABC and JIT as practice tools.
