Unit 1: Marketing Research — Concepts & Process

📖 Unit 1: Marketing Research Fundamentals

Introduces the meaning, need, and importance of marketing research, the research process, scope of applications, limitations, and the user's perspective on research usefulness.

▶
Marketing Research Process

Point 1: Need for Marketing Research

  • Marketing managers need information to plan and implement strategies. Pingali's 4Cs: Customer needs, Competition, Cost, Context.
  • MIS vs. Marketing Research: MIS uses existing data (continuous); Marketing Research generates new information (project-based).
  • Marketing research is required when decision-makers face uncertainty and risk.
  • Conditions for conducting research: (a) Research can fill the information gap, (b) Cost of research < Cost of wrong decision, (c) Time for research is available.
💡 Indian Example

FMCG Company: A branded cookie company notices declining market share in a region. Marketing research is commissioned to understand why customers are switching to competitors — this is a classic trigger for research (uncertainty about cause).

🧠 Memory Aid

"MR is needed when RUC" — Risk is high, Uncertainty exists, and Cost of wrong decision is significant.

⚠ Exam Tip

When asked "Why is marketing research needed?", always structure your answer around three conditions: (1) Decision-maker faces uncertainty, (2) Research can reduce the uncertainty, and (3) Cost of research is justified by the value of the decision. Add real-world examples for each condition.

💡 Indian Example — Why Research is Needed

Amul's Entry into Chocolate Market: Amul faced uncertainty about whether Indian consumers would accept an Indian chocolate brand over established players like Cadbury. Marketing research was needed to: (a) understand chocolate consumption patterns, (b) identify price points, (c) determine distribution strategy, and (d) evaluate brand positioning. The cost of research (Rs 50 lakh) was justified by the potential market (Rs 15,000 crore Indian chocolate market).

Point 2: Definition & Meaning of Marketing Research

  • Marketing Research is the objective and formal process of collecting information, analyzing results, and communicating findings for marketing decisions.
  • It is systematic — all stages follow a logical sequence.
  • Includes both quantitative (surveys, experiments) and qualitative (focus groups, depth interviews) methods.
  • Establishes an information channel between the consumer and the company.
📑 Definitions by Scholars
  • American Marketing Association: "Marketing research links the consumer, customer, and public to the marketer through information"
  • Green & Tull: "Marketing research is the systematic and objective search for and analysis of data relevant to the identification and solution of any problem in the field of marketing"
  • Philip Kotler: "Marketing research is systematic problem analysis, model building, and fact-finding for the purpose of improved decision-making and control in the marketing of goods and services"
⚙ Characteristics of Good MR
  • Systematic: Follows a logical, step-by-step process
  • Objective: Unbiased data collection and analysis
  • Scientific: Uses proven methods and tools
  • Comprehensive: Covers all relevant aspects
  • Timely: Information available when needed for decisions
  • Ethical: Respects respondent privacy and consent
💡 Indian Example

Nielsen India: Nielsen is one of the largest MR firms operating in India. They track FMCG sales across 6 million outlets using retail audits. Their data helps companies like HUL, P&G, and ITC understand market share, distribution gaps, and pricing effectiveness. Nielsen's "Total Consumer View" combines purchase data, media consumption, and consumer attitudes for a 360-degree market view.

Point 4: Scope of Marketing Research

📊 Sales Analysis
  • Market potential & demand projection
  • Market characteristics & share estimation
  • Business trends & competitive analysis
  • Consumer buying habits & brand loyalty
💼 Sales Methods & Policies
  • Evaluating distribution effectiveness
  • Sales territories & quotas
  • Compensation design
  • Promotional activity assessment
🏭 Product Management
  • Competitive product feedback
  • Market segmentation

Point 5: Limitations of Marketing Research

  • Does not guarantee success — it indicates current conditions, not the future
  • Surrogate Information Error: Researching the wrong variable (e.g., Nestle Maggi initially researched food substitution, not the convenience need)
  • Time & Cost Trade-off: Research is expensive; sometimes a quick guesstimate is more practical
  • Confidentiality Risk: Competitors may observe field research and copy the idea faster
  • Not needed for small decisions: Many small companies operate successfully without formal research
💡 Indian Example

Maggi Noodles (1982): Nestle's initial research found Indians were not comfortable substituting traditional food. But they underestimated the convenience need of working women. The product succeeded despite the research finding — a classic "surrogate information" error.

Point 6: User's Perspective on Research

  • Estimate vs. Guesstimate: When time is limited, managers may need to choose between research-based estimates and quick guesstimates
  • Researcher should own the process: Decision-maker identifies the problem; researcher conducts the study
  • Be critical: Decision-makers should question the research process to ensure validity

✅ Unit 1 Summary

  • Marketing research is the systematic collection and analysis of information for marketing decisions
  • Needed when decision-makers face uncertainty and risk; cost of research must be < cost of wrong decision
  • The 11-step research process starts with problem definition (most critical step)
  • Scope includes: sales analysis, product management, advertising research, corporate research
  • Limitations: no guarantee of success, surrogate information error, time/cost trade-offs

Point 5: Ethical Issues in Marketing Research

✅ DOs
  • Obtain informed consent from participants
  • Maintain anonymity and confidentiality
  • Ensure transparency in research purpose and methodology
  • Cite the research firm and funding source
  • Use appropriate methods for the data type needed
❌ DON'Ts
  • Don't ask for confidential respondent information
  • Don't dictate methods to research professionals
  • Don't ask for quantitative data from qualitative studies
  • Don't conduct primary research when secondary data exists
  • Don't use deceptive practices to influence responses

Point 6: Problems in Conducting MR in India

  • Linguistic Diversity: 22 official languages, 400+ dialects; questionnaires must be translated into 5-6 languages minimum
  • Rural Accessibility: 70% rural population; rural market growing 5x faster than urban
  • High Attrition: ~30% annual turnover in MR firms due to low salaries
  • Quality Concerns: 58% of businesses feel MR quality falls short of expectations
  • Time Delays: 42% of managers feel MR "takes too long to be of real use"
  • Fraud Risk: Interviewers may demonstrate "straightlining" or steer interviews for quick completion

Rule 1: Speak their language — Translate into local languages, use simple vocabulary
Rule 2: Don't get lost in translation — Back-translate to verify accuracy
Rule 3: Respect culture — Adapt questions to local customs and sensitivities
Rule 4: Don't always rely on technology — Rural India still needs face-to-face methods
Rule 5: Beware of fraud —> Implement quality checks, GPS tracking, call-back verification

Point 6b: Government of India & Market Research

  • NSSO (National Sample Survey Office): Conducts large-scale household surveys
  • CMIE (Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy): Provides economic and business data
  • RBI Surveys: Consumer confidence, inflation expectations, industrial outlook
  • Government data is a valuable secondary source for marketing researchers
💡 Indian Example

PLFS (Periodic Labour Force Survey): Conducted by NSSO, provides data on employment, unemployment, and labour force participation. Marketing researchers use this data to understand consumer purchasing power, employment trends, and demographic shifts for market sizing and segmentation.

Point 1: Decision Area vs. Research Problem

  • Decision Area: The marketing manager's problem — what to do in a specific situation (e.g., declining market share).
  • Research Problem: Finding the underlying causes and information needed to make the best decision.
  • A symptom (declining sales) is NOT the problem — the true problem lies in pricing, promotion, availability, brand acceptance, etc.
💡 Indian Example

Organic Farm Retailer: A farmer selling organic vegetables faces low demand. The symptom is low sales. The decision area is whether to continue organic farming or switch back. The research problem is understanding: Who buys organic? Why? What price are they willing to pay? How can awareness be increased?

🧠 Memory Aid

"A problem well defined is a problem half-solved" — The most important principle in marketing research. Problem definition is the foundation of the entire research process.

Point 2: Converting Decision Problems to Research Problems

📊 Three Methods of Conversion
1. Interaction Method
Q&A between researcher and decision-maker
2. Decision Tree Approach
Hierarchical breakdown of the problem
3. Conceptualization
Breaking concepts into measurable variables

Point 3: Interaction Method

  • Used when the decision-maker cannot clearly state the problem.
  • Researcher asks general questions: "Why do you need research?" "What do you know about the situation?"
  • Progressively deeper questions narrow down to specific research objectives.
  • A "dummy test" helps prioritize research objectives and may reveal the decision-maker needs to rethink the decision area.

Point 4: Decision Tree Approach

  • A hierarchical model where information is sorted by connected nodes.
  • Starts with basic questions and breaks them down into specifics.
  • Particularly helpful for complex multistage decision problems.
💡 Indian Example — Tata Steel GalvaRoS

Decision Area: Create a green ecosystem for MSME buyers of coated steel products.
Decision Tree: Segments (Ducting, HVAC, Cable Tray, Appliances) → Sub-segments → Influencers → Strategy (awareness, interest, desire, action) → Information needs (channel, communication, buying process).

Point 5: Conceptualization

  • Breaking down a concept into measurable variables and identifying relationships between them.
  • Two approaches: (a) Applying existing concepts, (b) Developing new concepts.
Applying Existing Concepts
  • AIDA Model applied to environmental consciousness study:
  • Awareness: What is environment? What causes damage?
  • Interest: Possible actions, why protect?
  • Desire: Attitudes toward protection
  • Action: What are you currently doing?
Developing New Concepts
  • DLA Framework for online vs. offline MBA:
  • Delivery, Learning, Acceptance
  • Variables: Institute (facilities, resources), Faculty (comfort, preference), Students (connectivity, motivation), Recruiters (perceptions)
⚠ Exam Tip

In exams, always clearly distinguish between decision area (what to do) and research problem (what information is needed). Use the Tata Steel or Garage Butler case to illustrate your answer for extra marks.

✅ Unit 3 Summary

  • Decision area = manager's problem; Research problem = underlying causes and information needs
  • Three conversion methods: Interaction, Decision Tree, Conceptualization
  • Interaction method uses progressive questioning to narrow down research objectives
  • Decision trees break complex problems into hierarchical nodes
  • Conceptualization uses existing (AIDA) or new (DLA) frameworks to identify variables

Point 1: Research Design

  • A research design is a framework or blueprint for conducting a marketing research project.
  • It details the procedures for obtaining information needed to address the research problem.
  • A good design ensures the project is conducted effectively and efficiently.
📊 Types of Research Design
  • Exploratory: Discover insights when problem is unclear (literature review, expert interviews)
  • Descriptive: Describe market characteristics (surveys, panels)
  • Causal: Establish cause-and-effect relationships (experiments)
⚙ Classification by Causality
  • Exploratory: Low structure, flexible
  • Descriptive: Pre-planned structure, specific hypotheses
  • Causal: Highest structure, tests specific cause-effect relationships
Design TypePurposeMethodsWhen to Use
ExploratoryGain insights, clarify ambiguous problemsLiterature review, expert interviews, focus groups, pilot studiesWhen the problem is not well understood
DescriptiveDescribe market characteristics or functionsSurveys, panels, observation, structured questionnairesWhen specific questions need quantified answers
CausalDetermine cause-and-effect relationshipsExperiments (lab, field), A/B testingWhen testing specific hypotheses about causation
🧠 Memory Aid

Research Design Types: "EDC" — Exploratory (what's happening?), Descriptive (how much/many?), Causal (does X cause Y?).

Point 2: Concept and Meaning of Hypotheses

  • A hypothesis is a conjectural statement of the relationship between two or more variables (Kerlinger, 1986).
  • It is written so it can be proven or disproven by valid and reliable data (Grinnell, 1993).
  • Hypotheses are always in declarative statement form (not questions).
  • They narrow the scope of data collection compared to broad research questions.
💡 Example

Research Question: Identify the factors influencing salesperson performance.
Hypothesis: Marital status of salesperson has significant effect on sales performance.
The hypothesis focuses data collection on one specific variable relationship rather than many.

Point 3: Characteristics of a Good Hypothesis

  • Simple & Declarative: Clear, explicit statement testing one relationship at a time
  • Measurable & Quantifiable: Must be expressible in terms that can be statistically tested
  • Grounded in Knowledge: Based on existing theory, not intuition or subjective judgment
  • Testable: If it cannot be measured, it cannot be tested, and no conclusions can be drawn
⚠ Exam Tip

A hypothesis must always be testable and falsifiable. If there's no method to verify it, it's useless for research. However, new methods may emerge during research to test it.

Point 4: Importance of Hypotheses in Marketing Research

  1. Provides Focus: Narrows the study to specific facets, preventing data overload
  2. Prevents Delusion: Keeps researchers from finding "interesting" but irrelevant patterns
  3. Framework for Questions: Helps design focused survey questions that test specific relationships
  4. Decision Support: Translates research objectives into testable propositions for decision-making

Point 5: Types of Hypotheses

Null Hypothesis (H₀)
  • States there is no relationship between variables
  • Always tested for possible rejection
  • Example: "There is no significant difference in sales performance between trained and untrained salespeople"
Alternative Hypothesis (H₁)
  • States there is a relationship between variables
  • Accepted when null hypothesis is rejected
  • Example: "Trained salespeople perform significantly better than untrained ones"

Point 7: Types of Errors in Hypothesis Testing

Error TypeDescriptionAlso CalledExample
Type I ErrorRejecting a true null hypothesis (false positive)Alpha (α) errorConcluding a new ad campaign works when it actually doesn't
Type II ErrorAccepting a false null hypothesis (false negative)Beta (β) errorConcluding a new ad campaign doesn't work when it actually does
🧠 Memory Aid

Type I = "False Alarm" (rejected H₀ when it was true). Type II = "Missed Detection" (accepted H₀ when it was false). Think: "Type I is like convicting an innocent person; Type II is like letting a guilty person go free."

📐 Case Study: Hypothesis Testing in Indian Retail

Decision Area: A leading Indian retail chain wants to know if extending store hours from 9 PM to 11 PM will increase revenue.
H₀: Extended store hours do not significantly increase revenue.
H₁: Extended store hours significantly increase revenue.
Method: Selected 20 stores in metros, extended hours for 3 months, compared revenue with control stores.
Result: H₀ rejected at 95% confidence. Revenue increased by 18% in extended-hour stores. Decision: Roll out extended hours across all metro stores.

Point 8: Data Analysis Techniques in Marketing Research

📊 Univariate Analysis
  • Analyzing a single variable at a time
  • Techniques: Frequency distributions, mean, median, mode, standard deviation
  • Example: What percentage of customers prefer online shopping?
📊 Bivariate Analysis
  • Analyzing relationship between two variables
  • Techniques: Cross-tabulation, correlation, t-test, chi-square
  • Example: Is there a relationship between age and brand preference?
📊 Multivariate Analysis
  • Analyzing relationships among three or more variables
  • Techniques: Regression, factor analysis, cluster analysis, discriminant analysis
  • Example: What factors (price, quality, brand image) best predict customer satisfaction?
📊 Qualitative Analysis
  • Analyzing non-numerical data (text, images, observations)
  • Techniques: Content analysis, thematic analysis, coding
  • Example: What themes emerge from customer reviews about a new product?
⚠ Exam Tip

In exams, always match the analysis technique to the research question type. Use univariate for descriptive questions, bivariate for relationship questions, and multivariate for predictive questions. Mention specific tests (t-test, chi-square, ANOVA) for bonus marks.

Point 9: Research Report Writing

  • Executive Summary: Brief overview of key findings and recommendations (1-2 pages)
  • Introduction: Background, problem statement, research objectives
  • Methodology: Research design, sampling, data collection methods
  • Findings: Data presentation with tables, charts, and statistical analysis
  • Conclusions: Interpretation of findings in context of research objectives
  • Recommendations: Actionable suggestions for decision-makers
  • Appendices: Questionnaire, raw data, detailed statistical outputs
🧠 Memory Aid

Report Structure: "EIMFCRA" — Executive summary, Introduction, Methodology, Findings, Conclusions, Recommendations, Appendices. Write the executive summary last, even though it appears first.

✅ Unit 4 Summary

  • Research design is the blueprint: Exploratory, Descriptive, or Causal
  • A hypothesis is a testable, declarative statement about variable relationships
  • Good hypotheses are simple, measurable, grounded in knowledge, and testable
  • Hypotheses provide focus, prevent delusion, and support decision-making
  • Null (H₀) vs. Alternative (H₁) hypotheses; Type I (false alarm) and Type II (missed detection) errors
  • The 5-step testing process: State hypotheses → Choose α → Select test → Calculate → Decide

📖 Simple Linear Regression

Y = b₀ + b₁X + u
Y = Dependent variable (what we predict) | X = Independent variable (what we use to predict)
b₀ = Intercept | b₁ = Slope (change in Y for unit change in X) | u = Error term

📖 R² (Coefficient of Determination)

Proportion of variance in Y explained by X. Range: 0 to 1 (or 0% to 100%). R² = 0.75 means X explains 75% of variation in Y.

💡 Real-World Example

Regression: Sales = 100 + 5 × Advertising Spend. For every Rs 1 lakh increase in advertising, sales increase by Rs 5 lakhs. R² = 0.80 means advertising explains 80% of sales variation.

⚠ Exam Tip

Interpret the slope (b₁) in business terms, not just mathematically. Examiners want applied interpretation.

✅ Unit 8 Summary

  • Correlation (r): -1 to +1, measures strength and direction
  • Regression: Y = b₀ + b₁X + u, predicts Y from X
  • R² = proportion of variance explained (0 to 1)
  • Pearson's r for interval/ratio; Spearman's for ranked data

Adjusted R²R² adjusted for number of predictors

⚠ Multicollinearity

Problem: Independent variables are highly correlated with each other.
Effect: Unstable coefficient estimates.
Detection: Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) > 10.
Solution: Remove correlated variables, use PCA.

💡 Real-World Example

Predicting sales using advertising spend, price, distribution coverage, and competitor activity simultaneously.

⚠ Exam Tip

Explain why Adjusted R² is preferred over R² in multiple regression.

✅ Unit 9 Summary

  • Multiple regression: Y = b₀ + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + ... + u
  • Key metrics: Multiple R, R², Adjusted R², F-test, t-test
  • Multicollinearity: VIF > 10 indicates problem; remove correlated variables or use PCA

📊 Discriminant Analysis
  • Creates discriminant function to classify cases into groups
  • Assumes multivariate normal distribution
  • Example: Classifying customers as "loyal" vs "switcher"

  • Example: Probability of customer churning (yes/no)

📊 Factor Analysis
  • Purpose: Data reduction, identify underlying structure
  • Input: Many observed variables
  • Output: Fewer factors
  • Key metric: Factor loading (correlation between variable and factor)
  • Extraction: Eigenvalue > 1 rule
  • Rotation: Varimax (orthogonal), Promax (oblique)
📊 Cluster Analysis
  • Purpose: Group similar cases together
  • Input: Cases (customers, products)
  • Output: Homogeneous clusters
  • Methods: Hierarchical (dendrogram), K-means
  • Distance: Euclidean, Manhattan
