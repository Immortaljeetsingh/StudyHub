Q1. Concepts of macro economics and their importance for managers (20 Marks)

Answer: Macro economics studies the economy as a whole - GDP, inflation, unemployment, interest rates, exchange rates, and fiscal and monetary policy - and managers need these concepts to forecast demand, time investment, control costs, and plan strategy.

Macro economics is the study of the economy as a whole. Instead of looking at one firm or one household, it looks at aggregates: total output, total employment, and the general price level. The basic concepts are:

  • GDP and economic growth: the total value of goods and services produced in the country in a year.
  • Inflation: the general rise in prices over time.
  • Unemployment: the share of the workforce without jobs.
  • Money supply and interest rates: how much money circulates and what it costs to borrow.
  • Exchange rate: the value of the rupee against foreign currencies.
  • Fiscal policy: government spending and taxation; monetary policy: the central bank's control of money and interest rates (the RBI's repo rate).
  • Balance of payments: the record of all money flowing in and out of the country.

Why managers must understand these concepts:

  • Demand forecasting: when the economy grows fast, demand rises; when it slows, sales fall. A manager who reads GDP trends plans production accordingly.
  • Investment decisions: high interest rates make borrowing costly, so expansion plans must wait; low rates make them attractive.
  • Cost management: inflation raises raw material and wage costs, so prices and budgets need revision.
  • Trade decisions: a weak rupee makes exports cheaper and imports costlier, changing sourcing and pricing.
  • Policy impact: tax changes (like GST) and subsidies directly change costs and profitability.
  • Strategic planning: managers analyze the economic environment before launching products - it is part of every business plan.

Example: when the RBI raised the repo rate to control inflation, an auto company's loan costs rose, so it postponed its new plant and offered financing schemes to support demand.

Key points to remember
  • Macro economics = economy as a whole: GDP, inflation, unemployment, interest rates, exchange rate, fiscal and monetary policy.
  • Managers use it for demand forecasting, investment, costs, trade, and policy planning.
  • Example: repo rate changes affect borrowing costs and expansion plans.

Takeaway: Read macro data (GDP, inflation, repo rate) before every plan - these aggregates set the demand, cost, and funding conditions for every business decision.

Q2. Inflation and its effects on the economy (20 Marks)

Answer: Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, caused by demand-pull, cost-push, or structural factors, and it erodes purchasing power, hurts fixed-income earners and savers, raises business costs, and weakens export competitiveness.

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level of goods and services. It is measured by indices such as the Consumer Price Index (CPI) and the Wholesale Price Index (WPI). Inflation can be demand-pull (too much money chasing too few goods), cost-push (rising costs of inputs push prices up), or structural (weak supply chains and bottlenecks).

Effects of inflation on various aspects of the economy:

  • Consumers: purchasing power falls - the same salary buys less; the poor suffer most because food and essentials take a large share of their income.
  • Fixed income earners: salaried employees, pensioners, and rent receivers find their real income shrinking.
  • Savers and investors: the real value of savings and fixed deposits erodes; people shift to gold, real estate, and stocks as hedges.
  • Businesses: input costs rise, uncertainty increases, and wage demands climb. Firms with pricing power pass on costs; others see margins shrink.
  • Export competitiveness: if domestic prices rise faster than abroad, exports become costlier and less competitive.
  • Government: the fiscal deficit grows because welfare spending and debt interest rise with prices.
  • Wage-price spiral: workers demand higher wages to match prices, which pushes costs up again - inflation feeds itself.

Mild inflation is considered healthy for growth, but high and unpredictable inflation hurts everyone. Example: when global crude prices rose, Indian fuel and transport costs jumped, FMCG companies raised pack prices, and the RBI responded by raising interest rates.

Key points to remember
  • Inflation = sustained rise in general prices; types: demand-pull, cost-push, structural.
  • Effects: lower purchasing power, hurt fixed earners and savers, higher business costs, weaker exports.
  • Wage-price spiral = inflation feeding itself.

Takeaway: Inflation is a silent tax - it shifts income from savers and fixed earners to borrowers and asset owners; whoever controls it stabilises the whole economy.

Q3. Structure of the money market (20 Marks)

Answer: The money market is the market for short-term funds (up to one year) where the RBI, banks, institutions, and corporates borrow and lend through instruments like call money, T-bills, commercial bills, commercial paper, CDs, and repo - and it is the channel through which monetary policy is transmitted.

The money market is the market for short-term funds - lending and borrowing for periods up to one year. It provides liquidity to banks, businesses, and the government, and it is where the central bank (RBI) conducts monetary policy.

Structure of the money market - instruments:

  • Call money market: very short-term borrowing between banks, from one day to two weeks; the call rate is the benchmark for short-term rates.
  • Treasury bills (T-bills): short-term borrowing instruments of the government, issued for 91, 182, or 364 days, sold at a discount.
  • Commercial bills: bills of exchange arising from trade, which are discounted by banks to give sellers early cash.
  • Commercial paper (CP): unsecured short-term promissory notes issued by large, creditworthy companies to fund working capital.
  • Certificates of deposit (CDs): short-term deposit instruments issued by banks to raise funds.
  • Repo and reverse repo: RBI's tool where banks borrow or lend against government securities for very short periods; these rates signal monetary policy.

Participants: the RBI (regulator and lender of last resort), commercial banks, co-operative banks, financial institutions like LIC, mutual funds, primary dealers, NBFCs, and large corporations.

Structure in India: the organized market (RBI-regulated instruments and institutions) and a small unorganized segment (indigenous bankers, money lenders). The organized market is supported by institutions such as the Discount and Finance House of India (DFHI) and primary dealers.

Functions: the money market helps banks manage daily liquidity, helps companies finance working capital cheaply, and helps the RBI transmit monetary policy - when the repo rate changes, money market rates follow. Example: a company needing cash for 6 months issues commercial paper at a discount instead of taking a costlier bank loan.

Key points to remember
  • Money market = short-term funds (up to 1 year).
  • Instruments: call money, T-bills, commercial bills, CP, CDs, repo/reverse repo.
  • Participants: RBI, banks, LIC, mutual funds, corporates.
  • Functions: liquidity management and monetary policy transmission.

Takeaway: The money market is the plumbing of the financial system - it prices short-term money and transmits every RBI policy move (like the repo rate) into the real economy.

Q4. Analysis of the New Industrial Policy, 1991 (20 Marks)

Answer: The New Industrial Policy, 1991 replaced the license-permit raj with liberalisation, privatisation, and globalisation - abolishing industrial licensing, opening reserved industries to private and foreign capital, and orienting the economy towards exports and competition.

The New Industrial Policy (NIP) of 1991 was announced when India faced a severe balance of payments crisis with foreign exchange reserves barely enough for a few weeks of imports. The policy opened the Indian economy by shifting from the license-permit raj to liberalization, privatization, and globalization.

Main features of NIP 1991:

  • Abolition of industrial licensing: licensing was removed for all industries except a small list of strategic and security-related ones.
  • Opened industries to the private sector: many sectors earlier reserved for the public sector were opened to private enterprise.
  • Liberal foreign investment: foreign direct investment (FDI) up to 51% was allowed under the automatic route in many industries, and foreign technology agreements received automatic approval.
  • Relaxation of MRTP: monopoly and restrictive trade practices restrictions were reduced, so large companies could expand without prior approval.
  • Public sector restricted: the public sector was confined to a few strategic areas; disinvestment of sick units was encouraged.
  • De-reservation of small industries: many items were removed from small-scale reservation so larger firms could enter.
  • Export promotion: a thrust to export-oriented units and trade liberalization, including tariff reduction and rupee devaluation to boost exports.

Analysis and impact: the policy ended decades of protection, brought competition and consumer choice, attracted foreign investment, and forced Indian industry to improve quality and efficiency. Critics note it also caused job losses in protected public-sector units and uneven regional development. Example: Bajaj Auto transformed from a license-protected producer into a globally competitive manufacturer, and the telecom sector went from a state monopoly to India's most competitive market.

Key points to remember
  • NIP 1991 came out of the balance of payments crisis - the birth of liberalization.
  • Features: licensing abolished, FDI 51% automatic, MRTP relaxed, public sector limited, exports thrust.
  • Impact: competition, consumer choice, FDI, efficiency; criticism: public-sector job losses.
  • Example: Bajaj Auto and telecom liberalization.

Takeaway: 1991 is the dividing line of Indian business history - before it, licence; after it, competition - and every industrial policy question in this course flows from that shift.

Q5. Latest Economic Policies (2014 onwards) with focus on Trade policy (20 Marks)

Answer: Since 2014 the government has driven reforms - Make in India, GST, IBC, digital economy, FDI liberalisation, PLI schemes and corporate tax cuts - while trade policy has shifted to export promotion, free trade agreements, and Atmanirbhar Bharat.

Since 2014, the government has carried out major economic reforms:

  • Make in India (2014): attracting manufacturing investment and building domestic production capacity.
  • Goods and Services Tax (2017): one unified indirect tax for the whole country, replacing many central and state taxes.
  • Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016): a single law to resolve failed businesses quickly and recover dues.
  • Digital economy push: Digital India, UPI payments, and the world's largest digital public infrastructure.
  • FDI liberalization: automatic routes raised in defence, retail, and insurance; many sectors now allow higher foreign stakes.
  • Privatization and disinvestment: selling stakes in public sector companies and corporatizing strategic units.
  • Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes: cash incentives to manufacturers in electronics, autos, textiles, and pharmaceuticals.
  • Corporate tax cut (2019): lower company tax rates to attract investment.

Focus on Trade policy:

  • Export promotion: the Foreign Trade Policy aims to push exports through schemes like RoDTEP (duty refunds on exports).
  • Free Trade Agreements: India signed trade agreements with the UAE, Australia, and others, and negotiates with the UK and the EU.
  • Atmanirbhar Bharat: self-reliance combined with being open to the world - promoting local manufacturing while keeping export markets.
  • Infrastructure for trade: better ports, logistics parks, and faster customs clearance to cut export costs.

Example: with PLI support and trade agreements, India's electronics exports boomed - the iPhone manufacturing cluster in India is a flagship result of Make in India and trade policy together.

Key points to remember
  • Reforms 2014+: Make in India, GST, IBC, digital economy, FDI liberalization, PLI, tax cuts.
  • Trade policy: export promotion (RoDTEP), FTAs, Atmanirbhar Bharat, logistics.
  • Example: electronics/iPhone manufacturing boom in India.

Takeaway: Trade policy after 2014 is export-led self-reliance - open to the world, but with domestic manufacturing capacity built first through Make in India, PLI, and FTAs.

Q6. Functions and policies of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) (20 Marks)

Answer: The IMF (est. 1944, Bretton Woods) promotes global monetary stability through surveillance, balance-of-payments lending tied to conditionality, capacity development, and SDR allocations - with quotas deciding members' voting power and borrowing limits.

The IMF was established in 1944 at the Bretton Woods Conference to promote global monetary cooperation, financial stability, and international trade. India is a founding member.

Functions of the IMF:

  • Surveillance: the IMF monitors the economies of member countries (Article IV consultations) and publishes world economic reports, warning about risks early.
  • Financial assistance: it lends money to member countries facing balance of payments difficulties, so they can stabilize without harmful measures.
  • Capacity development: it provides technical help and training to improve tax systems, banking regulation, and economic statistics of member countries.
  • SDR allocation: Special Drawing Rights - an international reserve asset the IMF can allocate to members during crises to add liquidity.
  • Forum for cooperation: it brings finance ministers and central bankers together for coordinated policy action.

Policies of the IMF:

  • Quota system: each member contributes a quota based on its economic size; the quota decides voting power and borrowing limits.
  • Lending facilities: different windows for different needs - the Stand-By Arrangement for short-term balance of payments support, the Extended Fund Facility for longer problems, and concessional facilities for poor countries.
  • Conditionality: loans are tied to economic reforms - fiscal discipline, removal of subsidies, exchange rate corrections, and structural reforms - so that the root causes are fixed.
  • Global reports: the World Economic Outlook and Global Financial Stability Report guide policy everywhere.

Example: India borrowed from the IMF in 1981 and 1991 (pledging gold) when foreign reserves ran low, and the conditionality pushed the 1991 reforms; more recently, the IMF supported Sri Lanka and Pakistan with bailout packages tied to reforms.

Key points to remember
  • IMF (1944, Bretton Woods): global monetary cooperation and stability.
  • Functions: surveillance, financial assistance, capacity development, SDRs.
  • Policies: quotas, lending facilities (SBA, EFF), conditionality.
  • Example: India's 1991 IMF loan pushed liberalization reforms.

Takeaway: The IMF is the world's crisis manager - it lends only with reform conditions, and those conditions literally shaped India's 1991 liberalisation.

Q7. Trends in the technological environment (20 Marks)

Answer: The technological environment is the fastest-changing part of the business environment, with AI, IoT, cloud, big data, 5G, robotics, fintech, blockchain and green technology reshaping how firms produce, sell, and manage.

The technological environment is the fastest-changing part of the business environment. Technology shapes how companies produce, sell, and manage, and it continuously creates new industries while destroying old ones. The major trends are:

  • Artificial Intelligence (AI) and machine learning: AI powers recommendations, chatbots, fraud detection, and automation of decisions.
  • Internet of Things (IoT): sensors connect machines, vehicles, and appliances, enabling smart factories and smart homes.
  • Cloud computing: companies rent computing power and storage instead of buying servers, cutting costs and enabling scale.
  • Big data and analytics: firms analyze huge volumes of data to understand customers and predict demand.
  • 5G connectivity: faster networks enable real-time applications in telemedicine, gaming, and industrial control.
  • Automation and robotics: robots do repetitive work faster and more accurately; India is a top market for industrial robotics.
  • Digital payments and fintech: UPI has made digital payments a daily habit for hundreds of millions of Indians.
  • Blockchain: secure, transparent ledgers for supply chains, contracts, and cryptocurrencies.
  • Green technology: solar, electric vehicles, and energy efficiency, pushed by climate goals.
  • Cybersecurity: as business goes digital, protecting data has become a critical technology function.

Impact on managers: technology lowers costs, personalizes customer experience, speeds innovation, and changes skill needs - so managers must invest in digital transformation, reskill employees, and manage data responsibly. Example: Reliance Jio's 2016 launch of cheap 4G data disrupted the whole telecom industry and created the digital infrastructure for India's apps economy, while UPI transformed retail payments from cash to QR codes.

Key points to remember
  • Trends: AI, IoT, cloud, big data, 5G, robotics, fintech, blockchain, green tech.
  • Impact: lower costs, personalization, faster innovation, new skills.
  • Examples: Jio's disruption, UPI payments, Make in India electronics.

Takeaway: Technology is a strategic variable, not an IT matter - firms that adopt AI, data, and automation early outperform those that wait, while whole industries are created and destroyed in the process.

Q8. Short notes (20 Marks)

Answer: The four short notes cover the drivers of CSR (led by the 2% rule of the Companies Act 2013), GST as India's one-nation-one-tax reform, BoP equilibrium (current + capital account balances), and ADRs as US-listed receipts for Indian shares.

(a) Drivers of CSR: CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility) is business's responsibility towards society and the environment. Its drivers are: (1) regulatory pressure - the Companies Act 2013 made it compulsory for large companies in India to spend 2% of average net profit on CSR; (2) ethical values - owners and managers who believe in giving back; (3) stakeholder pressure - investors, NGOs, and media now question companies on social and environmental conduct; (4) reputation and brand - responsible firms win customer trust; (5) consumer expectations - buyers prefer ethical brands; (6) long-term sustainability - protecting the environment and communities protects the business itself; and (7) global standards like ISO 26000. Example: the Tata Group has practiced CSR for over a century through trusts funding education and health.

(b) Goods and Services Tax (GST): GST, launched on 1 July 2017, is a single indirect tax on goods and services for the whole country. It subsumed about 17 central and state taxes into one. The structure has CGST (central), SGST (state), and IGST (inter-state). Rates are broadly 0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28%. Benefits: a common national market, removal of cascading taxes through input tax credit, more transparency, and easier compliance. Challenges: compliance burden on small businesses and the need for rate rationalization. Example: before GST, a truck crossing state borders faced check-posts and octroi; after GST, goods move freely across India.

(c) Equilibrium in BoP: The Balance of Payments is the record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world - the current account (trade in goods and services, income, transfers) plus the capital account (investment and loans). The BoP is in equilibrium when total receipts equal total payments, i.e., the current account balance and capital account balance together sum to zero. Automatic adjustment happens through price changes (a deficit may devalue the currency, making exports cheaper), interest rates (higher rates attract foreign capital), and income effects. A deficit must be financed by borrowing, drawing down reserves, or devaluation. Example: India's oil import bill widens the current account deficit whenever crude prices spike, and the gap is financed by capital inflows like FDI and FII.

(d) American Depository Receipts (ADRs): An ADR is a dollar-denominated instrument that represents shares of a non-US company and is traded on US stock exchanges such as the NYSE or NASDAQ. A US depositary bank holds the actual shares and issues receipts against them; one ADR equals one or more underlying shares. ADRs give Indian companies access to the deep US capital market, better visibility, and a global investor base, while US investors can buy Indian shares without cross-border hassle. Costs include SEC compliance and depositary fees. ADRs exist at three levels (I, II, III) with different listing and disclosure requirements; there are also GDRs traded on European exchanges like London. Example: Infosys, Wipro, and Dr. Reddy's Laboratories have listed ADRs in the US, while many Indian firms use GDRs in London.

Key points to remember
  • CSR drivers: law (2% rule), ethics, stakeholders, brand, sustainability.
  • GST (2017): CGST+SGST+IGST, subsumed 17 taxes, common market.
  • BoP equilibrium: receipts = payments (CA + KA = 0); deficit financed by capital flows.
  • ADR = US dollar receipts for foreign shares; Infosys, Wipro are examples.

Takeaway: Short notes test precision - give the definition, the structure, and one example in three compact lines each, and you have the full 20 marks.

Q1. Define business environment and discuss the different components of business environment and their influence on organisations.

Answer: The business environment is the sum total of all internal and external forces affecting a firm, operating at three levels - internal, micro (task), and macro (PESTEL) - each of which shapes strategy, costs, and demand differently.

Key points:
  • Internal environment: resources, management style, culture, structure - controllable by the firm.
  • Micro (task) environment: suppliers, customers, competitors, intermediaries, publics - directly touches daily operations.
  • Macro environment (PESTEL): Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal forces - indirect and beyond firm control.
  • Influence: political-legal forces set the rules (Companies Act, GST, labour laws); economic forces set demand and cost conditions (inflation, interest rates); social forces shape markets and workforce; technology changes products and processes.
  • Dynamic and inter-related: a change in one factor (e.g., a new law) ripples through the others.
  • SWOT link: opportunities and threats come from the external environment, strengths and weaknesses from the internal one.

Takeaway: One-line exam formula - environment = internal + micro + macro; scan it with PESTEL and answer through SWOT.

Q2. Distinguish between economic growth and economic development and discuss their indicators.

Answer: Economic growth means rising output (GDP), while economic development adds improvement in living standards, health, education, and equity - growth is quantity, development is quality.

Key points:
  • Growth indicators: GDP/GNI growth rate, per capita income, industrial production, savings and investment rates.
  • Development indicators: Human Development Index (HDI - income, education, life expectancy), poverty headcount, literacy, infant mortality, employment quality.
  • Growth without development: high GDP can coexist with inequality - India's high-growth decades still showed regional and social disparities.
  • India's story: growth accelerated after the 1991 reforms; development indicators improved steadily - literacy, life expectancy, poverty reduction, electrification.
  • Inclusive growth: modern policy aim - benefits must spread across sectors, regions, and social groups (e.g., Jan Dhan, Ujjwala).
  • Why managers care: growth raises demand for products; development raises the quality of markets, infrastructure, and the workforce.

Takeaway: Growth = GDP (quantity), development = HDI/equity (quality) - examiners repeatedly test this distinction.

Q3. What is CSR? Discuss the key drivers of CSR and the CSR initiatives of Indian companies.

Answer: CSR is business's commitment to operate in an economically, socially, and environmentally responsible way, driven by regulation, ethics, stakeholders, and brand - and in India it is partly mandated by the Companies Act 2013's 2% rule.

Key points:
  • Regulatory driver: Section 135, Companies Act 2013 - companies above a threshold must spend 2% of average net profit on CSR in Schedule VII areas.
  • Ethical and value-driven: owners and managers who believe business owes society a return.
  • Stakeholder pressure: investors, NGOs, media, and rating agencies now question firms on social and environmental conduct.
  • Reputation and brand: responsible firms earn customer trust and a licence to operate.
  • Indian initiatives: Tata Trusts (education, healthcare - over a century of giving), ITC (watershed development, e-Choupal), Reliance Foundation (rural health, sports), Wipro (sustainability, e-waste).
  • Long-term sustainability: a healthy society and environment protect the business itself - "doing well by doing good".

Takeaway: Indian CSR is half-voluntary, half-mandated (2% of net profit) - always quote the Companies Act 2013 rule and one company example.

Q4. Describe the structure of the Indian financial system - institutions, money market, and capital market.

Answer: The Indian financial system channels savings into investment through institutions (banks, NBFCs, insurers), two key markets (money and capital), instruments, and regulators (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA).

Key points:
  • Institutions: RBI, commercial and cooperative banks, NBFCs, LIC and mutual funds, development banks (NABARD, SIDBI, EXIM).
  • Money market: short-term funds (up to 1 year) - call money, T-bills, commercial paper, CDs, repo.
  • Capital market: long-term funds - equity (NSE, BSE), corporate bonds, government securities, derivatives.
  • Regulators: RBI (banking, payments), SEBI (securities), IRDAI (insurance), PFRDA (pensions).
  • Debt and forex markets complete the system for government borrowing and external trade.
  • Function: efficient credit allocation - a healthy financial system accelerates growth and transmits monetary policy.

Takeaway: Financial system = institutions + markets + instruments + regulators; money market is short-term, capital market is long-term.

Q5. Discuss the industrial policy framework of India - compare the Industrial Policy Resolution 1956 with the New Industrial Policy 1991.

Answer: India's industrial policy moved from the state-dominated IPR 1956 (socialist pattern, licence raj) to the liberalised NIP 1991 (LPG), and then to Make in India/Atmanirbhar Bharat after 2014.

Key points:
  • IPR 1956: socialist pattern of society, public sector dominance (17 reserved industries), industrial licensing under the IDR Act 1951, MRTP controls on big business.
  • Consequences of 1956 policy: licence-permit raj, monopoly inefficiency, slow growth - the "Hindu rate of growth" of ~3.5%.
  • NIP 1991: licensing abolished for all but a few strategic industries, FDI up to 51% under automatic route, MRTP relaxed, public sector de-reserved, exports promoted.
  • Outcomes: competition, better quality, consumer choice, rising FDI, global competitiveness.
  • Post-2014: Make in India, PLI schemes, privatisation and disinvestment, ease of doing business.
  • Exam formula: 1956 = socialism, 1991 = LPG, 2020 = Atmanirbhar Bharat.

Takeaway: 1956 = state-led industry, 1991 = market-led industry - the shift is the single most repeated fact in this course.

Q6. Explain the New Economic Policy 1991 (Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation) and its impact on the Indian economy.

Answer: NEP 1991 - triggered by the balance of payments crisis - liberalised, privatised, and globalised the Indian economy, ending decades of protection and setting India on a higher growth path.

Key points:
  • Trigger: forex reserves fell to about two weeks of imports; gold was pledged and India borrowed from the IMF with reform conditions.
  • Liberalisation: industrial licensing abolished, import tariffs cut, interest rates deregulated, MRTP relaxed.
  • Privatisation: disinvestment of PSUs, fewer reserved areas, competition allowed in telecom, banking, and airlines.
  • Globalisation: FDI/FII entry permitted, rupee devalued and later market-determined (1993), trade and current account opened.
  • Impact: 8-9% growth in the 2000s, FDI boom, IT services global leadership, consumer choice and quality improvement.
  • Criticism: job losses in protected public-sector units, uneven regional development, initial adjustment pains.

Takeaway: One crisis (1991) triggered LPG - know the cause, the content, and the consequences in that order.

Q7. Discuss the roles of the IMF, World Bank, and WTO in the international financial system.

Answer: The Bretton Woods twins - IMF and World Bank (1944) - plus the WTO (1995) govern global monetary stability, development finance, and trade rules respectively.

Key points:
  • IMF: surveillance of members' economies, balance of payments lending with conditionality, capacity development, SDR allocations.
  • World Bank Group: long-term development finance - IBRD, IDA (concessional), IFC (private sector), MIGA (guarantees).
  • WTO (successor to GATT, 1995): trade liberalisation, dispute settlement, binding of tariffs, plus GATS and TRIPS.
  • India: founding member of IMF and World Bank; WTO member since 1995; active in Doha Round agricultural negotiations.
  • Division of labour: IMF = short-term stabilisation, World Bank = long-term development, WTO = rule-based trade.
  • Regional additions: ADB, AIIB, BRICS New Development Bank complement the Bretton Woods system.

Takeaway: IMF = short-term stabilisation, World Bank = long-term development, WTO = trade rules - one line, three institutions.

Q8. Write short notes on the following:

Answer: (a) Balance of Payments (b) Need for foreign trade and its barriers (c) FDI vs FPI (d) Technological environment and international business.

Key points:
  • (a) BoP: the systematic record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world - current account (goods, services, income, transfers) plus capital account (investment, loans, reserves).
  • (b) Foreign trade: needed because no country is self-sufficient - it brings foreign exchange, technology, cheaper inputs, and larger markets; barriers are tariff (customs duty, anti-dumping) and non-tariff (quotas, licensing, standards, exchange controls).
  • (c) FDI vs FPI: FDI is long-term investment with management control (e.g., a foreign auto plant); FPI is short-term portfolio buying of shares and bonds for returns (e.g., FII flows) - FDI is stable, FPI is volatile.
  • (d) Technology and international business: technology shrinks distance (logistics, communication), enables global production networks, creates digital services exports (IT, BPO), and forces firms to upgrade - or be overtaken by global competitors.

Takeaway: Each short note needs a definition, a structure, and one Indian example - four notes, twelve lines, full marks.

Q1. Explain the internal and external environment of business with examples.

Answer: The internal environment (resources, culture, management) is inside the firm and controllable, while the external environment (micro - suppliers, customers, competitors; macro - PESTEL) is outside and largely beyond the firm's control.

Key points:

Q6. What are the functions and policies of International Monetary Fund (IMF)? Discuss in detail.

Q6. Highlight the importance of Balance of Payments (BoP) for an economy and analyse the trends in India's BoP.

Answer: The BoP matters because it signals external solvency, drives the exchange rate, guides policy, and determines reserve needs - and India's BoP shows a structural goods deficit financed by services exports, remittances, and capital inflows, with the CAD swinging around 1-3% of GDP.

Key points:
  • Importance: warns of crises (1991), guides exchange-rate and trade policy, shapes reserve management, attracts or repels investors.
  • Current account trends: persistent goods deficit (oil, gold) offset by services surplus (IT ~$325 billion) and remittances ($100+ billion); CAD ~1-3% of GDP.
  • Capital account trends: strong FDI, volatile FPI, ECBs, NRI deposits financing the gap.
  • Reserves: $600+ billion, covering 10+ months of imports - a huge buffer since 1991.
  • Crisis episodes: 1991 (gold pledge) and 2013 (taper tantrum) showed the BoP's fragility; post-2013 buffers are much stronger.
  • Exchange rate: managed float since 1993 - the rupee absorbs shocks.

Takeaway: India's BoP = structural goods deficit + strong invisibles + volatile capital flows, cushioned by huge reserves - quote the 1991 vs today contrast.
