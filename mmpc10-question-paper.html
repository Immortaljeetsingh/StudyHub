Q1. How is present value calculated under the discounting principle? Explain with the help of an example.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the concept of discounting and the formula, 5 marks for the worked example.

Answer: Under the discounting principle a future amount is worth less than the same amount today, so present value is calculated as PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n - reversing compound interest to bring tomorrow's rupees into today's value.

The discounting principle says a rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow, because today's money can be invested and earn interest. The present value (PV) of a future amount is its current worth calculated by reversing compound interest.

  • Formula: PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n, where FV is the future value, r is the rate of interest and n is the number of years.
  • If you expect ₹10,000 one year from now at 10% interest, PV = 10,000 ÷ 1.10 = ₹9,091. So ₹9,091 invested today at 10% grows to ₹10,000.
  • For multiple future cash flows, managers add the present values of each year's cash flow.
  • Managers use discounting for capital budgeting, comparing projects whose costs come today and benefits arrive over many years.

A firm comparing two projects chooses the one with the higher total present value, because that project creates more value in today's rupees. The higher the interest rate, the lower the present value of any future amount.

Key points to remember: PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n; money has time value; discounting converts future cash flows into today's money; used in investment decisions.

Takeaway: A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow - discount everything before comparing projects.

Q2. How is decision taken under risk? Differentiate between risk-averse and risk-seeking behaviour.

Marks guide: 10 marks for decision-making under risk and expected value, 5 marks for the risk-averse vs risk-seeking comparison.

Answer: Decisions under risk are made by comparing expected values (EV = Σ probability × outcome) and choosing the highest EV; risk-averse managers prefer a certain outcome, risk-seeking managers prefer the gamble.

Under risk, a manager knows the possible outcomes of a decision and the probability of each outcome, but cannot be sure which one will occur. Decisions under risk are made using expected value.

  • Expected value: EV = Σ (probability × outcome). The manager picks the option with the highest expected value.
  • Example: a project gives a 60% chance of ₹2 lakh profit and a 40% chance of ₹50,000 loss. EV = (0.6 × 2,00,000) + (0.4 × −50,000) = ₹1,00,000.
  • Risk-averse behaviour: prefers the certain outcome over a gamble with the same or higher expected value - for example, accepting a fixed ₹1 lakh salary over a 50% chance of ₹2.5 lakh.
  • Risk-seeking behaviour: prefers the gamble even when the certain option has equal or higher expected value - entrepreneurs and startup founders often behave this way.

Risk-neutral managers decide purely on expected value. Insurance companies, banks and most established firms are risk-averse, while venture capital and new-venture businesses are typically risk-seeking.

Key points to remember: risk means known probabilities; decide by expected value (EV = Σ p × outcome); risk-averse prefer certainty, risk-seeking prefer gambles.

Takeaway: Expected value decides which gamble to take; attitude to risk decides whether you take it at all.

Q3. Write short notes on: (a) Income as a determinant of demand (b) Tastes and Preferences as determinants of demand

Marks guide: 5 marks for each short note, with definition, effect on demand and an example.

Answer: Income and tastes are demand determinants - higher income shifts demand right for normal goods (left for inferior goods), and a change in tastes shifts the whole demand curve, whereas a price change only moves along it.

(a) Income as a determinant of demand: Disposable income (Yd) appears in the demand function Qd = f(Po, Pc, Ps, Yd, T, A, CR, R, E, N). For normal goods, higher income raises demand and shifts the demand curve right; for inferior goods, higher income reduces demand. As Indian household incomes rise, demand for cars, branded goods and air travel increases, while demand for cheap substitutes like local bus travel falls.

(b) Tastes and preferences as determinants of demand: A change in tastes changes demand at every price, causing a shift of the whole demand curve - unlike a price change, which only causes movement along the curve. Remember: "Move on price, shift on the rest." During COVID-19, awareness campaigns shifted the demand curve for face masks sharply right; similarly, health consciousness has shifted demand away from sugary drinks and toward fitness products in India.

For managers, income changes tell them which markets are growing, while taste changes signal when to relaunch, rebrand or drop a product.

Key points to remember: income and tastes are demand determinants (PITY-CANE); income rise shifts demand right for normal goods; taste changes shift the curve, price changes only move along it.

Takeaway: Price moves along the curve, income and tastes shift the whole curve - the demand curve only shifts when the "rest" changes.

Q4. Describe the estimation of demand using regression analysis. Explain how is regression analysis used for managerial decision-making.

Marks guide: 10 marks for regression estimation and its metrics, 5 marks for managerial applications.

Answer: Regression estimates demand by fitting Q = a + bP + cY + dA to data, and managers use the estimated coefficients to forecast sales for planned prices, incomes and advertising budgets.

Demand estimation quantifies the relationship between quantity demanded and its determinants using regression analysis, while forecasting predicts future demand levels.

  • Regression equation: Q = a + bP + cY + dA, where P is price, Y is income and A is advertising. The coefficients measure how strongly each variable affects demand.
  • Example: for Indian car demand, Q = 5000 − 200P + 150Y + 300A means a ₹1 lakh price rise cuts demand by 200 cars, while ₹1,000 more income adds 150 cars.
  • Checking the model: R² shows the percentage of variation explained (0.85 means 85%); the t-statistic (|t| > 2) shows whether each variable is significant; the F-statistic tests the model overall.
  • Managerial use: with estimated coefficients, managers can predict sales for a planned price, advertising budget or expected income change, then set prices and budgets accordingly.

FMCG companies like HUL use regression to estimate how price changes, advertising spend and festival seasons affect demand for products like Surf Excel across Indian markets. The DREG sequence helps: collect Data, run Regression, Evaluate R²/t-stats, Generate the forecast.

Key points to remember: regression estimates Q = a + bP + cY + dA; check R², t-stat and F-stat; regression converts data into price, income and advertising decisions.

Takeaway: A fitted regression equation is a demand forecast machine - but only as good as its R² and t-stats.

Q5. Explain the following in short run using diagrams: (a) TC = TFC + TVC (b) ATC = AFC + AVC

Marks guide: 10 marks for the two cost identities with diagrams, 5 marks for the behaviour of each cost curve.

Answer: In the short run TC = TFC + TVC (TC starts at the TFC level and TVC rises with output) and ATC = AFC + AVC (AFC falls continuously; AVC and ATC are U-shaped, with MC cutting both at their minimum points).

In the short run, at least one input is fixed, so total cost splits into fixed and variable parts.

  • (a) TC = TFC + TVC: Total fixed cost (TFC) - rent, salaries of permanent staff - does not change with output and is a horizontal line. Total variable cost (TVC) - raw materials, power - rises with output. Total cost (TC) is the vertical sum: it starts at the TFC level and rises with output, so the diagram shows TC always lying above TVC by exactly TFC.
  • (b) ATC = AFC + AVC: AFC = TFC/Q and always declines as output spreads fixed cost (a rectangular hyperbola). AVC = TVC/Q is U-shaped because of the law of diminishing returns. ATC = AFC + AVC is therefore also U-shaped, lying above AVC. Marginal cost (MC) crosses both AVC and ATC at their minimum points - the rule MC < ATC means ATC is falling, MC > ATC means it is rising.

Example: Tata Steel's blast furnaces are fixed costs while iron ore and coal are variable. Its break-even output uses Q = FC ÷ (P − AVC), and production continues as long as price covers average variable cost - the shutdown point is P < AVC.

Key points to remember: TC = TFC + TVC and ATC = AFC + AVC; AFC always falls; ATC and AVC are U-shaped; MC cuts both at their minimums; shut down when P < AVC.

Takeaway: MC is the boss of the average curves - below their minimum it pulls them down, above it pushes them up.

Q6. Explain long-run cost functions.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the LRAC concept and economies of scale, 5 marks for the learning curve and economies of scope.

Answer: Long-run cost functions describe the lowest possible cost of any output when all inputs are variable - the LRAC curve is the envelope of all SRAC curves and is U-shaped because of economies followed by diseconomies of scale.

In the long run all inputs are variable, so the firm can build any plant size. The long-run average cost curve (LRAC) is the envelope of all short-run average cost curves - it is U-shaped because of economies followed by diseconomies of scale. At the minimum point of LRAC, SRAC = SRMC = LRAC = LRMC.

  • Economies of scale lower average cost as output rises: production (specialisation, as in Ford's assembly line), marketing (HUL spreads advertising over 50+ brands), managerial, transport/storage, pecuniary (Walmart's bulk-buying discounts) and external economies (Silicon Valley's supplier ecosystem).
  • Cost-output elasticity: Ec = MC ÷ AC. Ec < 1 means economies of scale, Ec > 1 means diseconomies.
  • Learning curve: C(n) = C(1) × n−b - unit costs fall with cumulative production, as Maruti's manufacturing costs fell over decades.
  • Economies of scope: producing two products together is cheaper than separately: TC(Q1, Q2) < TC(Q1, 0) + TC(0, Q2).
  • Diseconomies of scale: morale problems, communication breakdowns, coordination complexity and bureaucracy push the LRAC up at very large sizes.

Tata Steel's Jamshedpur plant produces at lower per-unit cost than smaller mills because of scale economies - the essence of long-run cost analysis.

Key points to remember: LRAC envelopes the SRAC curves; economies of scale make it fall (Ec < 1), diseconomies make it rise; learning curve and scope economies also cut long-run costs.

Takeaway: In the long run you choose the plant size - LRAC shows the cheapest plant for every output.

Q7. Explain the characteristics of Monopoly. How is equilibrium price and output determined under monopoly in the long run?

Marks guide: 10 marks for characteristics and the MR = MC equilibrium, 5 marks for the long-run result and an example.

Answer: Monopoly is a market with one seller, a unique product and blocked entry, so the firm is a price maker; equilibrium output is where MR = MC and price is read from the demand curve, allowing supernormal profit even in the long run.

Characteristics of monopoly: one seller, a unique product with no close substitutes, blocked entry, the firm is a price maker, MR < P so the MR curve lies below the downward-sloping demand curve, and the firm can earn supernormal profits even in the long run.

  • Profit maximisation: the monopolist produces where MR = MC, then charges the price that the demand curve allows at that output - so P > MC, which creates a deadweight loss.
  • Long-run equilibrium: because entry is blocked, supernormal profits persist; the firm stays at the MR = MC output and does not shift to minimum ATC, so monopoly is neither allocatively nor productively efficient.
  • Example: Indian Railways is a natural monopoly - huge capital requirements block entry. It practises third-degree price discrimination with different per-kilometre fares for sleeper and AC classes.

Welfare comparison: under perfect competition P = MC with maximum total surplus; under monopoly P > MC with reduced consumer surplus transferred to the producer and a deadweight loss - the reason governments regulate monopolies.

Key points to remember: one seller, price maker, entry blocked; equilibrium at MR = MC with P charged from demand; long-run supernormal profit possible; P > MC creates deadweight loss.

Takeaway: MR = MC sets the quantity, the demand curve sets the price, and blocked entry keeps the profit.

Q8. Explain third degree price discrimination giving real life examples.

Marks guide: 10 marks for the concept and conditions, 5 marks for real life examples.

Answer: Third degree price discrimination charges different prices for the same product to different customer segments (age, income, location, time), which works when the firm has market power, can identify segments and resale between them is impossible.

Third degree price discrimination means charging different prices for the same product to different customer segments - the firm divides the market by age, income, location or time and charges each group its maximum willingness to pay.

  • Three conditions must hold: market power (the firm can set price, not a price taker); identifiable segments with different willingness to pay; and no arbitrage - customers cannot resell the product across segments.
  • Airlines: business travellers pay 3–5 times more than leisure travellers for the same seat because business tickets cannot be resold.
  • Student and senior citizen discounts on cinema tickets, trains and software - price-sensitive groups pay less.
  • Indian pharmaceutical companies sell the same medicine cheaper in India than in the US.
  • Indian Railways charges ₹0.34/km in sleeper class but ₹3.36/km in AC First - different fares for different segments of the same journey.

Geographic pricing and early-bird discounts are also third-degree discrimination. The firm extracts more consumer surplus than uniform pricing, but unlike first degree, some surplus remains with buyers.

Key points to remember: third degree = different prices for different segments; needs market power, segmentation and no arbitrage; examples - airlines, student discounts, Indian pharma, Railways fare classes.

Takeaway: Same product, different prices - charge more where demand is less elastic and resale is impossible.

Q1. Briefly discuss the nature and scope of Managerial Economics.

Answer: Managerial Economics applies microeconomic theory to business decisions - it is economics in the service of the manager, covering demand, production, cost, pricing, profit and capital decisions.

Key points:

  • Nature: it uses the theory of the firm plus decision sciences (OR, statistics) to solve real managerial problems; it is both positive ("what is") and normative ("what should be").
  • Microeconomic core: demand analysis, production function, cost analysis, market structures, pricing - with a macro overlay (business environment, trade cycles).
  • Scope: demand analysis and forecasting, production and cost analysis, pricing decisions, profit management and capital budgeting.
  • Role in management: internal decisions (output, price, input mix) and external/environmental decisions (competition, government policy).
  • Links: draws on economic theory, statistics, mathematics/OR, accounting and psychology of the consumer.
  • Limitations: simplifying assumptions, ceteris paribus conditions and data problems restrict practical application.

Takeaway: Managerial Economics = applied microeconomics + decision sciences for practical business choices.

Q2. How is profit maximisation an objective of the firm? Explain.

Answer: Profit is maximised where MR = MC (with MC rising); at that output the difference between total revenue and total cost is at its greatest, and profit maximisation is the classical objective around which the theory of the firm is built.

Key points:

  • Rule: produce up to the unit where MR = MC; beyond it each extra unit adds more cost than revenue, so total profit falls.
  • Profit = TR − TC; it is maximised where the slope of TR equals the slope of TC, i.e. MR = MC.
  • In perfect competition P = MR, so the rule becomes P = MC; the firm is a price taker and only chooses output.
  • Criticisms: short-run vs long-run conflict, uncertainty about MC, divorce of ownership and control, multiple goals (sales maximisation - Baumol; growth - Marris; satisficing - Simon).
  • Modern view: value/wealth maximisation - maximising the present value of all future profits - is the refined form of the same objective.
  • Profit also serves survival, expansion and dividend goals, which is why it remains the firm's central objective.

Takeaway: MR = MC is the universal profit-maximising rule; profit is a means to value, survival and growth.

Q3. Explain the difference between firm's demand curve and market demand curve.

Answer: The market demand curve is the horizontal sum of every buyer's demand at each price (downward sloping), while the individual firm's demand curve shows what one firm can sell - horizontal under perfect competition, but the whole market curve under monopoly.

Key points:

  • Market demand = sum of all individual demands at each price; it is what the industry faces.
  • Perfect competition: the firm's demand is perfectly elastic (horizontal) at the market price - it can sell any amount without affecting price.
  • Monopoly: the firm's demand curve is the market demand curve itself, since the firm is the industry.
  • Imperfect markets: the firm's curve is a downward-sloping share of the market curve, flatter the more substitutes exist.
  • Determinants of the difference: number of firms, product homogeneity, buyers' choices and market share.
  • Managerial use: pricing and output decisions depend on which curve the firm faces - price takers decide only output, price makers decide both.

Takeaway: The market curve sums all buyers; the firm's curve is horizontal (competition) or the market curve itself (monopoly).

Q4. What are the major approaches to demand measurement?

Answer: Demand is measured through direct approaches (consumer surveys, market experiments, expert opinion) and indirect/statistical approaches (trend projection and regression), chosen according to data availability and the purpose of estimation.

Key points:

  • Direct - surveys: consumer interviews and market surveys ask buyers about intentions, preferences and reactions to price changes.
  • Direct - market experiments: test markets and controlled experiments observe actual buying behaviour for new products or new prices.
  • Direct - expert opinion: Delphi rounds and industry panel judgements, used when data are scarce.
  • Indirect - statistical: trend (time series) projection extrapolates past demand; regression analysis estimates Q as a function of price, income, advertising and other variables.
  • Choice depends on: new vs existing product, cost of the study, speed needed and data quality.
  • Indian example: FMCG firms (HUL, P&G) use test-market launches before national rollouts, then regression for on-going forecasting.

Takeaway: No data, new product = direct methods; data available = regression and trend analysis.

Q5. Briefly explain production function with two variable inputs.

Answer: With two variable inputs, output is Q = f(L, K); the firm substitutes between labour and capital along isoquants, and the least-cost combination is where the isoquant is tangent to the isocost line (MRTS = w/r).

Key points:

  • Production function: maximum output obtainable from every combination of the two inputs, shown as isoquants.
  • Isoquant: all L-K combinations producing the same output; convex to the origin; higher isoquants mean more output; they never intersect.
  • MRTS = −ΔK/ΔL = MPL/MPK: the rate at which capital is replaced by labour, diminishing as substitution continues.
  • Isocost line: C = wL + rK, all input combinations costing the same; its slope is −w/r.
  • Optimum: least-cost combination where isoquant touches isocost, i.e. MRTS = w/r.
  • Returns to scale: proportional increases of both inputs - increasing, constant or decreasing - describe the long-run behaviour of the function.
  • Indian example: a textile unit substituting automation for workers as wages rise, choosing the mix where MRTS = w/r.

Takeaway: Cheap input mix = tangency of isoquant and isocost (MRTS = w/r).

Q6. What are the cost concepts frequently used in the managerial decision-making process? Briefly discuss.

Answer: Managers use actual vs opportunity costs, explicit vs implicit costs, direct vs indirect costs, fixed vs variable costs, and average vs marginal costs - with opportunity cost and marginal cost being the decision-critical pair.

Key points:

  • Actual vs opportunity: actual cost is the outlay made; opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative foregone - the true cost of any choice.
  • Explicit vs implicit: explicit costs are out-of-pocket payments; implicit costs are imputed values of owned resources (owner's capital and time).
  • Direct vs indirect: direct costs are traceable to a product (raw material); indirect costs are shared overheads (rent, administration).
  • Fixed vs variable: fixed costs do not vary with output in the short run; variable costs change with output - the basis of break-even analysis.
  • Average vs marginal: AC = TC/Q for unit comparisons; MC = ΔTC/ΔQ drives output decisions (MC = MR).
  • Sunk vs avoidable: sunk costs (past, unrecoverable) should be ignored; avoidable costs decide continuation and shutdown.
  • Economic vs accounting cost: economic cost = explicit + implicit, the basis of economic profit and entry/exit decisions.

Takeaway: For decisions, ignore sunk costs and always count opportunity costs.

Q7. How does economies of scale differ from economies of scope? Explain with examples.

Answer: Economies of scale lower average cost as the output of a single product increases, while economies of scope lower cost when one firm produces multiple products together - scale is "bigger", scope is "broader".

Key points:

  • Scale economies come from: production specialisation, technical indivisibilities (blast furnaces), marketing spread, managerial specialisation, pecuniary (bulk-buying) and external (industry-wide) economies.
  • Scope economies: TC(Q1, Q2) < TC(Q1, 0) + TC(0, Q2) - shared plants, brands, R&D and distribution channels make joint production cheaper.
  • Example of scale: Tata Steel's Jamshedpur plant produces at lower per-tonne cost than small mills because of scale; Ford's assembly line is the classic case.
  • Example of scope: Maruti shares one platform, dealership network and brand across models; HUL spreads advertising over 50+ brands; a bank selling loans + cards + insurance through one branch.
  • Scale lowers the LRAC along the quantity axis; scope shifts the cost structure down when products are combined.
  • Both ultimately run into diseconomies: beyond optimum scale, coordination and bureaucracy raise cost.

Takeaway: Scale = cheaper per unit of one product; scope = cheaper by sharing across many products.

Q8. Explain the characteristics of Perfect Competition. How is equilibrium output and price determined under perfect competition in the long-run?

Answer: Perfect competition needs many buyers and sellers, a homogeneous product, free entry and exit, and perfect information - so the firm is a price taker; in the long run entry and exit drive price to minimum average cost, leaving zero economic profit at P = MR = MC = min ATC.

Key points:

  • Characteristics: large numbers, homogeneous product, free entry/exit, perfect information, no transport or selling costs, price-taking behaviour.
  • Firm equilibrium: each firm produces where P = MR = MC; because demand is horizontal, MR = P.
  • Short run: firms can earn supernormal profit or incur loss, producing only if P > AVC.
  • Long run: profits attract entry (supply shifts right, price falls); losses force exit (supply shifts left, price rises) - until P = min ATC and profit is zero.
  • Efficiency: P = MC (allocative efficiency) and P = min ATC (productive efficiency) - no deadweight loss.
  • Indian example: agricultural mandi markets and many commodity markets approximate perfect competition - farmers take the mandi price as given.

Takeaway: Free entry and exit squeeze long-run profit to zero at minimum average cost.

Q1. Describe derivatives and partial derivatives as optimising techniques used in Managerial Economics.

Answer: Derivatives locate the profit-maximising (or cost-minimising) point by setting the slope of a function to zero, while partial derivatives isolate the effect of one variable (e.g. price or income) on another while holding the rest constant.

Key points:

  • Derivative: dY/dX measures the rate of change - the marginal value; a maximum or minimum occurs where dY/dX = 0 (second derivative decides max or min).
  • Applications: MR = dTR/dQ, MC = dTC/dQ; profit maximisation solves dπ/dQ = 0, i.e. MR = MC.
  • Partial derivative: ∂Q/∂P, ∂Q/∂Y - the effect of one demand determinant with others held fixed; signs show direction of influence (∂Q/∂P < 0 confirms the law of demand).
  • Constrained optimisation: the Lagrangian method uses partial derivatives to maximise output or profit subject to budget or capacity limits.
  • Worked example: TR = 100Q − Q² gives MR = 100 − 2Q; with MC = 20, profit maximum at 100 − 2Q = 20, so Q* = 40.

Takeaway: Set the first derivative to zero for the free optimum; with limits, the optimum sits on the constraint.

Q2. Briefly explain regression analysis. Describe how the regression analysis is used for managerial decisions.

Answer: Regression analysis fits a line Q = a + bP + cY + dA through historical data by least squares, quantifying how each determinant affects demand; managers use the estimated equation to forecast and to set prices, budgets and sales targets.

Key points:

  • Concept: the line of best fit minimises squared errors (OLS); simple regression has one independent variable, multiple regression has several.
  • Coefficients: each b measures the change in Q for a unit change in that variable; signs show the direction of influence.
  • Diagnostics: R² (share of variation explained), t-statistic (|t| > 2 significant) and F-statistic (overall fit).
  • Steps (DREG): specify the equation, collect data, estimate, evaluate R²/t-stats, generate forecasts.
  • Managerial use: price sensitivity (elasticity from b), advertising response, income effects - e.g. Q = 5000 − 200P + 150Y + 300A: a ₹1 lakh price rise cuts demand by 200 cars; ₹1,000 extra income adds 150 cars.
  • Indian example: HUL and carmakers regress sales on price, advertising and festival-season dummies to plan quarterly targets.

Takeaway: Regression turns past data into a forecast equation - trust it only with strong R² and t-stats.

Q3. Identify the variables included in demand function and explain its impact on demand.

Answer: Demand is Qd = f(Po, Pc, Ps, Yd, T, A, CR, R, E, N) - own price, related prices, income, tastes, advertising, credit, interest, expectations and buyer numbers each shift or move the demand curve.

Key points:

  • Own price (Po): inverse relationship - movement along the curve (law of demand).
  • Complement prices (Pc): inverse; substitute prices (Ps): direct (petrol-car, tea-coffee).
  • Disposable income (Yd): direct for normal goods, inverse for inferior goods.
  • Tastes (T) and advertising (A): shift the whole curve; credit availability (CR) and interest rates (R) move durable demand; expectations (E) create speculative buying; buyer numbers (N) scale the market.
  • Only a change in own price moves along the curve; any other variable shifts it - "move on price, shift on the rest".
  • Example: Jio's cheap data raised subscriber numbers and tastes for streaming, shifting the whole telecom demand curve right.

Takeaway: PITY-CANE - only own price moves along the curve; everything else shifts it.

Q4. Briefly explain production function with one variable input.

Answer: With one variable input, output follows the TP, AP and MP curves: marginal product first rises, then falls (law of diminishing returns), and the rational firm operates in Stage II where MP > 0 but declining, hiring labour up to the point VMP = wage.

Key points:

  • Curves: TP = total output; AP = TP/L; MP = ΔTP/ΔL.
  • Law of diminishing returns: beyond some labour, each extra worker adds less output.
  • Relationships: MP > AP → AP rising; MP = AP at AP's maximum; MP = 0 at TP's maximum; MP < 0 → TP falling.
  • Three stages: I (MP rising), II (MP falling but positive - rational zone), III (MP negative).
  • Hiring rule: employ labour until VMP = P × MP = w (wage).
  • Indian example: a farmer adding fertiliser to fixed land - output rises, then falls beyond the optimum dose.

Takeaway: Keep adding the variable input while VMP ≥ its price, and stay in Stage II.

Q5. How is Break-even output determined? Explain with the help of break-even chart.

Answer: Break-even output is where total revenue equals total cost: Q = FC ÷ (P − AVC); on the break-even chart it is the output at which the TR line crosses the TC line.

Worked solution: FC = ₹10,00,000; Price P = ₹800/unit; AVC = ₹500/unit.
Contribution per unit = P − AVC = 800 − 500 = ₹300.
Q_BE = FC ÷ (P − AVC) = 10,00,000 ÷ 300 = 3,333.33 ≈ 3,333 units.
Break-even sales value = 3,333 × 800 = ₹26.66 lakh. Target profit of ₹6 lakh needs Q = (10,00,000 + 6,00,000) ÷ 300 = 5,333 units.

Key points:

  • TR = P × Q is a straight line from the origin; TC = TFC + TVC rises from the TFC intercept.
  • Below Q_BE: TR < TC, the firm makes a loss; above Q_BE: profit.
  • Margin of safety = (actual output − Q_BE) ÷ actual output - how far sales can fall before loss.
  • Managers use it for pricing, profit planning, capacity utilisation and shutdown decisions (produce if P > AVC even below BEP).
  • Assumptions: linear revenue and costs, constant price, single product, no stock changes.

Takeaway: Q_BE = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit; sell more than that to make profit.

Q6. Explain long-run cost functions.

Answer: In the long run all inputs are variable, so the firm faces only one cost structure per output - the LRAC curve, which is the envelope of all short-run average cost curves and is U-shaped because of economies then diseconomies of scale.

Key points:

  • Every point on LRAC is the lowest cost at which the chosen plant can produce that output - it is the "planning curve".
  • At the minimum of LRAC: SRAC = SRMC = LRAC = LRMC.
  • Economies of scale (production, marketing, managerial, pecuniary, external) pull LRAC down; diseconomies (morale, communication, coordination, bureaucracy) push it up.
  • Cost-output elasticity Ec = MC ÷ AC: Ec < 1 economies, Ec = 1 constant, Ec > 1 diseconomies.
  • Learning curve: C(n) = C(1) × n−b - unit cost falls with cumulative output (Maruti's decades of manufacturing experience).
  • Economies of scope make joint production of two products cheaper than separate production.
  • Indian example: Tata Steel's Jamshedpur plant out-competes smaller mills through scale economies.

Takeaway: LRAC = envelope of SRACs; Ec = MC/AC tells you which side of the trough you are on.

Q7. Explain different approaches of estimation of cost function. Discuss the functional forms of cost function.

Answer: Cost functions are estimated by the accounting method, the econometric (statistical) method and the engineering method, using linear, quadratic or cubic functional forms - the cubic form producing the textbook U-shaped average cost curves.

Key points:

  • Accounting method: builds cost from accounting records - simple but uses only explicit costs and misses opportunity costs.
  • Econometric method: regresses TC (or AC) on output using historical data; statistically robust but suffers from collinearity, accounting distortions and time lags.
  • Engineering method: estimates cost from technical input-output relationships - best for new plants and processes.
  • Linear form: TC = a + bQ - constant MC, used for simple break-even work.
  • Quadratic form: TC = a + bQ + cQ² - linearly rising MC (U-shaped MC).
  • Cubic form: TC = a + bQ + cQ² + dQ³ - gives U-shaped ATC and MC, matching the theory of costs.
  • The choice depends on data, technology and whether short-run or long-run costs are being estimated.

Takeaway: Cubic cost functions match textbook cost curves; MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimums.

Q8. Explain the characteristics of monopoly. How is equilibrium output and price determined under monopoly?

Answer: Monopoly means one seller, a unique product, blocked entry and price-making power; equilibrium is where MR = MC, the price is then read from the demand curve, and the firm earns supernormal profit because P > MC.

Key points:

  • Characteristics: single seller, no close substitutes, entry blocked (legal, natural, strategic barriers), price maker, MR < P, persistent long-run profits.
  • Equilibrium: output at MR = MC; price from the demand curve at that output; profit per unit = P − ATC.
  • Because entry is blocked, supernormal profit continues in the long run - no tendency for it to erode.
  • The monopolist has no supply curve: the same price can coexist with different outputs and vice versa.
  • Welfare: P > MC creates a deadweight loss and a transfer of consumer surplus to the producer.
  • Indian example: Indian Railways is a natural monopoly - capital barriers block entry and it practises price discrimination across classes.

Takeaway: Monopoly sets quantity with MR = MC but price with the demand curve - and keeps the profit because entry is blocked.

Q1. Explain Equi-Marginal Principle of the firm. How is it different from discounting principle of the firm?

Answer: The equi-marginal principle allocates a fixed budget so that the last rupee spent on every use gives equal marginal return (MUx/Px = MUy/Py); the discounting principle compares money across time by converting future amounts into present value.

Worked example (discounting): PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n. A receipt of ₹10,000 one year from now at r = 10%: PV = 10,000 ÷ 1.10 = ₹9,091; two years away: 10,000 ÷ 1.21 = ₹8,264.

Key points:

  • Equi-marginal: a firm spreads advertising, investment or inputs across uses until the marginal return per rupee is equal everywhere - no reallocation can improve total profit.
  • It is a within-period allocation rule; utility maximisation (MUx/Px = MUy/Py) is its consumer-side twin.
  • Discounting: values tomorrow's cash flows at today's prices using PV = FV/(1 + r)n - an across-time comparison rule.
  • Difference: equi-marginal distributes a given resource among competing uses now; discounting ranks projects whose costs and benefits arrive at different times.
  • Together they drive capital budgeting: allocate funds so that the present values of marginal projects are equal.

Takeaway: Equi-marginal spreads today's budget across uses; discounting spreads value across time.

Q2. How is decision taken under risk? Describe how decision tree helps in strategic decision making? Explain with examples.

Answer: Under risk, outcomes have known probabilities, so decisions are taken by maximising expected value (EV = Σ p × outcome); a decision tree maps sequential choices and chance events, and the tree is solved by rolling expected values back from the branches to the decision node.

Worked solution: Expand: 0.7 × 30 + 0.3 × (−10) = 21 − 3 = EV ₹18 lakh.
Do not expand: 0.5 × 15 + 0.5 × 5 = EV ₹10 lakh.
Decision: choose Expand (EV 18 > 10). Sensitivity: if the success probability fell to 0.5, EV(Expand) = 0.5×30 + 0.5×(−10) = ₹10 lakh, making the firm indifferent.

Key points:

  • Risk vs uncertainty (Knight): risk has known probabilities; uncertainty does not - under uncertainty managers use judgement and scenario planning.
  • Decision tree elements: square = decision node (manager chooses); circle = chance node (nature/probability); branches carry payoffs; probabilities on chance branches.
  • Solving: compute EV at each chance node, then choose the branch with the highest EV at each decision node (backward induction).
  • Beyond EV, variance/standard deviation measures the riskiness of the alternatives chosen.
  • Example: new product launch, R&D options, capacity expansion - e.g. an Indian pharma firm choosing between building a plant or licensing a drug.

Takeaway: Trees force you to list every branch, its probability and payoff - then EV picks the route.

Q3. Identify the variables included in demand function and explain its impact on demand.

Answer: Qd = f(Po, Pc, Ps, Yd, T, A, CR, R, E, N) - own price, related prices, income, tastes, advertising, credit, interest, expectations and buyer numbers; own price moves along the curve, every other variable shifts it.

Key points:

  • Own price (Po): inverse - movement along the curve (law of demand).
  • Complements (Pc) inverse, substitutes (Ps) direct: petrol price up cuts car demand; tea price up raises coffee demand.
  • Income (Yd): direct for normal goods, inverse for inferior goods; rising Indian incomes shift demand for cars and air travel right.
  • Tastes (T), advertising (A), credit (CR), interest rates (R), expectations (E), buyer numbers (N): each shifts the whole curve.
  • "Move on price, shift on the rest" - the critical distinction for exam diagrams.
  • Example: expectation of a Maruti price hike in April pulls demand forward into March (E works on the curve).

Takeaway: PITY-CANE drives the curve; only Po moves along it.

Q4. Differentiate between income elasticity and cross elasticity of demand. Explain their application in managerial decision making.

Answer: Income elasticity Ey = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔY classifies goods as normal (Ey > 0) or inferior (Ey < 0); cross elasticity Exy = %ΔQx ÷ %ΔPy identifies substitutes (Exy > 0) and complements (Exy < 0) - both are demand forecasting and pricing tools.

Worked solution: Income rises from ₹40,000 to ₹48,000 (+20%) and demand from 800 to 920 (+15%): Ey = 15 ÷ 20 = +0.75 (normal necessity).
Petrol price rises 10% and car demand falls 5%: Exy = −5 ÷ 10 = −0.5 (complements).

Key points:

  • Ey classification: > 1 luxury, 0 to 1 necessity, < 0 inferior good.
  • Exy classification: > 0 substitutes, < 0 complements, = 0 independent goods.
  • Managerial use of Ey: forecast demand as incomes grow - India's rising incomes favour premium segments (cars, packaged foods).
  • Managerial use of Exy: price reactions to rivals (cola wars), bundling complements, assessing entry threat.
  • Government use: indirect tax policy targets inelastic goods for revenue.

Takeaway: Ey says where growth comes from as incomes rise; Exy says who competes with you and who complements you.

Q5. Differentiate between economic costs and accounting costs. Explain with illustrations.

Answer: Accounting cost counts only explicit, out-of-pocket expenses recorded in the books; economic cost adds implicit costs - the opportunity cost of the owner's capital, labour and other owned resources - so economic profit is always less than accounting profit.

Key points:

  • Accounting cost: wages, materials, rent, interest paid - the accountant's record of cash outflows.
  • Economic cost: explicit + implicit + opportunity cost of the next best alternative.
  • Illustration: a shopkeeper with ₹10 lakh of own capital in the business could earn 8% in an FD - the foregone ₹80,000 is an implicit cost that accounts never show.
  • Economic profit = TR − economic cost; normal profit (the minimum return to keep resources in the business) is part of economic cost, not profit.
  • Zero accounting profit is not the same as zero economic profit - a business can look profitable in books yet earn less than its capital could elsewhere.
  • Decisions (pricing, entry, exit) must follow economic costs; accounting costs serve tax and reporting.

Takeaway: Books show accounting cost; decisions must be made on economic cost.

Q6. Explain short-run cost functions.

Answer: In the short run at least one input is fixed, so TC = TFC + TVC, ATC = AFC + AVC, with AFC falling continuously, AVC and ATC U-shaped, and MC crossing both at their minimum points.

Key points:

  • TFC (rent, salaries) is constant; TVC (materials, power) rises with output; TC = TFC + TVC at every output.
  • AFC = TFC/Q - always declining (rectangular hyperbola) as fixed cost is spread over more units.
  • AVC and ATC are U-shaped because of the law of diminishing returns; ATC = AVC + AFC lies above AVC, the gap closing as Q grows.
  • MC = ΔTC/ΔQ is U-shaped and cuts AVC and ATC at their minimums (MC below average pulls it down, above pushes it up).
  • Shutdown rule: produce only while P ≥ AVC; below that, losses exceed fixed costs.
  • Indian example: an airline's aircraft leases and crew are fixed; fuel and catering are variable - high operating leverage magnifies profit swings.

Takeaway: MC rules the averages - it cuts AVC and ATC at their lowest points.

Q7. Explain the different types of market structures with the help of examples.

Answer: Markets range from perfect competition (many firms, homogeneous product, price takers) through monopolistic competition (many firms, differentiated) and oligopoly (few interdependent firms) to monopoly (one firm, price maker) - classified by firm numbers, product, entry and pricing power.

Key points:

  • Perfect competition: many sellers, identical product, free entry - agricultural mandis, commodity markets; zero long-run profit.
  • Monopolistic competition: many sellers, differentiated products, free entry - restaurants, salons, clothing brands; zero long-run profit with excess capacity.
  • Oligopoly: few firms, interdependence and strategic behaviour - telecom (Jio, Airtel, Vi), airlines, steel; supernormal profit possible; price rigidity (kinked demand).
  • Monopoly: single seller, unique product, blocked entry - Indian Railways, state utilities; sustained supernormal profit.
  • Classification criteria: number and size of firms, product homogeneity, entry barriers, degree of price control, market information.
  • Concentration measures (CR4, HHI) quantify how far a market is from competition.

Takeaway: Count the sellers, check the product and the doors - that tells you the structure.

Q8. Differentiate between monopolistic competition and oligopoly markets giving real life examples.

Answer: Monopolistic competition has many small firms selling differentiated products with free entry (zero long-run profit, excess capacity); oligopoly has a few large interdependent firms whose actions affect each other, allowing supernormal profit and price rigidity.

Key points:

  • Number of firms: many (monopolistic) vs few (oligopoly, often 2-10 dominant).
  • Product: differentiated but close substitutes vs homogeneous or differentiated; oligopoly products can be identical (cement) or branded (soft drinks).
  • Entry: relatively free vs difficult (high barriers - scale, capital, licences).
  • Interdependence: low in monopolistic competition (each firm is small) vs high in oligopoly - firms react strategically (game theory, collusion).
  • Price behaviour: monopolistic competitors set price independently; oligopolists face a kinked demand curve and price rigidity - rivals match cuts but not rises.
  • Long-run profit: zero economic profit with excess capacity vs possible supernormal profit.
  • Examples: restaurants and salons in Connaught Place (monopolistic) vs Jio-Airtel-Vi telecom and Indian airlines (oligopoly).

Takeaway: Many and free entry = zero profit; few and interdependent = strategic games and sticky prices.

Q1. What is fundamental nature of Managerial Economics? Discuss with reference to the three choice problems of an economy.

Answer: Managerial Economics is the application of economic theory and decision sciences to business decisions, and its fundamental nature follows from the economy's three choice problems - what, how and for whom to produce - which scarcity forces every society to solve.

Key points:

  • Fundamental nature: microeconomic theory (demand, production, cost, pricing) + decision sciences (OR, statistics) applied to managerial choices under scarcity.
  • What to produce: which goods and services, in what quantities - the allocation problem, solved by consumer demand signals.
  • How to produce: which technique and input mix (labour vs capital) - solved by relative factor prices and technology.
  • For whom to produce: how output is distributed - solved by income distribution and the price system.
  • The price mechanism coordinates all three: demand pulls production, costs choose technique, purchasing power decides distribution.
  • Managerial economists help firms read these signals - forecasting demand, choosing inputs and setting prices.

Takeaway: Scarcity creates the three choice problems; managers are the economy's problem-solvers at firm level.

Q2. "The opportunity cost principle is the return that can be had from the next best alternative use." Explain this statement with the help of an example.

Answer: Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative foregone when a resource is committed to one use - the true cost of any decision is what you gave up, not what you spent.

Worked example: An entrepreneur invests ₹10 lakh of her own money in her business. The next best use is an FD at 8%: opportunity cost = 8% × 10,00,000 = ₹80,000 per year. If the business earns only ₹60,000, it makes a book profit but an economic loss of ₹20,000 - better to close it and take the FD.

Key points:

  • The statement means: cost is measured by the sacrifice of the best alternative, not by the money actually spent.
  • Applies to all resources: funds, land, machinery, manager's time, even the student's study time.
  • The production possibility frontier (PPF) illustrates it: moving along the curve, producing more of one good costs the output of the other foregone.
  • Implicit costs are opportunity costs of owned resources; explicit costs are out-of-pocket sacrifices.
  • Managers apply it in make-or-buy, project choice, capital budgeting and pricing decisions.

Takeaway: Cost = best alternative given up; decisions that beat the next best alternative are worth taking.

  • Tastes (T) and advertising (A): shift the whole curve; credit (CR) and interest rates (R) matter most for durables (cars, houses, mobiles).
  • Expectations (E): anticipated price rises pull demand forward; number of buyers (N) scales market demand.

Q4. Differentiate between arc price elasticity and point price elasticity? Explain.

Answer: Point elasticity measures responsiveness at a single point on the demand curve (slope × P/Q), while arc elasticity measures it over a range between two points using the midpoint formula - arc is used for discrete changes, point for marginal ones.

Worked solution: Price rises ₹10 to ₹12 and demand falls 100 to 80 units.
Arc: Ed = [(Q2−Q1)/(Q2+Q1)] ÷ [(P2−P1)/(P2+P1)] = [(80−100)/(180)] ÷ [(12−10)/(22)] = (−0.111) ÷ (0.091) = −1.22 (elastic).
Point (at P = 10 on Q = 200 − 10P): dQ/dP = −10, so Ed = (dQ/dP) × P/Q = −10 × 10/100 = −1.0.

Key points:

  • Point elasticity: Ed = (dQ/dP) × (P/Q) - slope of the tangent at one point; exact for tiny price changes.
  • Arc elasticity: midpoint formula over the range - symmetric, so it gives the same answer whether price rises or falls.
  • Arc is preferred for large, discrete price changes; point is used for marginal analysis and from regression coefficients.
  • The two answers differ (here −1.22 vs −1.0) because arc averages over the whole segment while point measures one spot.
  • Managerial use: pricing at the current price (point) vs pricing over a planned range (arc).

Takeaway: Arc for big jumps, point for fine analysis - and they can disagree, so state which you used.

Q5. Explain production function framework. Differentiate between short-run and long-run.

  • Framework: inputs (land, labour, capital, materials) → technology → output; measured in physical units, not rupees.

  • Long run: all inputs variable → returns to scale, isoquant-isocost choice of plant, LRAC envelope.

  • Example: a restaurant's kitchen is fixed in the short run (add waiters only); in the long run it can rebuild, expand or relocate.

Takeaway: Short run = fixed plant and diminishing returns; long run = everything adjustable and returns to scale.

Q8. Explain the pricing under a perfectly competitive market.

Q1. Discuss in detail value maximization as an objective of the firm. Give examples.

Answer: Value maximisation means running the firm to maximise the present value of all future cash flows (shareholder wealth), rather than today's profit - it accounts for timing and risk and is the modern refinement of profit maximisation.

Worked solution: A project yields ₹2 lakh in year 1 and ₹3 lakh in year 2 at r = 10%: PV = 2,00,000/1.1 + 3,00,000/1.21 = 1,81,818 + 2,47,934 = ₹4,29,752. Value is maximised by choosing the strategy with the highest present value, not the highest current profit.

Key points:

  • Concept: V = Σ [CFt ÷ (1 + r)t] - the sum of discounted future cash flows; wealth maximisation for shareholders.
  • Superior to profit maximisation because it handles timing (rupee today > rupee tomorrow) and risk (higher r discounts riskier flows).
  • It explains sacrifice of short-run profit for long-run value - e.g. Amazon's years of thin profits building market share, Jio's free-launch losses that created a giant.
  • Decisions - investment, pricing, dividends, capital structure - are ranked by their effect on firm value.
  • Constraints: agency problem (managers vs owners), stakeholder obligations and governance; hence corporate governance exists to align managers with value.
  • Indian examples: Tata group's long-horizon value building; RIL's pivot from refining into telecom and retail.

Takeaway: Value = PV of future cash flows; sometimes losing money today is the value-maximising move.

Q2. Discuss the importance of regression analysis for a manager. Explain the process of specifying the regression equation.

Answer: Regression is important to managers because it quantifies how demand responds to price, income and advertising and produces forecasts; specification means choosing the dependent and independent variables, the data and the functional form before estimation.

Key points:

  • Importance: estimates elasticities, supports price and budget decisions, forecasts sales, isolates the effect of each factor (ceteris paribus).
  • Specify the model: decide the dependent variable (e.g. Q) and the determinants (P, Y, A, seasonality) based on demand theory.
  • Choose data: time series or cross-section, with enough observations and reliable measurement.
  • Choose the functional form: linear Q = a + bP + cY, or log-linear for constant elasticity.
  • Estimate and evaluate: OLS estimation; check R², t-stats and F-stat; test for problems (multicollinearity, autocorrelation).
  • Use: forecast demand under planned prices/budgets and update decisions as new data arrive.
  • Example: an auto firm specifying Q = a + bP + cY + dA + eS (S = festival season dummy) to plan launch pricing.

Takeaway: A good equation starts with theory, data and form - then OLS and diagnostics earn the forecast trust.

Q3. Differentiate between market demand curve and individual demand curve. Explain with examples.

Answer: The individual demand curve shows one consumer's quantity at each price; the market demand curve is the horizontal sum of all individuals' demands - larger, flatter and the one firms actually face.

Key points:

  • Individual demand: derived from one buyer's utility, income and substitution effects; steep, small scale.
  • Market demand: sum of quantities every buyer wants at each price; the industry demand curve used for pricing and revenue decisions.
  • At any price, market Q = sum of individual Qs - hence horizontal summation.
  • The market curve is usually flatter (more elastic) because more buyers and more substitutes respond to a price change.
  • Example: one commuter's demand for metro rides vs all of Delhi's; one subscriber vs all telecom users.
  • Managerial significance: the firm faces the market curve only under monopoly; under competition it faces a horizontal slice of it.

Takeaway: Add up every buyer at every price and you get the market curve the firm must serve.

Q4. Explain the following techniques of demand forecasting: (i) Expert opinion (ii) Surveys

Answer: Expert opinion pools the judgement of specialists (often in Delphi rounds) to forecast demand when data are absent; surveys ask consumers or salespeople directly about buying intentions - both are qualitative methods best for new products and new markets.

Key points:

  • Expert opinion: structured panels of industry experts; the Delphi method iterates anonymous rounds until convergence - cheap, fast, flexible, but subjective and expert-biased.
  • Consumer surveys: intention-to-buy questions, brand preference and price-sensitivity checks; direct but prone to response bias and cost per respondent.
  • Sales-force opinion surveys: field staff aggregate customer feedback - realistic but can be optimistic or conservative for quota reasons.
  • Use: new product launches (no historical data), novel markets, technological shifts (e.g. EVs in India).
  • Weaknesses: judgement errors, wishful thinking, low response rates; results should be triangulated with market experiments where possible.

Takeaway: No data? Ask the experts and the market - then verify with a real experiment.

Q5. Explain the types of statistical analysis. Discuss the limitations of different types of statistical analysis.

Answer: Statistical analysis is descriptive (summarising data) or inferential (drawing conclusions and testing hypotheses from samples), each with limitations - biased samples, wrong assumptions, spurious correlations and ceteris paribus violations.

Key points:

  • Descriptive statistics: mean, median, mode, standard deviation, tables and charts - summarise what has happened; limited to description, no causation.
  • Inferential statistics: estimation and hypothesis testing - regression, ANOVA, confidence intervals - generalise from sample to population.
  • Time series vs cross-section: trend and seasonality analysis vs comparisons across units at a point in time.
  • Limitations: sample bias and small samples; measurement errors; spurious correlation (correlation ≠ causation); violated assumptions (normality, independence); non-stationary data; omitted variables.
  • Managerial caution: a model that fits past data may fail when structure changes (e.g. post-COVID demand patterns).
  • Example: Diwali sales correlate with advertising spend, but income and festival effects also move them - isolating each needs careful model design.

Takeaway: Statistics summarise and test - they never prove cause; check the assumptions first.

Q6. Briefly differentiate between short-run cost functions and long-run cost functions. Also use diagrams to illustrate your answer.

Answer: Short-run cost functions have fixed inputs: TC = TFC + TVC with U-shaped ATC, AVC and MC; long-run cost functions have all inputs variable: the LRAC envelope of SRAC curves, shaped by economies and diseconomies of scale.

Key points:

  • Short run: at least one input fixed; TFC constant; AFC falls; AVC and ATC U-shaped; MC crosses both at their minimums; the plant size is given.

  • The short-run curves are "operating" curves; the long-run curve is the "planning" curve.
  • Example: Tata Steel operates today's furnaces (SR curves) but plans new furnaces (LR curve) around scale economies.

  • Features: many firms, product differentiation, free entry, non-price competition (advertising, quality), some pricing power.
  • Short-run equilibrium: MR = MC sets Q*; price P* is read from the downward-sloping demand curve; if P* > ATC the firm earns supernormal profit.
  • Because demand is fairly elastic (close substitutes), P* sits well above MC but the firm still has discretion over price.
  • Long-run contrast: profits attract entry, shifting each firm's demand left until P = ATC (tangent) - zero economic profit with excess capacity.
  • Example: restaurants, salons, clothing brands - differentiated menus, ambience and brands with many competitors.

Takeaway: Short run: like monopoly; long run: free entry erases the profit.

Q1. Explain the scope of Managerial Economics. Describe how is Managerial Economics linked with other disciplines.

Answer: The scope of Managerial Economics covers demand analysis and forecasting, production and cost analysis, pricing, profit management and capital budgeting; it is linked with economic theory, statistics, mathematics/OR, accounting and the behavioural sciences.

Key points:

  • Scope - demand side: demand analysis, elasticity, estimation and forecasting guide pricing and output plans.
  • Scope - supply side: production function, cost analysis and break-even decisions choose technique and scale.
  • Scope - outcome side: market structure, pricing strategies, profit management and investment (capital budgeting) decisions.
  • Link with economic theory: microeconomics provides demand, cost and market theory; macroeconomics supplies the business environment.
  • Link with decision sciences: statistics (estimation, testing), mathematics and operations research (optimisation) are the tools.
  • Link with accounting and behavioural sciences: accounting supplies cost data; psychology and organisational behaviour explain consumer and firm behaviour.

Takeaway: ME = micro theory + macro environment + decision tools, applied across the whole management map.

Q2. Differentiate between the following with the help of an example: (a) Unconstrained Optimization Technique (b) Constrained Optimization Technique

Answer: Unconstrained optimisation chooses the free optimum where the marginal condition holds (MR = MC); constrained optimisation maximises or minimises subject to a limit (budget, capacity), and when the constraint binds the optimum sits on the boundary.

Key points:

  • Unconstrained: set the first derivative of the objective to zero - e.g. choose advertising spend where MR of ads = MC of ads, with no budget cap.
  • Constrained: maximise profit subject to, say, a ₹10 crore ad budget or plant capacity; methods: Lagrangian multipliers or substitution.
  • Example: a firm wants maximum output from a given budget - the optimum is where the isoquant touches the isocost (MRTS = w/r), not at the free maximum.
  • The shadow price of the constraint measures how much profit rises if the limit relaxes by one unit.
  • Interior solutions satisfy marginal rules; boundary (corner) solutions apply when the constraint binds hard.
  • Indian example: Reliance deciding plant capacity under land/regulatory caps vs its unconstrained sales-maximising price choice.

Takeaway: No limits - set marginal benefit = marginal cost; with limits - go to the boundary until it hurts.

Q3. Explain the relationship between demand, price elasticity and revenue with the help of diagrammatic representation.

Answer: TR = P × Q; when demand is elastic (|Ed| > 1) a price cut raises revenue and MR > 0, when inelastic (|Ed| < 1) a price cut lowers revenue and MR < 0, and revenue is maximum where Ed = 1 and MR = 0.

Worked solution: Price falls ₹10 to ₹9, quantity rises 100 to 120.
Arc Ed = [(120−100)/(220)] ÷ [(9−10)/(19)] = (0.091) ÷ (−0.053) = −1.73 (elastic).
TR before = 10 × 100 = 1,000; TR after = 9 × 120 = 1,080 - revenue rose because demand is elastic.

Key points:

  • On a linear demand curve the upper half is elastic (MR > 0), the midpoint is unit elastic (MR = 0) and the lower half is inelastic (MR < 0).
  • Elastic: price and revenue move in opposite directions - discount to grow revenue.
  • Inelastic: price and revenue move together - hike prices (petrol-like goods).
  • TR curve: rises while MR > 0, peaks where MR = 0 (Ed = 1), falls afterwards.
  • Managerial use: airlines' yield management and telecom tariffs are designed around segment elasticity.

Takeaway: Elastic → cut price, inelastic → raise it; revenue tops out where Ed = 1.

Q4. Write short notes on the following demand forecasting techniques: (a) Expert opinion (b) Surveys

Answer: Expert opinion (including the Delphi method) pools specialist judgement for forecasts when data are absent; surveys collect buying intentions directly from consumers or the sales force - both qualitative techniques for new products and markets.

Key points:

  • (a) Expert opinion: panels of industry experts; Delphi runs anonymous rounds to converge on consensus; cheap and quick, but subjective and prone to expert bias.
  • (b) Consumer surveys: intention-to-buy and preference questionnaires; direct and flexible, but response bias, sampling problems and cost limit reliability.
  • Sales-force surveys: field staff estimates from customer contact - realistic, yet distorted by quota incentives.
  • Best for: new product launches, technology shifts, markets without history - e.g. forecasting EV adoption in India.
  • Combine with market experiments or regression when data accumulate; never rely on judgement alone.

Takeaway: Judgment first, data later - but keep triangulating.

Q5. Describe Production Isoquant. How does the Marginal Rate of Technical Substitution (MRTS) explain the relationship between labour and capital? Explain using diagram.

Answer: An isoquant shows all labour-capital combinations producing the same output; the MRTS = −ΔK/ΔL = MPL/MPK is the rate at which capital substitutes for labour along the isoquant, diminishing as more labour replaces capital.

Key points:

  • Isoquant properties: downward sloping, convex to the origin, higher isoquants = more output, and two isoquants never intersect.
  • MRTS = MPL/MPK: when labour is scarce, its marginal product is high and one worker replaces a lot of capital; as labour becomes abundant, its marginal product falls and each extra worker replaces less capital - diminishing MRTS gives the convex shape.
  • Optimum: the cheapest combination is where the isoquant is tangent to the isocost line, i.e. MRTS = w/r.
  • Managerial use: choosing automation vs workers as wages and capital costs change (Indian manufacturing's increasing automation).
  • Returns to scale describe what happens when both inputs rise proportionally.

Takeaway: MRTS = MPL/MPK = slope of the isoquant; equal to w/r at the cheapest combination.

Q6. Explain break-even chart as an analytical tool frequently employed by managerial economists.

Answer: The break-even chart plots total revenue and total cost against output; the break-even point is where TR = TC (Q = FC ÷ (P − AVC)), and the chart shows loss below and profit above that output - a planning, pricing and risk tool.

Worked solution: FC = ₹10 lakh, P = ₹800, AVC = ₹500: Q_BE = 10,00,000 ÷ 300 = 3,333 units (sales value ₹26.7 lakh).
For a target profit of ₹6 lakh: Q = (10,00,000 + 6,00,000) ÷ 300 = 5,333 units.
Margin of safety at 5,333 units: (5,333 − 3,333) ÷ 5,333 = 37.5% - sales can fall 37.5% before losses begin.

Key points:

  • Components: TR line (P × Q from origin), TC line (TFC + TVC), TFC line; BEP where the lines cross.
  • Analytical uses: profit planning (target output), pricing (contribution = P − AVC), capacity utilisation, cost control and shutdown decisions.
  • Margin of safety measures downside risk; the operating-leverage insight links fixed-cost share to profit volatility.
  • Assumptions: linear TR/TC, constant price and variable cost, single product, no inventories - so treat results as approximations.
  • Indian example: IndiGo's break-even load factor (~75%) - below it the airline loses money.

Takeaway: One chart, three answers - how much to sell, what to charge, and how safe your sales target is.

Q7. Differentiate between the following: (a) Effect of a change in demand on price and quantity (b) Effect of a change in supply on price and quantity

Answer: A demand increase moves equilibrium up the supply curve - both price and quantity rise; a supply increase moves equilibrium down the demand curve - price falls while quantity rises. Demand shifts move P and Q together; supply shifts move them in opposite directions.

Key points:

  • (a) Demand rises: D shifts right → P↑ and Q↑ (e.g. festival-season demand raises both onion prices and quantities sold).
  • (a) Demand falls: D shifts left → P↓ and Q↓ (e.g. COVID lockdowns cut travel demand, fares and trips fell together).
  • (b) Supply rises: S shifts right → P↓ and Q↑ (e.g. a bumper crop lowers mandi prices while volumes rise).
  • (b) Supply falls: S shifts left → P↑ and Q↓ (e.g. onion export bans and bad weather shrink supply, prices spike).
  • Magnitudes depend on elasticities: the flatter the other curve, the bigger the quantity effect relative to the price effect.
  • Policy reading: taxes and subsidies work through the supply side; advertising and income changes work through demand.

Takeaway: Demand shifts - P and Q travel together; supply shifts - they part ways.

Q8. Write short notes on the following: (a) Game Theory (Prisoner's dilemma) (b) Price Leadership

Answer: (a) The prisoner's dilemma shows two rational players choosing a dominant strategy (confess) that leaves both worse off than cooperation - it models why collusion is unstable; (b) price leadership is tacit coordination where one firm sets price and rivals follow, giving oligopoly price stability.

Key points:

  • (a) Game theory: players, strategies, payoffs; dominant strategy = best regardless of rival; Nash equilibrium = no one wants to deviate unilaterally.
  • Prisoner's dilemma: both confessing (-5, -5) is the Nash equilibrium even though both staying silent (-2, -2) is jointly better - trust is the problem.
  • Real PDs: advertising wars, price wars, OPEC quota cheating, telecom tariff collusion breaking down.
  • (b) Price leadership types: dominant-firm leadership (low-cost or largest firm leads), barometric leadership (most credible firm signals) - followers match price.
  • Leadership is implicit collusion: avoids ruinous price wars, keeps prices stable; leaders risk antitrust attention.
  • Example: telecom tariff revisions in India - one operator announces, others follow within days.

Takeaway: Self-interest beats cooperation in one-shot games; leadership replaces war with order.
