  • Q1(a) Solved - LP Formulation and Graphical Method

    Answer: The furniture problem is Max Z = 40x + 30y subject to 2x + y ≤ 80, x + 2y ≤ 100 and x, y ≥ 0, solved graphically at the corner (20, 40) for a maximum weekly profit of Rs 2,000.

    Let x = number of units of product A and y = number of units of product B produced per week. Machine time: product A uses 2x hours and product B uses 1y hour, so the machine constraint is 2x + y ≤ 80. Labour time: A uses 1x hour and B uses 2y hours, so x + 2y ≤ 100. Since we cannot produce negative quantities, x, y ≥ 0. The objective is to maximize profit Z = 40x + 30y. The LP model is therefore:

    • Maximize Z = 40x + 30y
    • Subject to: 2x + y ≤ 80; x + 2y ≤ 100; x, y ≥ 0

    Graphical solution: Step 1 - Draw the constraint lines: 2x + y = 80 through (40, 0) and (0, 80); x + 2y = 100 through (100, 0) and (0, 50). Step 2 - Shade the feasible region below both lines in the first quadrant. Step 3 - Corner points: (0, 0), (40, 0), (0, 50) and the line intersection. Solving 2x + y = 80 and x + 2y = 100 gives x = 20, y = 40. Step 4 - Evaluate Z at each corner: (0,0): 0; (40,0): 1600; (0,50): 1500; (20,40): 40(20) + 30(40) = 2000.

    Optimum: produce 20 units of A and 40 units of B, giving a maximum weekly profit of Rs 2,000. Both resources are fully used: 2(20) + 40 = 80 machine hours and 20 + 2(40) = 100 labour hours.

    Key points to remember: define decision variables first; write each resource as a constraint; the optimum always lies at a corner point of the feasible region; test every corner point in Z; an isoprofit line tangent to the feasible region also locates the optimum.

    Takeaway: Set up variables, write one constraint per resource, then evaluate every corner of the feasible region in Z - the optimum is always a corner point.

  • Q1(b) Solved - OR Model and Limitations of Graphical Method

    Answer: An OR model combines an objective function, decision variables and constraints, and the graphical method is limited to two variables, assumes linearity and finds the optimum by visual inspection.

    An OR model is a mathematical representation of a real decision problem. It expresses the objective (maximize profit or minimize cost) as a function of decision variables, subject to constraints that describe limited resources such as time, money, capacity and demand. Models may be linear, non-linear, deterministic or probabilistic; a good model is simple enough to solve but realistic enough to guide decisions.

    The graphical method has three main limitations. First, it works only for two decision variables - three variables need three-dimensional drawing and more than three cannot be drawn at all. Second, it assumes perfect linearity of the objective and constraints; real relationships are often curved, so the solution may be only an approximation. Third, it gives the optimum by visual inspection of corner points, which is slow and prone to error for problems with many constraints, and it cannot handle integer restrictions directly. For larger problems we therefore use the simplex method, and for integer restrictions we use integer or goal programming. Despite these limits, the graphical method is valuable because it gives insight: you can see the feasible region, the binding constraints and how sensitive the optimum is to changes in coefficients.

    Key points to remember: OR model = objective function + decision variables + constraints; graphical method is limited to 2 variables; linearity and visual inspection are its weaknesses; use simplex for bigger problems.

    Takeaway: Quote the model's three parts, the two-variable ceiling and the linearity assumption, then hand over to the simplex method for larger problems.

  • Q2(a) Solved - Simplex Method with Full Iterations

    Answer: Two simplex pivots on Max Z = 3x₁ + 5x₂ give x₁ = 2, x₂ = 3 and Z = 21, with both constraints exactly tight.

    Maximize Z = 3x₁ + 5x₂ subject to x₁ + 2x₂ + s₁ = 8; 3x₁ + 2x₂ + s₂ = 12; all variables ≥ 0, where s₁, s₂ are slack variables.

    Iteration 1: initial table has x₁ = x₂ = 0, s₁ = 8, s₂ = 12, Z = 0. Entering variable: x₂ (largest Cˇ-Zˇ = 5). Ratios: 8/2 = 4 and 12/2 = 6, so s₁ leaves. After pivoting, x₂ = 4, s₂ = 4, Z = 20.

    Iteration 2: new Cˇ-Zˇ row has x₁ = 0.5 positive, so x₁ enters. Ratios: 4 ÷ 0.5 = 8 and 4 ÷ 2 = 2, so s₂ leaves. After pivoting, x₁ = 2, x₂ = 3, s₁ = 0, s₂ = 0.

    Optimality check and answer: all Cˇ-Zˇ values are now zero or negative, so the solution is optimal: x₁ = 2, x₂ = 3, Z = 3(2) + 5(3) = 21. Verify: x₁ + 2x₂ = 2 + 6 = 8 and 3x₁ + 2x₂ = 6 + 6 = 12, so both constraints are tight.

    Key points to remember: convert ≤ constraints by adding slack; the entering variable has the largest positive Cˇ-Zˇ; the leaving variable has the smallest positive ratio; stop when all Cˇ-Zˇ ≤ 0 for a maximization problem.

    Takeaway: Slack turns ≤ into equations, the largest positive Cˇ-Zˇ enters and the smallest ratio leaves - stop when all Cˇ-Zˇ ≤ 0.

  • Q2(b) Solved - Slack, Surplus, Artificial Variables and Big-M

    Answer: Slack (for ≤), surplus (for ≥) and artificial variables (for ≥ and =) convert inequalities into equations, and Big-M penalises artificial variables with a huge M when no natural starting basis exists.

    Slack, surplus and artificial variables convert inequalities into equations so the simplex method can work. A slack variable is added to a ≤ constraint; it represents unused capacity and is always non-negative (x + 2y + s = 8 means s = 8 - x - 2y is the leftover resource). A surplus variable is subtracted from a ≥ constraint; it represents the amount by which the left side exceeds the requirement (3x + y - s = 10). Artificial variables are added to ≥ and = constraints purely to create an initial identity matrix so the method can start; they have no real meaning and must be driven to zero in the final solution.

    The Big-M method is used when constraints are of the ≥ or = type, because in such problems the initial solution is not feasible - there is no natural starting basis of slack variables. In Big-M, a very large penalty M is placed on each artificial variable in the objective (M for minimization, -M for maximization), forcing the algorithm to push them out of the basis. If an artificial variable remains positive in the final table, the problem has no feasible solution. The alternative is the two-phase method, which first minimizes the sum of artificial variables and then solves the original problem.

    Key points to remember: slack for ≤, surplus for ≥, artificial for ≥ and =; Big-M penalizes artificial variables with a huge M; a positive artificial variable in the final solution means infeasibility.

    Takeaway: Match the variable to the sign of the constraint - and remember a positive artificial variable in the final table means infeasibility.

  • Q3(a) Solved - Transportation Problem by Least Cost Method and MODI

    Answer: Least-cost allocations A-W1 = 20, A-W3 = 20, B-W2 = 25, B-W3 = 5 cost Rs 370, and the MODI test confirms optimality because every d = c - (u + v) is non-negative.

    Total supply = 40 + 30 = 70 tonnes; total demand = 20 + 25 + 25 = 70 tonnes. The problem is balanced.

    Step 1 - Initial solution by least cost method. Cost matrix: A-W1: 4, A-W2: 8, A-W3: 6, B-W1: 7, B-W2: 5, B-W3: 9. Pick the cheapest cell each time. A-W1 (cost 4): min(40, 20) = 20, so A-W1 = 20 and W1 is satisfied. Next B-W2 (cost 5): min(30, 25) = 25, so B-W2 = 25 and W2 is satisfied. Remaining supply: A = 20, B = 5; W3 needs 25. A-W3 (cost 6): min(20, 25) = 20, so A is exhausted and W3 needs 5 more. B-W3 (cost 9): 5. Final allocations: A-W1 = 20, A-W3 = 20, B-W2 = 25, B-W3 = 5.

    Step 2 - Cost of initial solution: 20(4) + 20(6) + 25(5) + 5(9) = 80 + 120 + 125 + 45 = Rs 370.

    Step 3 - MODI optimality test. With u₁ = 0 for row A: v₁ = 4 (cell A-W1), v₃ = 6 (cell A-W3). For row B: u₂ + 6 = 9 gives u₂ = 3, then v₂ = 5 - 3 = 2. For unoccupied cells, dᵢˇ = cᵢˇ - (uᵢ + vˇ): A-W2: 8 - (0 + 2) = 6 and B-W1: 7 - (3 + 4) = 0. All d ≥ 0, so the solution is optimal.

    Answer: minimum transportation cost = Rs 370 with allocations A-W1 = 20, A-W3 = 20, B-W2 = 25, B-W3 = 5 (4 allocations = m + n - 1 = 2 + 3 - 1, so non-degenerate).

    Key points to remember: balance the problem first (add dummy row/column if needed); least cost method starts from the cheapest cell; MODI computes uᵢ + vˇ for occupied cells and d = c - (u + v) for empty cells; optimal when all d ≥ 0.

    Takeaway: Balance first, allocate from the cheapest cell, then check with uᵢ + vˇ = cᵢˇ on occupied cells and d = c - (u + v) on empty ones.

  • Q3(b) Solved - Degeneracy in Transportation Problem

    Answer: Degeneracy means fewer than m + n - 1 occupied cells, which blocks the MODI test, and it is resolved by placing an epsilon allocation in an independent empty cell.

    Degeneracy occurs in a transportation problem when the number of occupied (allocated) cells is less than m + n - 1, where m is the number of rows and n the number of columns. It usually appears when a supply and a demand are satisfied simultaneously in one allocation, creating two filled cells in the same step. Degeneracy is a problem because the MODI optimality test requires exactly m + n - 1 basic cells to solve for the uᵢ and vˇ values - with fewer cells, the system of equations is incomplete and optimality cannot be checked.

    Degeneracy is resolved by allocating an infinitesimally small quantity, denoted by epsilon (ε, often written as a very small positive number), to one or more independent empty cells so that the number of occupied cells becomes exactly m + n - 1. The epsilon cell is treated as a basic cell in MODI, taking part in the uᵢ/vˇ computation, but its allocation is zero for cost purposes. After each iteration of the transportation algorithm, degeneracy must be re-checked because a reallocation can again reduce the number of basic cells. In assignment and queuing contexts, similar degeneracy handling keeps the solution methods valid.

    Key points to remember: degeneracy = fewer than m + n - 1 occupied cells; add epsilon in an independent empty cell; never let epsilon cells distort the cost; re-check after every iteration.

    Takeaway: Count occupied cells before testing, patch the shortfall with epsilon, and re-check after every iteration because new cycles can regenerate degeneracy.

  • Q4(a) Solved - Assignment Problem by Hungarian Method

    Answer: The Hungarian method (row and column reduction, minimum-line cover, matrix improvement) assigns J1-M3, J2-M4, J3-M1 and J4-M2 at a minimum total cost of Rs 21.

    Cost matrix (jobs vs machines): J1: 9, 7, 5, 6 | J2: 8, 6, 7, 5 | J3: 6, 7, 8, 5 | J4: 7, 5, 6, 4.

    Step 1 - Row reduction: subtract the minimum of each row from all its elements. J1: 4, 2, 0, 1; J2: 3, 1, 2, 0; J3: 1, 2, 3, 0; J4: 3, 1, 2, 0.

    Step 2 - Column reduction: subtract the minimum of each column. Column 1 minimum 1: 3, 2, 0, 2; column 2 minimum 1: 1, 0, 1, 0; columns 3 and 4 already have 0. Matrix: J1: 3, 1, 0, 1; J2: 2, 0, 2, 0; J3: 0, 1, 3, 0; J4: 2, 0, 2, 0.

    Step 3 - Cover all zeros with minimum lines: the zeros can be covered with 3 lines (e.g., row J3 and columns 2 and 4). Since 3 < 4, improve the matrix.

    Step 4 - Improvement: smallest uncovered element is 1. Subtract 1 from uncovered elements and add 1 at line intersections. Result: J1: 2, 1, 0, 1; J2: 1, 0, 2, 0; J3: 0, 2, 4, 1; J4: 1, 0, 2, 0.

    Step 5 - Optimal assignment and answer: independent zero assignments: J1-M3, J2-M4, J3-M1, J4-M2. Minimum cost = 5 + 5 + 6 + 5 = Rs 21, verified from the original matrix.

    Key points to remember: subtract row minima, then column minima; draw minimum lines to cover zeros; if lines < n, improve the matrix; make one zero assignment per row and column; total matches the original cost matrix.

    Takeaway: Follow reduce-cover-improve until n independent zeros exist, then make one assignment per row and column - and verify the total against the original matrix.

  • Q4(b) Solved - Duality in Linear Programming

    Answer: Every LP has a dual - a minimization problem for a maximization primal - with transposed constraints, equal optimal objective values and dual variables that are shadow prices of the primal resources.

    Every linear programming problem has a twin problem called its dual. The original problem is the primal; the dual is formed by interchanging the roles of constraints and objective coefficients. If the primal is a maximization problem with ≤ constraints, the dual is a minimization problem with ≥ constraints; the profit coefficients of the primal become the resource (right-hand side) values of the dual, and the resource values of the primal become the objective coefficients of the dual. If the primal has m constraints and n variables, the dual has n constraints and m variables, and the constraint matrix is transposed.

    The dual variables have a real economic meaning: each dual variable is the shadow price of the corresponding primal resource - the amount by which the optimal profit would increase if one more unit of that resource became available. The duality theorem states that if one problem has an optimal solution, so does the other, and their optimal objective values are equal. Complementary slackness connects the two: if a primal constraint is not binding, its dual variable is zero. Duality is used for sensitivity analysis (testing how the optimum changes when resource availability changes), for solving the dual when it is computationally easier, and for economic interpretation. For example, in our furniture problem, the dual tells us the maximum price worth paying for an extra machine hour or an extra labour hour.

    Key points to remember: dual of max is min; constraints and RHS swap roles; matrix is transposed; optimum values of primal and dual are equal; dual variables are shadow prices of resources.

    Takeaway: Dual of max is min, RHS and profit coefficients swap and the matrix transposes - then read the dual variables as the worth of one extra unit of each resource.

  • Q5(a) Solved - Queuing Theory (M/M/1) Numerical

    Answer: For λ = 12 and μ = 15 customers per hour, P₀ = 0.2, L = 4, Lₙ = 3.2, W = 20 minutes, Wₙ = 16 minutes and P(n ≥ 3) = 0.512.

    This is a single-server queue with Poisson arrivals and exponential service. Arrival rate λ = 12 customers/hour; service rate μ = 15 customers/hour. Traffic intensity ρ = λ/μ = 12/15 = 0.8. Since ρ < 1 the system is stable and a steady state exists.

    Step 1 - Probability of an empty system: P₀ = 1 - ρ = 1 - 0.8 = 0.2, i.e., 20% of the time the counter is idle.

    Step 2 - Average number in the system: L = λ/(μ - λ) = 12/(15 - 12) = 12/3 = 4 customers.

    Step 3 - Average number in the queue: Lₙ = λ²/[μ(μ - λ)] = 144/[15(3)] = 144/45 = 3.2 customers.

    Step 4 - Average waiting time in the system: W = 1/(μ - λ) = 1/3 hour = 20 minutes.

    Step 5 - Average waiting time in the queue: Wₙ = λ/[μ(μ - λ)] = 12/45 = 0.267 hours = 16 minutes. (Check: Wₙ = W - 1/μ = 20 - 4 = 16 minutes.)

    Step 6 - Probability of 3 or more customers: P(n ≥ 3) = ρ³ = (0.8)³ = 0.512. So about 51% of the time at least 3 customers are in the system. Also P(n > k) = ρⁿ for an M/M/1 queue.

    Key points to remember: ρ = λ/μ must be < 1; L = λ/(μ-λ), Lₙ = λ²/μ(μ-λ); W = 1/(μ-λ), Wₙ = Lₙ/λ; P(n ≥ k) = ρⁿ; keep units consistent (hours or minutes).

    Takeaway: Check ρ = λ/μ < 1 first, then apply the four M/M/1 formulae and P(n ≥ k) = ρⁿ - and keep hours and minutes consistent.

  • Q5(b) Solved - Elements of a Queuing System

    Answer: A queuing system has four elements - the arrival process, the service mechanism, the queue and the system capacity - described compactly by Kendall's M/M/1 notation.

    A queuing system has four basic elements. The first is the arrival process: customers (people, orders, machines, messages) arrive according to a pattern, usually described by the Poisson distribution with arrival rate λ, where arrivals are random and independent of each other. The second is the service mechanism: the number of servers (single or multiple), the service time distribution (usually exponential with rate μ), and the service discipline - the order in which customers are served, such as FCFS (first come first served), LCFS, or priority rules like shortest processing time. The third is the queue itself - the waiting line where customers wait when all servers are busy; it may have a finite or infinite capacity, and a customer may balk (refuse to join a long queue), renege (leave after waiting) or jockey (switch lines). The fourth is the system capacity and population source: a finite or infinite calling population, and limits on how many customers the system can hold.

    Standard notation is Kendall's shorthand, e.g., M/M/1 meaning Markovian arrivals, Markovian service and one server. Queuing analysis computes measures like average queue length Lₙ, average waiting time Wₙ, system length L, system time W, and utilization ρ. Indian examples include bank tellers, railway ticket counters, hospital outpatient departments and call centres, where the analysis helps decide how many counters or agents to open so waiting stays acceptable.

    Key points to remember: four elements - arrivals, service mechanism, queue discipline, system capacity; Kendall notation M/M/1; key outputs are L, Lₙ, W, Wₙ, P₀.

    Takeaway: List arrivals, service mechanism, queue discipline and capacity, then name Kendall's shorthand and the L, Lq, W, Wq outputs with one Indian example (bank counters, call centres).

  • Q6(a) Solved - PERT Network, Critical Path and Probability

    Answer: Expected times A = 2, B = 5, C = 4, D = 5, E = 7, F = 3 put the critical path on 1-2-4-5-6 with a 16-week duration, and Z = 3.0 gives a 99.9% chance of completing within 18 weeks.

    Step 1 - Expected time of each activity: tₖ = (o + 4m + p)/6, and variance σ² = [(p - o)/6]². A: (1+8+3)/6 = 2; B: (2+20+8)/6 = 5; C: (3+16+5)/6 = 4; D: (4+20+6)/6 = 5; E: (6+28+8)/6 = 7; F: (2+12+4)/6 = 3.

    Step 2 - Draw the network: events 1-2-3-5-6 (path of A, B, D, F) and 1-2-4-5-6 (path of A, C, E, F).

    Step 3 - Forward pass (earliest times): E1 = 0; E2 = 2; E3 = 2+5 = 7; E4 = 2+4 = 6; E5 = max(7+5, 6+7) = max(12, 13) = 13; E6 = 13+3 = 16.

    Step 4 - Backward pass (latest times): L6 = 16; L5 = 13; L4 = 13-7 = 6; L3 = 13-5 = 8; L2 = min(8-5, 6-4) = min(3, 2) = 2; L1 = 0.

    Step 5 - Critical path: activities where ES = LS: A (1-2), C (2-4), E (4-5), F (5-6). Critical path = 1-2-4-5-6 with duration = 2 + 4 + 7 + 3 = 16 weeks. Activities B and D each have 1 week of float.

    Step 6 - Probability of completion within 18 weeks: critical path variance = σ²(A) + σ²(C) + σ²(E) + σ²(F) = 4 × 0.111 = 0.444, so σ = 0.667 weeks. Z = (18 - 16)/0.667 = 3.0. From the normal table, P(Z ≤ 3.0) = 0.9987.

    Answer: project duration = 16 weeks; critical activities A, C, E, F; probability of completing within 18 weeks ≈ 99.9%.

    Key points to remember: tₖ = (o + 4m + p)/6; forward pass gives earliest, backward pass latest; critical path has zero float; Z = (due date - project duration)/σ; add variances of critical activities only.

    Takeaway: Compute tₖ = (o + 4m + p)/6, run forward and backward passes, take the zero-float activities as critical and add only critical variances for the Z-score.

  • Q6(b) Solved - PERT vs CPM

    Answer: PERT is probabilistic with three time estimates for uncertain R&D projects, while CPM is deterministic with single estimates and a time-cost trade-off for repetitive, well-understood projects.

    PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique) and CPM (Critical Path Method) are both network techniques for planning, scheduling and controlling projects, but they differ in four main ways. First, their orientation differs: PERT is probabilistic, developed for R&D and projects where activity times are uncertain, so it uses three time estimates - optimistic, most likely and pessimistic - and computes an expected time with variance. CPM is deterministic, developed for construction and maintenance projects where durations are known with reasonable certainty from past experience, so it uses a single time estimate per activity.

    Second, PERT focuses on time alone and treats cost as secondary, while CPM explicitly considers the time-cost trade-off and supports crashing - reducing project duration at extra cost by analysing normal and crash times and costs of activities. Third, PERT is event-oriented (it numbers events and emphasizes the probability of meeting a schedule), while CPM is activity-oriented and emphasizes the critical path and floats. Fourth, PERT is more suited to new, non-repetitive projects such as launching a satellite or developing a new drug, whereas CPM suits repetitive, well-understood projects like building a bridge or laying a highway. Both identify the critical path - the longest chain of activities that determines the minimum project duration - and both are applied in Indian projects, for example infrastructure construction and event management.

    Key points to remember: PERT = probabilistic, three time estimates, R&D projects; CPM = deterministic, single estimates, cost trade-off and crashing; both find the critical path and floats.

    Takeaway: Contrast PERT and CPM on four axes - time estimates, cost handling, orientation and project type - and note that both converge on the critical path.
