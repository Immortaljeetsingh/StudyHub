Unit 1: Nature of Long-Term Financial Decisions

📖 Three Financial Decisions (IFD)
  • Investment Decision: Which assets to buy? (MOST IMPORTANT) — Long-term: Capital budgeting (fixed assets); Short-term: Working capital management (current assets)
  • Financing Decision: How to fund assets? Debt vs Equity mix
  • Dividend Decision: How much profit to distribute vs retain?
  • Inter-relationship: Investment and financing decisions are simultaneous; linked through NPV
  • Time Value of Money: Rs 100 today > Rs 100 tomorrow (due to earning capacity)

Key Definitions:

  • Investment Decision: Deciding the total amount of assets, their composition, and the business risk of the firm. The most important financial decision.
  • Financing Decision: Determining the optimal mix of debt and equity to maximize firm value.
  • NPV Rule: Accept a project if NPV > 0. NPV links investment and financing decisions through the discount rate (cost of capital).
💡 Real-World Example

When Reliance Jio invested Rs 1.5 lakh crore in telecom infrastructure (investment decision), it funded through a mix of internal accruals, debt, and equity (financing decision).

⚠ Exam Tip

Explain the inter-relationship among IFD decisions. The NPV formula captures this link: NPV = C0 + C1/(1+r), where r is cost of capital (financing decision) applied to project cash flows (investment decision).

Dividend Policy — Key Theories:

  • Walter's Model: Dividend policy affects firm value when r (return on investment) vs k (cost of capital). If r > k: retain earnings; if r < k: distribute dividends
  • Gordon's Model: "Bird in hand" theory — investors prefer current dividends over future capital gains due to uncertainty
  • MM Hypothesis: Dividend policy is irrelevant in perfect markets — firm value determined by investment policy, not dividend policy
  • Lintner's Model: Firms smooth dividends — reluctant to cut dividends, adjust gradually to target payout ratio

Factors Affecting Dividend Policy:

  • Legal Constraints: Companies Act restrictions on paying dividends from capital
  • Liquidity: Cash position determines ability to pay dividends
  • Debt Covenants: Loan agreements may restrict dividend payments
  • Tax Policy: Dividend Distribution Tax (DDT) was applicable until 2020; now dividends taxed in hands of shareholders
  • Shareholder Expectations: Regular income vs growth expectations

Unit 2: Cost of Capital

📖 Cost of Capital Components
  • Cost of Capital: The minimum rate of return a firm must earn on its investments
  • Component Costs:
  • Cost of Equity (Ke): Using CAPM or Dividend Growth Model
  • Cost of Debt (Kd): Interest rate x (1 - tax rate) [tax shield benefit]
  • Cost of Preference Shares (Kp): Fixed dividend / Net proceeds
  • WACC: Weighted Average Cost of Capital = (E/V x Ke) + (D/V x Kd x (1-t))
  • Marginal Cost of Capital: Cost of raising one additional rupee of capital
  • Lower cost of capital = competitive advantage

Key Definitions:

  • WACC: The average rate of return required by all investors (equity, debt, preference), weighted by their proportion in the capital structure.
  • Cost of Equity: The return required by equity shareholders. Can be calculated using CAPM: Ke = Rf + Beta x (Rm - Rf) or Dividend Growth Model: Ke = D1/P0 + g.
ComponentFormulaNotes
Cost of Equity (Ke)Ke = Rf + Beta × (Rm - Rf)CAPM model
Cost of Debt (Kd)Kd = Interest × (1 - tax)Tax shield benefit
Cost of Preference (Kp)Kp = Dividend / Net ProceedsFixed dividend

WACC = (E/V × Ke) + (D/V × Kd × (1-t)) — Debt is cheaper due to tax shield.

Marginal Cost of Capital: The cost of raising one additional rupee of capital. As a firm raises more capital, the marginal cost may increase due to higher risk perceived by investors. A firm should invest in projects only until the marginal cost of capital equals the marginal return on investment.

WACC Numerical Example: A company has equity of Rs 60 lakh at Ke = 15%, debt of Rs 40 lakh at Kd = 10%, and tax rate = 30%.

  • WACC = (60/100 × 15%) + (40/100 × 10% × (1 - 0.30))
  • WACC = 9% + 2.8% = 11.8%
  • This means the firm must earn at least 11.8% on its investments to satisfy all providers of capital.
💡 Real-World Example

Infosys has low WACC due to zero debt (mostly equity-funded), while a highly leveraged company like Bharti Airtel has higher WACC due to debt costs. TCS maintains a moderate WACC with a balanced debt-equity mix, optimizing tax benefits while keeping financial risk manageable.

⚠ Exam Tip

WACC calculation is a very common numerical problem. Know all component cost formulas. Understand why debt is cheaper (tax shield). Remember: Lower cost of capital gives a firm competitive advantage as more projects become viable.

Unit 3: Capital Structure Decisions

📖 Capital Structure & Leverage
  • Capital Structure: Mix of long-term debt and equity in the firm's capitalization
  • Operating Leverage: Fixed operating costs amplify EBIT changes. DOL = % change in EBIT / % change in Sales
  • Financial Leverage: Fixed financial costs (interest) amplify EPS changes. DFL = EBIT / (EBIT - Interest)
  • Combined Leverage: Both operating and financial leverage combined. DTL = DOL x DFL

Key Definitions:

  • Financial Leverage: The use of fixed-cost sources of funds (debt, preference shares) to magnify returns to equity shareholders. DFL = EBIT / (EBIT - Interest).
  • Operating Leverage: The use of fixed operating costs to magnify changes in EBIT relative to changes in sales. DOL = % change in EBIT / % change in Sales.
  • Combined Leverage: The total effect of both operating and financial leverage. DTL = DOL x DFL. Measures overall risk of the firm.

Leverage Numerical Example: A firm has Sales = Rs 10 lakh, Variable Costs = Rs 6 lakh, Fixed Costs = Rs 2 lakh, Interest = Rs 1 lakh.

  • EBIT = Sales - Variable Costs - Fixed Costs = 10 - 6 - 2 = Rs 2 lakh
  • DOL = Contribution / EBIT = (10 - 6) / 2 = 2.0
  • DFL = EBIT / (EBIT - Interest) = 2 / (2 - 1) = 2.0
  • DTL = DOL x DFL = 2.0 x 2.0 = 4.0
  • Interpretation: A 10% increase in sales leads to 40% increase in EPS (DTL = 4).

Tata Motors uses high financial leverage (large debt) to amplify equity returns during good times, but this amplifies losses during downturns.

Unit 4: Project Planning and Formulation

📖 Project Fundamentals
  • Project: A non-routine, one-off undertaking with specific goals, time, and budget
  • Project Types: Capacity expansion, Modernization, Diversification, Research & Development
  • Project Life Cycle: Conception → Planning → Execution → Termination
  • Work Breakdown Structure (WBS): Breaking project into manageable tasks
  • Project Management: Planning, scheduling, monitoring, controlling

Key Definitions:

  • WBS: A hierarchical decomposition of the total scope of work to be carried out by the project team.
  • Project Life Cycle: The series of phases a project passes through from initiation to completion.

💡 Real-World Example

Tata Motors' Nano project followed the CPET cycle from concept to plant commissioning in Gujarat, involving conception (feasibility study), planning (design, budgeting), execution (construction), and termination (commissioning).

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) Details:

  • Hierarchical Decomposition: Breaks project into manageable work packages
  • Levels: Project → Phases → Tasks → Sub-tasks → Work packages
  • 100% Rule: WBS must include 100% of work defined in project scope
  • Mutually Exclusive: No overlap between work packages
  • Benefits: Clear scope, accurate cost estimation, responsibility assignment, progress tracking

Project Report Components:

  • Executive Summary: Brief overview of the entire project
  • Market Analysis: Demand-supply gap, target market, competition analysis
  • Technical Analysis: Technology, raw materials, machinery, plant location
  • Financial Analysis: Capital expenditure, working capital, projected financial statements (P&L, Balance Sheet, Cash Flow)
  • Risk Assessment: Key risks and mitigation strategies
  • Implementation Schedule: Timeline with milestones using CPM/PERT
⚠ Exam Tip

Understand project classification and WBS. Explain project life cycle with examples. A project report question may ask you to list and explain all components.

  • WBS breaks project into manageable tasks (100% rule, mutually exclusive)

Unit 5: Capital Budgeting — Investment Appraisal

📖 Capital Budgeting Techniques
  • Non-Discounted Methods:
  • Payback Period: Time to recover initial investment. Payback = Investment / Annual Cash Flow
  • ARR (Accounting Rate of Return): Average Profit / Average Investment x 100
  • Discounted Methods (consider Time Value of Money):
  • NPV (Net Present Value): PV of cash inflows - PV of cash outflows. Accept if NPV > 0. Best method.
  • IRR (Internal Rate of Return): Discount rate where NPV = 0. Accept if IRR > Cost of Capital. Can give multiple IRRs for non-conventional cash flows.
  • PI (Profitability Index): PV of Inflows / PV of Outflows. Accept if PI > 1. Useful for capital rationing.

Key Definitions:

  • NPV: The difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows over a project's life. NPV = Sum of [CFt / (1+r)^t] - Initial Investment.
  • IRR: The discount rate that makes the NPV of a project equal to zero. It represents the project's expected rate of return.
  • Profitability Index: The ratio of the present value of future cash flows to the initial investment. PI = PV of Inflows / PV of Outflows.

NPV Step-by-Step Calculation: A project costs Rs 2,00,000 and generates Rs 60,000/year for 5 years. Discount rate = 12%.

  • Year 1: 60,000 / (1.12)^1 = 60,000 / 1.120 = Rs 53,571
  • Year 2: 60,000 / (1.12)^2 = 60,000 / 1.254 = Rs 47,832
  • Year 3: 60,000 / (1.12)^3 = 60,000 / 1.405 = Rs 42,707
  • Year 4: 60,000 / (1.12)^4 = 60,000 / 1.574 = Rs 38,131
  • Year 5: 60,000 / (1.12)^5 = 60,000 / 1.762 = Rs 34,046
  • PV of Inflows = 53,571 + 47,832 + 42,707 + 38,131 + 34,046 = Rs 2,16,287
  • NPV = 2,16,287 - 2,00,000 = Rs 16,287 (Accept)

NPV vs IRR — Key Differences:

  • Reinvestment Assumption: NPV assumes reinvestment at cost of capital; IRR assumes reinvestment at IRR itself (often unrealistic)
  • Scale: NPV gives absolute value; IRR gives percentage. A small project may have high IRR but low NPV
  • Multiple IRRs: Non-conventional cash flows (sign changes) can produce multiple IRRs. NPV always gives a unique answer
  • Mutually Exclusive Projects: When choosing between two projects, NPV and IRR may conflict. Always trust NPV in such cases
  • Ranking: NPV ranks projects by value added; IRR ranks by rate of return

💡 Real-World Example

If a project costs Rs 100,000 and generates Rs 30,000/year for 5 years at 10% discount rate: NPV = 30,000 x 3.791 - 100,000 = Rs 13,730 (Accept). PI = 113,730/100,000 = 1.137 (Accept).

⚠ Exam Tip

NPV, IRR, and PI are the most frequently asked topics. Practice numerical problems. Know decision rules and comparison of methods.

Mutually Exclusive Conflict Example: Project A costs Rs 10,000 and returns Rs 15,000 in Year 1 (IRR = 50%). Project B costs Rs 1,00,000 and returns Rs 1,30,000 in Year 1 (IRR = 30%). If cost of capital = 10%:

  • NPV of A = 15,000/1.10 - 10,000 = Rs 3,636
  • NPV of B = 1,30,000/1.10 - 1,00,000 = Rs 18,182
  • IRR prefers A (50% > 30%), but NPV prefers B (Rs 18,182 > Rs 3,636)
  • Always choose Project B — NPV adds more value to the firm

✅ Unit Summary

  • Non-discounted: Payback Period (simple, ignores TVM), ARR (accounting-based)
  • Discounted: NPV (best method, absolute), IRR (percentage return), PI (ratio, capital rationing)
  • NPV > 0 = Accept; IRR > r = Accept; PI > 1 = Accept
  • NPV assumes reinvestment at cost of capital; IRR assumes reinvestment at IRR
  • For mutually exclusive projects, always prefer NPV over IRR
  • PI is most useful when capital is rationed (limited budget across multiple projects)

Unit 6: Project Implementation and Control (PERT/CPM)

📖 PERT & CPM
  • PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique): Uses 3 time estimates (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic)
  • Expected time = (O + 4M + P) / 6
  • Variance = [(P - O) / 6]^2
  • CPM (Critical Path Method): Uses single time estimate per activity
  • Critical Path: Longest path through network (determines project duration). Zero float on critical path activities.
  • Gantt Charts: Visual timeline of project activities

Key Definitions:

  • Critical Path: The longest sequence of activities in a project network that determines the minimum project duration. Activities on this path have zero float/slack.
  • PERT: A project management tool that uses probabilistic time estimates to calculate expected project duration and identify critical activities.
FeaturePERTCPM
Time Estimates3 (O, M, P)Single
NatureProbabilisticDeterministic
FocusEvent-orientedActivity-oriented
Best ForUncertain/Research projectsConstruction/Routine projects
💡 Real-World Example

In a construction project, if foundation laying is on the critical path, any delay in it directly delays the entire project.

PERT Numerical Example: An activity has Optimistic time = 4 days, Most Likely = 7 days, Pessimistic = 16 days.

  • Expected Time = (O + 4M + P) / 6 = (4 + 4×7 + 16) / 6 = (4 + 28 + 16) / 6 = 48 / 6 = 8 days
  • Variance = [(P - O) / 6]^2 = [(16 - 4) / 6]^2 = [12/6]^2 = 2^2 = 4 days^2
  • Standard Deviation = √4 = 2 days
⚠ Exam Tip

PERT calculations (expected time, variance) are common numerical problems. Draw network diagrams and identify critical path. Remember: Critical path = longest path = minimum project duration. Activities on critical path have zero float.

🧠 Memory Aid

O-M-P = Optimistic, Most likely, Pessimistic (PERT time estimates)

✅ Unit Summary

  • PERT: 3 estimates, probabilistic; CPM: single estimate, deterministic
  • Critical path = longest path = minimum project duration
  • PERT time = (O + 4M + P) / 6; Variance = [(P-O)/6]^2

Unit 7: Social Cost Benefit Analysis (SCBA)

📖 SCBA Concepts
  • SCBA: Evaluates projects considering social costs and benefits, not just financial returns
  • Shadow Prices: Prices reflecting true social value (may differ from market prices)
  • UNIDO Approach: Uses world prices as basis; converts domestic prices to border prices
  • Little-Mirrlees Approach: Uses accounting prices (shadow prices) for evaluation
  • Social Discount Rate: Lower than private discount rate (reflects social time preference)
  • Important for public sector and infrastructure projects

Key Definitions:

  • Shadow Prices: Prices that reflect the true economic value of resources, accounting for externalities and market distortions.
  • SCBA: A methodology for evaluating projects that considers the broader social impact, including externalities, beyond just financial returns.
💡 Real-World Example

A highway project may have negative NPV financially but positive social NPV when considering reduced travel time, lower pollution, and economic development.

Why SCBA is Needed:

  • Market Failures: Markets don't always reflect true social costs and benefits
  • Externalities: Pollution, employment, education — not captured in financial appraisal
  • Public Goods: Infrastructure, defense — non-excludable and non-rivalrous
  • Distributional Effects: Projects may benefit the poor more than the rich (social value)

Explain why SCBA is needed (market failures, externalities). Compare UNIDO and Little-Mirrlees approaches. Shadow prices are a key concept — they differ from market prices due to taxes, subsidies, and distortions.

Unit 8: Investment Decisions — CAPM & Risk

📖 CAPM & Risk Concepts
  • Risk vs Uncertainty: Risk = measurable probability; Uncertainty = not measurable
  • Systematic Risk: Market risk (cannot be diversified away) - measured by Beta
  • Unsystematic Risk: Company-specific risk (can be diversified away)
  • CAPM Formula: E(R) = Rf + Beta x (Rm - Rf)
  • Rf = Risk-free rate; Beta = Measure of systematic risk; Rm = Expected market return; (Rm - Rf) = Market risk premium
  • Beta Interpretation: Beta = 1 (same as market), Beta > 1 (aggressive), Beta < 1 (defensive)
  • Security Market Line (SML): Graphical representation of CAPM

Key Definitions:

  • CAPM: A model that describes the relationship between systematic risk and expected return for assets. E(R) = Rf + Beta(Rm - Rf).
  • Beta: A measure of a stock's volatility in relation to the overall market. Beta = 1.5 means the stock moves 1.5% for every 1% market move.
💡 Real-World Example

If Rf = 7%, Beta = 1.2, Rm = 15%: Expected Return = 7% + 1.2 x (15% - 7%) = 7% + 9.6% = 16.6%

⚠ Exam Tip

CAPM formula and numerical problems are very important. Understand beta interpretation. Know the difference between systematic and unsystematic risk.

🧠 Memory Aid

CAPM = Rf + B(Rm-Rf) and B>1 = More Risk, B<1 = Less Risk

📊 CAPM Model
E(R) = Rf + Beta x (Rm - Rf)
↓
Systematic Risk (Market) vs Unsystematic Risk (Company)

✅ Unit Summary

  • CAPM: E(R) = Rf + Beta(Rm - Rf) — describes risk-return relationship
  • Systematic risk (market, can't diversify) vs Unsystematic (company-specific, can diversify)
  • Beta interpretation: 1 = market, >1 = aggressive (more volatile), <1 = defensive (less volatile)
  • Security Market Line (SML) plots expected return vs beta graphically
  • CAPM assumptions: rational investors, efficient markets, no transaction costs, homogeneous expectations
  • Risk premium (Rm - Rf) compensates investors for bearing market risk

Unit 9: Project Evaluation Under Risk

📖 Risk Analysis Techniques
  • Sensitivity Analysis: Tests how output changes when one input variable changes. "What if" analysis. Identifies most sensitive variables.
  • Scenario Analysis: Evaluates project under different scenarios (best, worst, most likely)
  • Monte Carlo Simulation: Random sampling from probability distributions to model uncertainty
  • Decision Trees: Sequential decision-making under uncertainty
  • Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate (RADR): Higher discount rate for riskier projects
  • Certainty Equivalent Method: Adjust cash flows (not discount rate) for risk

Key Definitions:

  • Sensitivity Analysis: A technique that determines how changes in an input variable affect the output (NPV/IRR), holding other variables constant.
  • Monte Carlo Simulation: A computational technique that uses random sampling from probability distributions to model the probability of different outcomes in a project.

Monte Carlo Simulation Steps:

  • Step 1: Identify key input variables (sales, costs, prices) and their probability distributions
  • Step 2: Generate random values for each variable from its distribution
  • Step 3: Calculate NPV/IRR for each set of random values (one iteration)
  • Step 4: Repeat thousands of times (e.g., 10,000 iterations)
  • Step 5: Analyze distribution of NPV/IRR outcomes — mean, standard deviation, probability of loss
  • Advantage: Provides complete picture of risk, not just point estimates
TechniqueApproachBest For
Sensitivity AnalysisChange one variable at a timeIdentifying key risk drivers
Scenario AnalysisBest/worst/most likely scenariosUnderstanding range of outcomes
Monte CarloRandom simulation of all variablesComplex projects with multiple risks
Decision TreesSequential decisions with probabilitiesMulti-stage investment decisions
💡 Real-World Example

For a new product launch, sensitivity analysis might show that NPV is most sensitive to selling price and least sensitive to administrative costs.

⚠ Exam Tip

Compare sensitivity analysis, scenario analysis, and Monte Carlo simulation. Decision tree problems are common.

🧠 Memory Aid

✅ Unit Summary

  • Sensitivity: "What if" one variable changes — identifies most sensitive variables
  • Scenario: Best, worst, most likely scenarios — understanding range of outcomes
  • Monte Carlo: Random sampling simulation — complex projects with multiple risks
  • Decision Trees: Sequential decisions with probabilities at each node
  • RADR: Higher discount rate for riskier projects (adjusts discount rate)
  • Certainty Equivalent: Adjusts cash flows (not discount rate) for risk — more conservative

Unit 10: Financing Through Domestic Capital Markets

📖 Capital Markets
  • Money Market: Short-term (≤ 1 year) - T-bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit
  • Capital Market: Long-term - shares, debentures, bonds
  • Primary Market: New issues (IPO, FPO, Rights Issue). IPO = Initial Public Offering (first-time); FPO = Follow-on Public Offer (subsequent); Rights Issue = Issue to existing shareholders
  • Secondary Market: Trading existing securities (stock exchanges - BSE, NSE)
  • Book Building: Price discovery mechanism for IPOs

Key Definitions:

  • Primary Market: The market where new securities are issued for the first time (IPO, FPO). Companies raise capital directly from investors.
  • Secondary Market: The market where existing securities are traded between investors (stock exchanges). Companies don't receive funds from these trades.

💡 Real-World Example

Zomato's IPO in 2021 raised Rs 9,375 crore from the primary market. After listing, its shares trade on BSE/NSE in the secondary market. Paytm's IPO raised Rs 18,300 crore — India's largest IPO at the time, though the stock later declined significantly.

Indian Debt Market:

  • G-Secs (Government Securities): Issued by RBI on behalf of Government of India. Safest investment, benchmark for all other debt instruments
  • Corporate Bonds: Issued by companies. Higher yield than G-Secs (credit risk premium). Rated by CRISIL, ICRA, CARE
  • Commercial Paper (CP): Short-term unsecured borrowing (maturity up to 1 year). Used by companies for working capital
  • Certificate of Deposit (CD): Issued by banks. Short-term, negotiable, traded in secondary market
  • Debentures: Long-term secured/unsecured debt. Can be convertible (into equity) or non-convertible

Functions of Stock Exchanges:

  • Continuous Market: Provides liquidity — investors can buy/sell securities at any time during trading hours
  • Price Discovery: Demand and supply determine fair market prices of securities
  • Capital Formation: Channels savings into productive investments through primary market issuances
  • Economic Barometer: Stock market indices (Sensex, Nifty) reflect overall economic health
  • Investor Protection: SEBI regulations ensure fair practices, disclosure, and grievance redressal

Book Building Process:

  • Price Band: Issuer sets a floor price and cap price (e.g., Rs 70-80 per share)
  • Bidding: Institutional and retail investors bid within the price band
  • Price Discovery: Final price determined by demand at various price levels
  • Allotment: Shares allotted at the cut-off price (highest price with full subscription)
  • SEBI Mandate: 75% of shares reserved for Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs) in book-built issues
⚠ Exam Tip

Distinguish primary vs secondary market. Know types of public issues (IPO, FPO, Rights). Book building is the most common IPO pricing mechanism in India — understand the price band and allotment process.

Unit 11: Financing Through Global Capital Markets

📖 Global Financing Instruments
  • GDR (Global Depository Receipts): Listed on European stock exchanges
  • ADR (American Depository Receipts): Listed on US stock exchanges (NYSE, NASDAQ)
  • ECB (External Commercial Borrowings): Borrowings from foreign sources
  • Eurobonds: Bonds issued in foreign currency, outside the country of that currency
  • Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds (FCCBs): Convertible into equity
  • Benefits: Access to larger capital pools, lower cost, global visibility

Key Definitions:

  • GDR: A negotiable instrument issued by a depository bank, representing shares of a foreign company, traded on stock exchanges outside the US.
  • ADR: A negotiable certificate issued by a US bank, representing a specified number of shares in a foreign company, traded on US stock exchanges.

💡 Real-World Example

Infosys has ADRs listed on NYSE, allowing US investors to invest in the Indian company through the American market. ICICI Bank, Wipro, and HDFC Bank also have ADRs/GDRs listed internationally.

ECB (External Commercial Borrowings) Details:

  • Definition: Borrowings by Indian companies from foreign sources (banks, financial institutions, bond holders)
  • RBI Regulation: ECBs regulated by RBI under FEMA guidelines
  • End-Use Restrictions: Cannot be used for real estate, stock market investment, or working capital
  • Benefits: Lower interest rates (especially in developed markets), longer maturity, access to large capital pools
  • Risks: Currency risk (INR depreciation increases repayment burden), regulatory changes

FCCB (Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds):

  • Hybrid Instrument: Combines features of bonds and equity
  • Conversion: Holder can convert bonds into equity shares at predetermined price/ratio
  • Benefit for Issuer: Lower coupon rate (conversion option compensates investor)
  • Benefit for Investor: Downside protection (bond) with upside potential (equity conversion)
  • Indian Example: Many Indian IT and pharma companies issued FCCBs in 2005-2008 period

Unit 12: Other Modes of Financing

📖 Alternative Financing
  • Leasing: Asset use without ownership
  • Operating Lease: Short-term, cancellable, lessor bears risk
  • Finance Lease: Long-term, non-cancellable, lessee bears risk
  • Hire Purchase: Asset ownership transfers after full payment
  • Venture Capital (VC): Funding for startups/high-growth companies
  • Securitization: Converting illiquid assets (loans, receivables) into tradeable securities
  • Supplier's Credit: Credit extended by equipment suppliers

Key Definitions:

  • Finance Lease: A long-term, non-cancellable lease where the lessee bears all risks and rewards of ownership.
  • Securitization: The process of pooling debt obligations (mortgages, receivables) and selling them as tradeable securities to investors.
  • Hire Purchase: Buyer pays in installments; ownership transfers only after full payment. HP interest is calculated on reducing balance method.

Operating vs Finance Lease — Detailed Comparison:

  • Duration: Operating lease = short-term (less than asset life); Finance lease = long-term (covers most of asset life)
  • Cancellation: Operating lease = cancellable; Finance lease = non-cancellable
  • Maintenance: Operating lease = lessor maintains; Finance lease = lessee maintains
  • Risk: Operating lease = lessor bears risk; Finance lease = lessee bears risk
  • Accounting: Operating lease = off-balance sheet; Finance lease = on-balance sheet (asset and liability recorded)
  • Tax Benefit: Operating lease = lease payments deductible; Finance lease = depreciation + interest deductible

DurationShort-termLong-term (full economic life)
CancellableYesNo
MaintenanceLessorLessee
RiskLessor bearsLessee bears
AccountingOff-balance sheetOn-balance sheet
ExampleOffice space rentalHeavy machinery lease
💡 Real-World Example

Flipkart received venture capital funding from Tiger Global, Accel Partners, and others before its acquisition by Walmart. Ola, Zomato, and Paytm all received VC funding in early stages before going public.

Venture Capital Stages:

  • Seed Stage: Initial funding for concept/prototype development (highest risk)
  • Startup Stage: Product development and initial marketing
  • Growth Stage: Scaling operations, expanding market share
  • Expansion Stage: New products, new markets, acquisitions
  • Exit Stage: IPO, trade sale, or buyback (VC realizes returns)

Securitization Process:

  • Step 1: Originator (e.g., bank) pools similar loans (mortgages, auto loans)
  • Step 2: Pool transferred to Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) — bankruptcy-remote entity
  • Step 3: SPV issues securities (asset-backed securities) to investors
  • Step 4: Cash flows from underlying loans passed through to investors
  • Benefits: Liquidity for originator, diversification for investors, lower funding costs

Compare operating lease vs finance lease. Explain securitization process with diagram. Know the stages of venture capital funding and exit mechanisms (IPO, trade sale).

Unit 13: Capital Restructuring (M&A)

📖 Mergers & Acquisitions
  • Mergers: Combination of two companies into one
  • Horizontal: Same industry (e.g., Vodafone + Idea)
  • Vertical: Different stages of same industry (e.g., Reliance Retail + Reliance Jio)
  • Conglomerate: Unrelated businesses (e.g., Tata Group diversification)
  • Acquisitions: Buying controlling interest in another company
  • Divestitures: Selling business units to focus on core operations
  • LBO (Leveraged Buyout): Acquisition using primarily borrowed funds
  • Demerger: Splitting a company into separate entities

M&A Valuation Methods:

  • DCF (Discounted Cash Flow): Value = Sum of projected free cash flows discounted at WACC. Most theoretically sound method
  • Comparable Company Analysis: Value based on multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA) of similar companies
  • Comparable Transaction Analysis: Value based on multiples from recent M&A transactions in same industry
  • Asset-Based Valuation: Net Asset Value = Total Assets - Total Liabilities. Suitable for asset-heavy businesses

Synergy Types in M&A:

  • Revenue Synergy: Combined entity generates more revenue (cross-selling, market access)
  • Cost Synergy: Elimination of duplicate functions (HR, IT, finance), economies of scale
  • Financial Synergy: Lower WACC due to diversification, better debt capacity, tax benefits

Key Definitions:

  • Horizontal Merger: A merger between companies operating in the same industry and at the same stage of production.
  • LBO: An acquisition where the buyer uses a significant amount of borrowed money to meet the cost of acquisition.

Unit 14: Financial Engineering

📖 Financial Engineering
  • Definition: Designing innovative financial instruments and processes to meet specific needs
  • Derivatives: Financial instruments whose value is derived from underlying assets
  • Futures: Obligation to buy/sell at predetermined price on future date
  • Options: Right (not obligation) to buy (call) or sell (put)
  • Swaps: Exchange of cash flows (interest rate swaps, currency swaps)
  • Securitization: Creating tradeable securities from asset pools
  • Structured Products: Customized investment products combining multiple instruments

Key Definitions:

  • Financial Engineering: The use of mathematical techniques, financial theory, and computational tools to design new financial instruments and strategies.
  • Derivative: A financial contract whose value is derived from the performance of an underlying asset, index, or rate.
DerivativeNatureObligationExample
FuturesStandardized exchange-tradedObligation to buy/sellNifty futures
OptionsRight to buy/sellRight, not obligationCall/Put options
SwapsOTC agreementExchange of cash flowsInterest rate swap
💡 Real-World Example

Indian companies use interest rate swaps to convert floating-rate debt to fixed-rate, managing interest rate risk. Reliance Industries uses currency hedging through forwards and options to protect against USD/INR fluctuations on its export revenues.

Derivatives — Detailed Comparison:

  • Futures: Standardized, exchange-traded, daily mark-to-market, margin requirements. Example: Nifty 50 futures on NSE.
  • Options: Call (right to buy) and Put (right to sell). Premium paid upfront. Maximum loss = premium paid. Example: Nifty call options.
  • Swaps: OTC agreements to exchange cash flows. Interest rate swap: Fixed rate vs floating rate. Currency swap: Exchange principal and interest in different currencies.

Structured Products: Customized instruments combining derivatives with traditional securities. Examples include principal-protected notes, convertible bonds, and credit-linked notes. These allow risk-return profiles tailored to specific investor needs.

Unit 15: Investor Relations

📖 Investor Relations & Governance
  • Disclosure: Transparency in financial reporting (SEBI regulations)
  • Corporate Governance: Board responsibilities, independent directors, audit committees
  • Insider Trading: Illegal use of non-public information for trading (SEBI Act)
  • Financial Reporting: Annual reports, quarterly results, corporate governance reports
  • Stakeholder Communication: Regular updates to shareholders, analysts, regulators
  • Code of Corporate Governance: SEBI's LODR (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements)

Key Definitions:

  • Corporate Governance: The system of rules, practices, and processes by which a company is directed and controlled, ensuring accountability to all stakeholders.
  • Insider Trading: Buying or selling securities based on material, non-public information about the company. Illegal under SEBI regulations.

SEBI LODR Key Requirements:

  • Board Composition: Minimum 50% independent directors for top 500 listed companies
  • Audit Committee: Minimum 3 members, all financially literate, majority independent
  • Quarterly Results: Must be filed within 45 days of quarter end
  • Annual Report: Must include corporate governance report, business responsibility report
  • Related Party Transactions: Must be approved by audit committee and disclosed
  • Whistleblower Policy: Mandatory for all listed companies

Corporate Governance Best Practices:

  • Board Independence: Independent directors bring objectivity and protect minority shareholders
  • Separation of Roles: Chairman and CEO roles should be separate for better oversight
  • Remuneration Committee: Determines executive compensation based on performance
  • Risk Management: Board must identify, assess, and mitigate key business risks
💡 Real-World Example

Infosys is known for best-in-class corporate governance practices — high transparency, independent board, regular disclosures — which attracts global investors. Tata Group companies follow strong governance standards with separation of chairman and CEO roles.

⚠ Exam Tip

Discuss corporate governance principles. Know SEBI's role in investor protection. Insider trading provisions are frequently asked.

  4. Capital structure theories — Compare MM, Trade-Off, Pecking Order

Model Answer Structure

For numerical: State formula → Substitute values → Calculate → State decision rule → Recommend.

For descriptive: Define → Explain components → Compare with table → Give examples → State significance.

Expected Question Patterns (10-15 marks each)

  1. "Calculate NPV and IRR from given data and recommend" — Show full working, apply decision rules, give clear recommendation
  2. "Explain CAPM and its application in capital budgeting" — Formula, assumptions, beta interpretation, numerical example
  3. "Compare capital budgeting methods" — Table format with TVM, decision rules, strengths/weaknesses
  4. "Discuss types of mergers with examples" — HVC with Indian examples, motives (synergy, growth, tax benefits)
  5. "Calculate WACC from given information" — All component costs, weights, final WACC, interpretation
  6. "Explain the role of derivatives in financial engineering" — Futures, options, swaps with examples
  7. "Compare GDR vs ADR" — Table with listing, currency, regulation, examples
  8. "Explain PERT and CPM with calculations" — Expected time, variance, critical path identification

Time Management in Exam

  • 15-mark question: 25-30 minutes (numerical: 15 min working + 10 min explanation)
  • 10-mark question: 15-20 minutes
  • Choose wisely: Attempt numerical questions first if confident (guaranteed marks with correct working)
  • Show all steps: Even if final answer is wrong, step marks are awarded
  • Draw diagrams: Network diagrams, SML graphs, flowcharts earn extra marks
  • Revise formulas: Keep last 10 minutes for formula review and cross-checking calculations

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting to tax-adjust cost of debt in WACC calculation
  • Using book values instead of market values for weights in WACC
  • Confusing DOL with DFL (DOL uses EBIT, DFL uses EBIT - Interest)
  • Not stating decision rule after NPV/IRR calculation
  • Ignoring mutually exclusive conflict when comparing NPV and IRR
