Q1. Functions of HR managers and emerging aspects of HRM (20 Marks)

Answer: An HR manager is the bridge between management and employees, performing four managerial functions (planning, organizing, directing, controlling) and four operative functions (acquisition, development, motivation/compensation, maintenance) - now increasingly powered by analytics and technology.

The HR manager performs both managerial and operative functions:

  • Managerial functions: planning (forecasting manpower needs), organizing (creating the HR department structure), directing (guiding and motivating employees), and controlling (checking that HR policies are followed).
  • Operative functions: acquisition (HR planning, recruitment, selection), development (training, career planning, performance appraisal), motivation and compensation (wages, salaries, incentives, rewards), and maintenance (welfare, safety, health, and employee relations, including grievance handling and discipline).

Emerging aspects of HRM functions:

  • HR analytics: data-driven decisions about hiring, attrition, and productivity. Example: HR teams predict which employees are likely to leave using exit data.
  • Technology and AI in HR: chatbots answer employee queries, AI shortlists resumes, and e-HRM software automates payroll and attendance.
  • Remote and hybrid work: HR now manages virtual teams, flexible hours, and work-from-home policies.
  • Gig and freelance workforce: companies combine permanent staff with gig workers, needing new contracts and engagement methods.
  • Diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI): ensuring fair representation of women, disabled, and minority groups at all levels.
  • Employee experience and wellbeing: mental health support, wellness programs, and strong employer branding to attract talent.

Example: an IT company like Infosys uses a digital HR platform where employees manage leave, training, and appraisals online, while HR analytics dashboards show attrition trends to managers.

Key points:
  • Dual functions: managerial (plan, organize, direct, control) + operative (acquire, develop, motivate, maintain).
  • Emerging aspects: HR analytics, AI/technology, remote work, gig workforce, DEI, wellbeing.
  • Examples score: cite a digital HR platform, chatbot, or attrition analytics.

Takeaway: HRM works through four managerial plus four operative functions - always quote one emerging aspect with an Indian example.

Q2. Factors influencing organizational environment + impact of the 4th Industrial Revolution on HRM (20 Marks)

Answer: The organizational environment - political, economic, social, technological, competitive and internal forces - shapes every HRM decision, and Industry 4.0 (AI, IoT, robotics, big data, cloud) is transforming HRM operations from administrative support to strategic, data-driven people management.

Factors influencing the organizational environment:

  • Political-legal: government policies, labor laws, and regulations. Example: new labor codes change how companies hire and fire.
  • Economic: GDP growth, inflation, interest rates, and exchange rates. Example: an economic slowdown forces HR to freeze hiring.
  • Social: demographics, education levels, and attitudes. Example: a young population means a large pool of freshers.
  • Technological: automation, AI, and digital tools that change jobs and skills.
  • Competitive: pay levels and talent practices of rival firms.
  • Internal factors: company strategy, structure, culture, leadership, and employee profile.

Impact of the 4th Industrial Revolution (Industry 4.0) on HRM operations: Industry 4.0 means smart factories and digital business using AI, the Internet of Things, robotics, big data, and cloud computing. Its impact on HRM includes:

  • Automation of routine HR work: payroll, attendance, and leave are now handled by software, freeing HR for strategic work.
  • AI-based recruitment: chatbots screen candidates and AI shortlists resumes, making hiring faster.
  • Predictive HR analytics: attrition, engagement, and performance are predicted from data.
  • New skill demands: HR must reskill and upskill employees for digital jobs and run e-learning platforms.
  • Gig and remote workforce: HR designs policies for freelancers and hybrid work teams.
  • Changing job design: routine jobs shrink; analytical and creative jobs grow.

Example: a manufacturing firm installing robots retrains line workers as robot operators through company e-learning courses - a direct HRM response to Industry 4.0.

Key points:
  • Environment scan: political-legal, economic, social, technological, competitive + internal factors.
  • Industry 4.0: AI, IoT, robotics, big data, cloud.
  • Impact on HRM: automation of HR, AI recruitment, analytics, reskilling, gig/hybrid work.

Takeaway: HRM is environment-sensitive - scan the six environment forces, then show how Industry 4.0 shifts HR from paperwork to analytics.

Q3. Selection and types of selection tests (20 Marks)

Answer: Selection is the process of choosing the most suitable candidate from among the applicants for a job - while recruitment attracts a pool, selection rejects unsuitable applicants and picks the best fit using a sequence of tests, interviews and checks.

Types of selection tests:

  • Aptitude tests: measure the ability to learn a skill. Example: clerical aptitude tests for bank clerks, mechanical aptitude for engineers.
  • Achievement or proficiency tests: measure existing knowledge of the job. Example: a typing speed test for a stenographer, coding tests for programmers.
  • Intelligence tests: measure reasoning, memory, and problem-solving ability.
  • Personality tests: measure traits such as confidence, emotional stability, and teamwork. Example: MBTI or Big Five tests used for sales roles.
  • Interest tests: check whether the candidate's likes match the job. Example: interest inventories for career counselling.
  • Situational or simulation tests: candidates face a realistic work situation. Example: an in-basket exercise where a candidate handles a manager's pending letters; assessment centres use such exercises.
  • Physical ability tests: strength and fitness checks for police, defence, and transport jobs.
  • Graphology and integrity tests: handwriting analysis and honesty checks, used selectively by some employers.

Selection process: preliminary screening, application form, selection tests, interviews, reference checks, medical examination, and final offer. Good tests must be reliable (same result every time) and valid (actually measure what they claim to measure). Example: banks use aptitude and situational tests together with interviews to select probationary officers.

Key points:
  • Selection vs recruitment: recruitment attracts, selection rejects and picks the best fit.
  • Test types: aptitude, achievement, intelligence, personality, interest, situational, physical.
  • Quality tests: must be reliable and valid.
  • Examples: typing test (achievement), MBTI (personality), in-basket (situational).

Takeaway: Selection is a funnel - tests must be job-related, reliable and valid, and each test type needs one concrete example.

Q4. Organizational socialization and integration of individual and organization (20 Marks)

Answer: Organizational socialization is the process by which a newcomer learns the values, norms, culture and expected behaviors of the organization and adapts to them - progressing through three stages (pre-arrival, encounter, metamorphosis) so that individual goals and organizational goals get aligned.

  • Pre-arrival stage: the candidate's expectations about the organization formed before joining - from interviews, brochures, and reputation. Unrealistic expectations cause problems later, so a realistic job preview helps.
  • Encounter stage: the newcomer faces the reality of the organization. If expectations match reality, adjustment is easy; if not, confusion and disappointment follow.
  • Metamorphosis stage: the employee finally adapts - adopts the organization's values and behavior, and feels like an insider.

Integration of the individual and the organization means aligning the employee's needs and goals with the organization's needs and goals. This two-way adjustment is supported by:

  • Orientation programs, buddy systems, and mentors who guide newcomers.
  • A clear psychological contract - the unwritten expectations of fair treatment, growth, and security on both sides.
  • Person-organization fit: matching values, skills, and career goals with the job.
  • Early feedback, challenging first assignments, and recognition that build commitment.

Successful socialization leads to lower turnover, higher satisfaction, and better performance. Example: Infosys runs a months-long training program for freshers that teaches both technical skills and company values, turning trainees into committed employees.

Key points:
  • Definition: learning values, norms, culture; 3 stages: pre-arrival, encounter, metamorphosis.
  • Integration: aligning individual goals with organizational goals.
  • Tools: orientation, mentor/buddy, psychological contract, person-organization fit.
  • Result: commitment, lower turnover, better performance.

Takeaway: Socialization is the induction bridge - name the three stages and the integration tools, then close with a company example.

Q5. Performance appraisal methods and problems in appraisal (20 Marks)

Answer: Performance appraisal is the systematic evaluation of an employee's performance against set standards; its methods divide into traditional (past-oriented, trait/result based) and modern (future-oriented, development based) families - and every method suffers from rating errors like halo, leniency and central tendency.

Performance appraisal is the systematic evaluation of an employee's job performance against set standards. Methods are grouped into two families:

Traditional methods:

  • Confidential report: the boss writes a secret annual report on the employee.
  • Ranking: employees are ranked from best to worst in the department.
  • Paired comparison: each employee is compared with every other employee.
  • Grading: employees are put into grades such as A, B, C.
  • Forced distribution: a fixed percentage of employees must be rated excellent, good, average, or poor.
  • Checklist: the rater ticks statements that describe the employee.
  • Critical incident: the manager records significant good or bad incidents during the year.
  • Essay method: the manager writes a free-form description of performance.

Modern methods: management by objectives (MBO - manager and employee set goals together and review achievement), 360-degree feedback (ratings from boss, peers, subordinates, and customers), behaviorally anchored rating scales (BARS - performance measured against specific behaviors), assessment centres, and human resource accounting.

Problems in appraisal:

  • Halo/horn effect: one good (or bad) quality colors the whole rating.
  • Leniency and strictness errors: some raters are too generous, others too harsh.
  • Central tendency: rating everyone average to avoid conflict.
  • Recency error: judging by the last few weeks instead of the whole year.
  • Personal bias: favoritism based on friendship, caste, or gender.
  • Unclear standards, no feedback to the employee, and resistance from appraisees.

Example: a call centre's forced distribution made good agents feel demotivated; moving to 360-degree feedback with coaching improved fairness.

Key points:
  • Traditional: confidential report, ranking, grading, forced distribution, critical incident, essay.
  • Modern: MBO, 360-degree, BARS, assessment centres.
  • Problems: halo, leniency, central tendency, recency, bias.

Takeaway: Quote two families of methods (traditional vs modern) and at least four rating errors - the example decides your marks.

Q6. Limitations of career planning and strategies for success (20 Marks)

Answer: Career planning matches an employee's career aspirations with organizational opportunities - but it fails without individual differences being respected, realistic paths, and continuous development, which can be fixed through mentoring, dual career ladders and succession planning.

  • Individual differences: employees differ in ability, ambition, and interests; one plan cannot fit everyone.
  • Limited opportunities: organizations have few senior positions, so many employees reach a career plateau.
  • Mismatch of aspirations and reality: employees may expect faster growth than the organization can offer.
  • Job insecurity and economic downturns: recessions and layoffs disrupt even well-made career plans.
  • Skill obsolescence: technology changes fast, and careers built on outdated skills collapse.
  • Poor information: many employees do not know the paths available within the company.

Strategies for making career planning a success:

  • Career counselling and mentoring: guide employees to realistic goals based on their strengths.
  • Dual career ladders: offer a technical path (senior engineer, architect) equal to the managerial path, so specialists are not forced into management.
  • Training and development: continuously update skills through e-learning and job rotation.
  • Succession planning: identify and groom future leaders early.
  • Transparent promotion policies: clear criteria reduce frustration and politics.
  • Periodic career reviews: revisit career plans with employees every year and adjust them as business changes.

Example: IT companies in India offer a "technical architect" track so top programmers earn as much as managers without leaving technology.

Key points:
  • Limitations: individual differences, few positions, plateau, obsolescence, insecurity.
  • Strategies: mentoring, dual career ladders, training, succession planning, transparency.
  • Example: technical architect track in IT firms.

Takeaway: Career planning needs realism - balance limitations with at least four success strategies and one Indian example.

Q7. Notes on compensation survey and executive survey (20 Marks)

Answer: A compensation survey benchmarks pay for comparable jobs to secure external equity, while an executive survey benchmarks top-management pay (salary, bonus, ESOPs, perks) so boards can fix competitive yet defensible packages.

(a) Compensation survey: A compensation survey is a systematic collection of data on the pay and benefits offered by other organizations for comparable jobs. Its purpose is to set competitive wages so the company can attract and retain talent without overpaying.

Sources of data include published surveys, industry associations, consultant reports, government statistics, and direct collection from competitors. The steps are: identify comparable jobs, collect salary data, analyze it (for example, using quartiles and percentiles), and then fix the company's pay structure. A good survey ensures external equity (fair compared with the market) and supports internal equity (fair between jobs in the company). Example: the Indian IT industry annually benchmarks software engineer salaries across cities like Bangalore and Pune using surveys.

(b) Executive survey: An executive compensation survey studies the pay packages of top management - directors, CEOs, and senior vice-presidents. Executive pay has special components beyond basic salary: performance bonuses, stock options and ESOPs (shares given at a future date), perquisites such as company cars, club memberships, and insurance, and long-term incentive plans. Surveys help boards fix competitive but defensible executive pay, because excessively high or secretive pay creates criticism from shareholders and the public. Governance rules (for example, SEBI disclosure norms in India) now require companies to report executive pay. Example: before finalizing a CEO's package, a company's board studies executive surveys of comparable firms in its industry.

Key points:
  • Compensation survey: market pay data for comparable jobs; ensures external equity.
  • Executive survey: top-management pay: salary, bonus, ESOPs, perks.
  • Analysis tools: quartiles/percentiles; governance demands disclosure.

Takeaway: Both surveys serve equity - external equity for employees, defensibility for executives - mention quartiles and SEBI-style disclosure.

Q8. Causes of grievance and the grievance handling procedure (20 Marks)

Answer: A grievance is a formal complaint about something the employee finds unfair (real or imagined) - caused by economic, supervisory, environmental, organizational or interpersonal factors - and is settled through a graded procedure from open-door to arbitration that must be simple, fast and impartial.

A grievance is a formal complaint by an employee about something in the workplace that they find unfair or wrong - whether real or imagined. If not settled, a grievance grows into unrest and industrial disputes.

Causes of grievance:

  • Economic causes: low wages, delayed increments, unfair incentive calculation, or unequal pay for similar work.
  • Supervisory causes: favoritism, rude or biased treatment by a boss, and unfair distribution of work.
  • Work environment causes: unsafe conditions, bad ventilation, and lack of basic facilities.
  • Organizational causes: unfair promotion or transfer policies, unclear roles, and violation of rules.
  • Interpersonal causes: conflict with colleagues or harassment.

Grievance handling procedure (typical steps):

  • Step 1 - Open-door: the employee raises the issue with the first-line supervisor, who tries to settle it within 48 hours.
  • Step 2 - Written complaint: if not settled, the employee files a written grievance with the department head.
  • Step 3 - Grievance committee: a committee (management + employee representatives) hears both sides and gives a decision.
  • Step 4 - Appeal: the employee can appeal to top management if dissatisfied.
  • Step 5 - Arbitration: as a last resort, the matter goes to conciliation or arbitration under labor law, sometimes involving the union.

A good procedure is simple, fast, and impartial, and ensures no victimization of the complainant. Example: a factory worker complained that his overtime was not counted. The supervisor checked the records and corrected it the next day (step 1). When a similar case involved many workers, the grievance committee revised the overtime rule for the whole plant (step 3).

Key points:
  • Causes: economic, supervisory, work environment, organizational, interpersonal.
  • Procedure: open door, written complaint, committee, appeal, arbitration.
  • Principles: simple, fast, impartial, no victimization.

Takeaway: Grievance handling works when it is fast and fair - list the five causes, the five steps, and one resolved-example.

Q1. What is Strategic Human Resource Management (SHRM)? Discuss any two theories of SHRM and their applicability. (20 Marks)

Answer: SHRM links every HR policy and practice to the organization's business strategy so that people are managed as a strategic resource, not an administrative cost.

Key points:

  • Meaning: formulation and execution of HR strategies aligned with corporate strategy; HR becomes a strategic partner in decision-making.
  • Behavioral theory: HR practices (selection, appraisal, rewards, training) are designed to evoke the employee behaviours the strategy needs - cost leadership needs efficiency and compliance, differentiation needs creativity and initiative.
  • Resource-based view (RBV): human capital that is valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable (VRIN) gives sustainable competitive advantage; applicability lies in building and retaining unique talent, e.g., TCS's large training academies create a hard-to-copy workforce.
  • Matching model (Fombrun et al.): strategy and HR systems must fit each other; every strategy change should trigger an HR system change.
  • Applicability: works where top management commits; in India, IT firms, banks and manufacturing groups use SHRM for workforce planning, capability building and culture change.

Takeaway: SHRM = strategy + people; explain two theories (behavioral and RBV are safest) and apply them with an Indian example.

Q2. Discuss the salient features and methods of job analysis citing examples. (20 Marks)

Answer: Job analysis is the systematic process of collecting and recording information about the duties, responsibilities, working conditions and human requirements of a job, producing the job description and job specification.

Key points:

  • Salient features: job identification and title; duties and tasks performed; machines and tools used; working conditions and hazards; supervision given and received; human requirements (education, experience, skills, traits).
  • Outputs: job description (what the job is - tasks and responsibilities) and job specification (who can do it - qualifications and traits).
  • Observation method: watching a shop-floor machine operator and recording tasks step by step - suitable for manual jobs.
  • Interview method: structured questions to job holders and supervisors - suitable for supervisory and knowledge jobs.
  • Questionnaire/PAQ: self-filled structured forms for desk jobs; critical incident and diary methods for jobs with varied events.
  • Example: a bank analyses the "cashier" job by observing counter work and interviewing cashiers, then writes a JD used for hiring and training.

Takeaway: Job analysis is the foundation of HR - its two outputs (JD and JS) feed recruitment, appraisal and training.

Q3. What is career planning? Explain the process of career planning. (20 Marks)

Answer: Career planning is a continuous process of matching an individual's career aspirations, abilities and needs with the opportunities available in the organization.

Key points:

  • Step 1 - Self-assessment: the employee evaluates interests, strengths, weaknesses and values.
  • Step 2 - Organizational analysis: studying career paths, openings and growth opportunities available in the company.
  • Step 3 - Matching: aligning individual needs with organizational opportunities to identify a realistic career direction.
  • Step 4 - Goal setting: fixing short-term and long-term career goals.
  • Step 5 - Action plans: training, job rotation, mentoring and assignments needed to reach the goals.
  • Step 6 - Review: periodic review and revision as the business and the person change.

Takeaway: Career planning is a joint responsibility - mention the six steps and stress that review keeps plans alive.

Q4. Write a descriptive note on the theories of trade unionism and their applicability in present day scenario. (20 Marks)

Answer: Trade unionism grew from labour movements; the classic theories explain why workers organize, and their present-day applicability lies in job protection and collective bargaining.

Key points:

  • Marxian/revolutionary theory: unions are instruments of class struggle against capitalism; applicability today is limited because unions now cooperate through bargaining rather than revolution.
  • Webb's industrial democracy theory: unions give workers a democratic voice in deciding wages, hours and working conditions - still the core of modern collective bargaining.
  • Perlman's job-consciousness theory: workers are job-conscious, not class-conscious; unions defend jobs, wages and skill rights - explains today's defensive unionism during layoffs and automation.
  • Dunlop's systems theory: unions are one of three actors (with employers and state) who jointly make the rules of the industrial relations system.
  • Present-day applicability: unions focus on job security, bonus, safety and resistance to retrenchment - seen in auto and IT worker protests and in negotiations under the new labour codes.

Takeaway: Unionism has moved from revolutionary ideology to pragmatic bargaining - quote Webb, Perlman and Dunlop with a current Indian example.

Q5. Write short notes on any four of the following: (a) Job Specification (b) Artificial Intelligence and HRM (c) Interview (d) Critical Incident Techniques (e) Incentives and Rewards (f) Components of HRM. (20 Marks)

Answer: Any four of the following six notes can be attempted - all six are given here so you can pick the ones you know best.

Key points:

  • (a) Job specification: statement of human requirements of a job - education, experience, skills, physical and personality traits; used to shortlist and select candidates; e.g., bank PO needs graduation, age 20-30, and analytical skills.
  • (b) AI and HRM: AI screens resumes, chatbots answer employee queries, predictive analytics forecast attrition and engagement; it speeds hiring and reduces bias; e.g., AI shortlisting in campus placement portals.
  • (c) Interview: structured face-to-face interaction to judge suitability; types - structured/unstructured, panel, stress, group discussion; valid when structured and job-related; e.g., panel interviews in IBPS/SBI selection.
  • (d) Critical incident technique: recording significant good or bad incidents of behaviour during the year; used for appraisal and training needs; e.g., a nurse's quick emergency response recorded as a critical incident.
  • (e) Incentives and rewards: variable pay linked to performance - individual (piece rate, bonus, commission), group (team bonus, profit sharing), organization-wide (ESOPs, gain sharing); they motivate higher output.
  • (f) Components of HRM: managerial functions (planning, organizing, directing, controlling) plus operative functions (acquisition, development, motivation, maintenance).

Takeaway: In short notes, define first, then list types/functions, then give one example - AI & HRM and incentives are the most repeated.

Section B - Compulsory Case Study (40 marks)

Case in brief: A people-oriented US-headquartered global company runs global processes (annual performance and development planning) with country-specific components. Induction has three tiers - a global web-based programme (strategy, structures, values, reward), a UK "Business and Networking Orientation Event" every 2-3 months with a senior-manager interview exercise and quiz, and a local manager checklist (equipment, first-day plan, health and safety induction). Managers own staff development; HR has no policing role; objectives cascade from divisions to individuals; there is no central check on induction effectiveness.

Case Q1. Critically examine the induction process and its role in placement of employees. (8 Marks)

Answer: The three-tier induction is a well-designed system that strongly supports placement - the process of adjusting a new employee to the job and the organization - but its success depends on manager ownership.

Key points:

  • Global tier: web-based programme standardizes company strategy, structures, values, behaviour and reward - builds a common identity worldwide.
  • Country tier: the networking orientation event connects recruits with senior managers and other businesses, answering "how my role fits the UK business".
  • Local tier: the manager's intranet checklist ensures readiness - equipment, voicemail, first-day plan, health and safety induction.
  • Placement role: induction is the first stage of placement; it reduces anxiety, creates commitment and links with performance planning through six-month objectives.
  • Critical examination: strengths - structured, multi-level, linked to recruitment; weaknesses - no central check on effectiveness, reliance on manager initiative, and the quiz is "unchecked" (not verified for learning).

Takeaway: Induction is placement's first step - judge both the design (three tiers) and the execution gap (no effectiveness check).

Case Q2. Do you think the three-tier induction would be enough for successful placement of employees? (8 Marks)

Answer: Largely yes - the three tiers cover values, context and logistics - but it is sufficient only if managers implement it faithfully and follow-up is built in.

Key points:

  • Coverage: global (values and strategy), country (business context and networks), local (equipment and safety) - every placement need is touched.
  • Reinforcement: the senior-manager interview exercise, feedback session and quiz reinforce learning actively, not passively.
  • Gaps: no compliance monitoring, no evaluation of effectiveness, and effectiveness depends entirely on each manager's diligence.
  • Safety net: the performance planning process (six-month objectives, regular reviews, open-door policy) catches placement failures early.

Takeaway: Three tiers are necessary but not sufficient - name the gaps (no evaluation) and the safety net (performance planning).

Case Q3. Can the localization of placement be improved? If so, how? (8 Marks)

Answer: Yes - localization can be improved by giving local units more flexibility, ownership and feedback loops within the global framework.

Key points:

  • Customize content: adapt local induction to regional language, culture and business priorities while keeping global values common.
  • Local design ownership: let local line managers co-design the checklist and induction day so they feel accountable.
  • Share best practice: circulate successful local innovations (like the UK networking event) across countries.
  • Measure locally: collect new-hire feedback, early turnover and time-to-productivity data per country to find weak spots.
  • Keep the global core: strategy, values and process standards remain global; only delivery is localized.

Takeaway: Localization improves with ownership + measurement - global framework, local delivery, and data feedback.

Case Q4. Does the performance planning have a role in placement? (8 Marks)

Answer: Yes - performance planning completes placement by converting induction learning into objectives, reviews and accountability.

Key points:

  • Objectives: new staff get short-term objectives for about six months, turning orientation into direction.
  • Reviews: regular reviews detect ineffective induction early - managers must explain non-progress.
  • Automation: the system carries forward previous objectives and development actions, keeping continuity.
  • Salary link: ratings indicate salary increases, giving placement a performance consequence.
  • Open-door: any dissatisfied employee can reach senior managers - an escape valve for placement failure.

Takeaway: Placement ends where performance planning begins - objectives, reviews and the open door consolidate integration.

Case Q5. Critically examine the individualization of performance planning vis-à-vis centralization and its effectiveness in an organization. (8 Marks)

Answer: The company blends a centralized global calendar with individualized objectives and manager ownership - a balanced design that is effective where managerial capability is high.

Key points:

  • Centralization benefits: one process, one timing worldwide - consistent, comparable and automated ratings.
  • Individualization benefits: objectives and development actions fit each person and local context; employees own their development.
  • Effectiveness: objective-based ratings with a salary link focus effort on results; the open-door policy and employee comment sections add voice.
  • Risks: rigor varies with the manager, there is no central quality check, and trust-based implementation may fail in low-trust cultures.
  • Balance: centralize the framework and calendar; individualize goals and development - the correct mix depends on manager capability and culture.

Takeaway: Don't choose sides - centralize the framework, individualize the content; effectiveness hinges on manager capability.

Q1. Define Human Resource Management. Discuss the roles and responsibilities of HR managers and the challenges faced by them citing examples. (20 Marks)

Answer: HRM is the management of people - acquiring, developing, motivating and maintaining human resources - to achieve individual and organizational goals.

Key points:

  • Strategic partner: aligning people strategy with business strategy - workforce planning, talent pipelines.
  • Administrative expert: policies, records, payroll, compliance with labour laws.
  • Employee champion: wellbeing, grievances, work-life balance, recognition.
  • Change agent: leading transformation - remote work adoption, digitalization, restructuring.
  • Challenges: talent attraction and retention (high IT attrition), technology and AI adoption, diversity and inclusion, gig and hybrid workforce, mental health, compliance with the new labour codes.
  • Example: during COVID-19, HR managers became change agents rolling out work-from-home and wellbeing programs overnight.

Takeaway: HR managers wear four hats - strategic partner, administrative expert, employee champion, change agent; quote one challenge with an example.

Q2. Describe the process of human resource planning with illustrations. Briefly discuss the problems in human resource planning. (20 Marks)

Answer: HR planning (HRP) forecasts the organization's future manpower needs and ensures the right number of people with the right skills at the right time.

Key points:

  • Step 1 - Environmental analysis: study business plans, technology and labour market conditions.
  • Step 2 - Forecasting demand: estimate future manpower needs from sales/production plans - quantitative and qualitative techniques.
  • Step 3 - Forecasting supply: assess internal supply (inventory of present employees, promotions, retirements) and external availability.
  • Step 4 - Matching: compare demand with supply to find surplus or deficit.
  • Step 5 - Action plans: recruit, train, transfer or redeploy for deficits; VRS, freeze or redeployment for surplus.
  • Illustration: a bank opening 50 branches forecasts a need of 500 staff; supply analysis shows 300 are promotable; the gap of 200 is met by campus recruitment and training.
  • Problems: uncertainty of the future, inaccurate forecasting, data gaps, resistance to planning, changing technology and attrition, HRP treated as mere paperwork.

Takeaway: HRP is a demand-supply matching exercise - give the five steps, one illustration, and at least four problems.

Q3. Discuss the salient features of job analysis. Explain any two methods of job analysis with the help of suitable examples. (20 Marks)

  • Transfer (lateral): same-level movement - branch to branch for exposure or family reasons.
  • Demotion (downward): lowering rank for poor performance or restructuring.
  • Separation (exit): resignation, retirement, retrenchment, VRS, dismissal - permanent movement out.
  • Examples: a bank officer promoted to manager; a trainee transferred across departments for rotation; an IT firm's VRS during restructuring.

Takeaway: Mobility = promotion, transfer, demotion, separation - one example for each form completes the answer.

Q6. Describe the concept of employee empowerment and its benefits, highlighting the types of employee empowerment. (20 Marks)

Answer: Empowerment gives employees the authority, resources and accountability to make decisions within their scope - from information sharing to self-managed teams.

Key points:

  • Benefits: faster decisions, higher motivation and ownership, better customer service, more innovation, reduced supervision cost, stronger retention.
  • Types/levels: information sharing → suggestion schemes → consultation and committees → job enrichment → autonomy in decisions → self-managed teams.
  • Example: empowered customer-service agents resolve complaints without manager approval - as in Ritz-Carlton's $2,000 guest-problem rule; Indian IT teams give engineers autonomy in sprint decisions.
  • Conditions: empowerment needs trust, skills, clear boundaries and accountability - it fails when authority is given without support.

Takeaway: Empowerment = authority + resources + accountability; climb the ladder from information sharing to self-managed teams.

Q7. Define discipline and enumerate the factors leading to indiscipline. Highlight forms of indiscipline. (20 Marks)

Answer: Discipline is orderly conduct in accordance with the organization's rules and expected standards - built by fair rules, consistent enforcement and good supervision.

Key points:

  • Factors leading to indiscipline: unfair or unclear rules, inconsistent enforcement, weak and biased supervision, favouritism, low wages and low morale, defective grievance handling, union-management conflict, personal problems.
  • Forms of indiscipline: minor - lateness, absenteeism, negligence, careless work; major - insubordination, dishonesty, theft, fighting, sabotage, strikes.
  • Positive vs negative discipline: positive discipline creates self-discipline through clear standards and fair leadership; negative discipline relies on punishment and fear.
  • Example: habitual late attendance (minor) escalates to disciplinary action if repeated; a theft (major) leads to dismissal after an enquiry.

Takeaway: Define discipline, list six-plus causes, split forms into minor/major - end with the enquiry-and-punishment sequence.

Q8. Write short notes on any four of the following: (a) Job specification (b) HR analytics (c) Separation (d) Forms of socialisation (e) Rewards management (f) Career anchor. (20 Marks)

Answer: Any four can be attempted - all six notes are given below.

Key points:

  • (a) Job specification: the human requirements of a job - education, experience, skills and traits needed to perform it; the selection benchmark derived from job analysis.
  • (b) HR analytics: data-driven HR decisions - attrition prediction, hiring-funnel analysis, engagement scores; example - exit-survey data modelling which teams are at retention risk.
  • (c) Separation: the permanent exit of an employee - resignation, retirement, retrenchment, VRS, dismissal; managed through exit interviews, full-and-final settlement and knowledge transfer.
  • (d) Forms of socialisation: Van Maanen's dimensions - formal vs informal, individual vs collective, sequential vs random, fixed vs variable; or the three stages: pre-arrival, encounter, metamorphosis.
  • (e) Rewards management: designing and administering total rewards - pay, incentives, benefits and recognition - for internal/external equity and motivation.
  • (f) Career anchor (Schein): the self-perceived talents, motives and values that guide career choices - e.g., technical/functional competence, managerial competence, autonomy, security, creativity, entrepreneurship.

Takeaway: Short notes = definition + one example; Schein's anchors and Van Maanen's forms are favourite exam picks.
