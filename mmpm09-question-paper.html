Actual IGNOU Term-End Examination question paper (June 2023, from the official IGNOU previous-year papers archive). Answer any three questions from Section A; Section B is compulsory.

Note: Answer any three questions from Section A. Section B is compulsory.

Solved Answers - MMPM-09 Question Paper

Easy model answers in plain English for every question of the June 2023 paper, grounded in the 16 units of the StudyHub study notes.

Q1(a). Explain: Hypermarket, Franchising, Catalogue Retailing, Private Labels. Q1(b). Role, scope and importance of retailing and its contribution to the Indian economy.

Answer: Hypermarket, franchising, catalogue retailing and private labels are four distinct retail formats, and retailing itself is the final link of the distribution chain that drives consumption and employment in the Indian economy.

(i) Hypermarket: a very large self-service store that combines a supermarket and a department store, selling food, groceries, apparel and general merchandise under one roof - typically 30,000-50,000 square feet with 30,000+ SKUs. Reliance Smart is a leading Indian example.

(ii) Franchising: an arrangement in which the franchisor licenses its brand name, products and operating system to a franchisee in return for fees and royalties; the franchisee runs the outlet to the franchisor’s standards. McDonald’s is the classic example - it trains franchise operators at Hamburger University to ensure global consistency.

(iii) Catalogue Retailing: a non-store retail format that sells through printed or digital catalogues, with orders placed by mail, telephone or online; it suits wide assortments such as apparel and consumer durables.

(iv) Private Labels: brands owned and sold exclusively by a retailer, offering better margins and differentiation - DMart earns 30%+ of its revenue from private labels across grocery, FMCG and apparel.

(b) Role and scope: retailing is the final link in the distribution chain that connects producers to consumers - it breaks bulk, holds inventory, offers assortment and provides service, covering everything from neighbourhood kiranas to hypermarkets and online stores. Importance and contribution: India has 12 million+ small retailers; kirana stores still hold about 88% of the market, while organised retail is growing at 25-30% annually. Retailing is a major employer, contributes significantly to the GDP, and drives consumption, modernisation and employment across tier II and tier III cities.

Key points to remember: hypermarket = big-box food + non-food; franchising = licensed brand system; catalogue = non-store ordering; private labels = retailer-owned brands. Retailing = final link, kirana 88% share, organised retail growing 25-30%.

Takeaway: Anchor each term to its distinguishing feature - size (hypermarket), brand licensing (franchising), ordering channel (catalogue) and brand ownership (private labels) - and retailing's role as the final link of distribution.

Q2(a). Strategic planning process and four planning approaches for retailers. Q2(b). Single-store independent ownership: features, advantages and limitations with an example.

Answer: Strategic planning is a seven-step cycle from mission to evaluation and control, executed through four approaches - top-down, bottom-up, combination and intuitive - while single-store independent ownership trades scale and bargaining power for flexibility and personal service.

(a) Strategic planning process: 1) define the mission, 2) conduct a situation analysis (SWOT - strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats), 3) set objectives, 4) formulate strategy, 5) allocate resources, 6) implement, and 7) evaluate and control. The four planning approaches a retailer can consider are: top-down (senior management decides the plan and lower levels execute it), bottom-up (plans and proposals flow up from store and department level and are consolidated), combination (a blend of both, balancing top-level vision with ground-level input), and intuitive/entrepreneurial (the owner plans from experience and judgement - common in small and single-store retail).

(b) Single-store independent ownership is one shop owned by an individual or family, where the owner usually manages daily operations. Features: full ownership and control, local market focus, personalised service, quick decisions, and direct contact with customers. Advantages: independence, flexibility, retained profits, close customer relationships and adaptability to local tastes. Limitations: limited capital, weak bargaining power with suppliers, no economies of scale, narrow assortment, and heavy dependence on the owner’s skills. Example: a neighbourhood kirana store that knows each customer by name, offers credit and home delivery - profitable but constrained in range and growth.

Key points to remember: planning = mission → SWOT → objectives → strategy → control; approaches = top-down, bottom-up, combined, intuitive. Single-store = independence and service, limited capital and scale.

Takeaway: Present planning as mission-SWOT-objectives-strategy-control, contrast the four approaches on control versus responsiveness, and for the single store balance independence against capital limits.

Q3(a). Two locational theories and retail market identification in the Indian context. Q3(b). Merchandising: definition, importance, scope and factors for merchandising objectives.

Answer: Central Place Theory and Reilly's Law of Retail Gravitation explain how retail centres serve trade areas by hierarchy and by size-versus-distance pull, and retail market identification fixes the trade area, purchasing power and competition before a store is located.

(a) Locational theories: the Central Place Theory (Christaller) says settlements form a hierarchy of centres, and each retail centre serves a market area of a minimum (threshold) population - so higher-order centres like malls serve larger areas than neighbourhood stores. The Spatial Interaction / Reilly’s Law of Retail Gravitation holds that a centre attracts customers from surrounding areas in proportion to its size and in inverse proportion to distance - used to estimate trade areas and divide markets between competing centres. Retail market identification means defining the trade area (primary, secondary, tertiary), assessing market potential, purchasing power and competition before locating a store. In emerging India, these tools guide hypermarket and mall locations in growing tier II cities while kiranas continue to serve neighbourhood catchments.

(b) Merchandising is the planning, buying and selling of products so that the right product is available in the right place, at the right time, in the right quantity and at the right price. Its importance lies in driving sales, margins and store image; its scope covers assortment planning, buying, pricing, promotion, inventory and in-store display. Factors to consider while setting merchandising objectives: target customer profile, store image and positioning, competition, desired sales volume and profit margins, budget, product life cycles, and seasonal or fashion trends.

Key points to remember: Central Place = hierarchy + threshold population; Reilly = size vs distance. Merchandising = right product, place, time, quantity, price; objectives = customer, image, competition, profit.

Takeaway: Link each theory to its tool - Central Place to threshold population and hierarchy, Reilly to trade-area division - and close with trade-area analysis in a tier II city.

Q4. Short notes: Emerging Rural Retail Formats, Dialectic Process Theory, Unplanned Markets, Category, Visual Merchandising In India.

Answer: The five short notes span rural formats, format-evolution theory, organic market clusters, managed product groups and in-store presentation - each definable in one line with an Indian example.

(a) Emerging Rural Retail Formats: rural markets are being served by modernised haats and melas, rural kiosks and company outlets, mobile vans and chiller vans for perishables, and hybrid stores blending traditional and organised retail - initiatives such as ITC e-Choupal and HUL Project Shakti show how companies reach rural consumers through local partners.

(b) Dialectic Process Theory: retail formats evolve in a cycle - a new format (thesis) attracts imitators and competition (antithesis), and the two blend into a hybrid format (synthesis). For example, department stores and discount stores competed, and the synthesis produced modern hypermarkets and category killers.

(c) Unplanned Markets: markets that grow naturally without formal planning - central business districts, traditional bazaars, railway-station markets and weekend street markets - located near transport nodes and dense population, where clustering of shops creates customer traffic.

(d) Category: a unit of merchandise management - a group of similar, substitutable products managed as one business unit (for example, oral care or snacks). Category management links buying decisions to store-level sales and profit targets.

(e) Visual Merchandising In India: the art of presenting products through window displays, fixtures, lighting, planograms and store layout to attract customers and drive impulse purchases - visual merchandising influences 60-70% of impulse purchase decisions. Indian examples: Tanishq’s jewellery displays and DMart’s end-cap displays of private labels.

Key points to remember: rural formats = modernised haats, vans, company outlets; dialectic = thesis-antithesis-synthesis; unplanned markets = organic clusters; category = managed product groups; visual merchandising = 60-70% of impulse purchases.

Takeaway: Structure each note as definition + process + Indian example; the dialectic (thesis-antithesis-synthesis) and the wheel of retailing are the same cycle seen from two angles.

Q5. Case: (i) criteria for locating digital marketing training centres in tier II cities of Southern India (ii) why mom-and-pop stores remain favoured by marketers and customers.

Answer: A training-centre location is judged on demographics, accessibility, rent, competition, infrastructure and growth potential, while mom-and-pop stores survive on trust, credit, convenience and low-cost reach for marketers.

(i) Location criteria for training centres: 1) population size and target segment (students, young professionals, working people), 2) purchasing power and fee-paying capacity of the local market, 3) accessibility - proximity to bus stands, railway stations and main roads, 4) visibility and signage, 5) rent and operating costs within budget, 6) competition from existing institutes and their course prices, 7) infrastructure - reliable power, internet connectivity and classrooms for digital training, 8) proximity to colleges, IT parks and business districts, 9) availability of skilled trainers willing to relocate, and 10) growth potential of the city. Tier II cities in the South with young demographics and a growing services sector score well on most criteria.

(ii) Why mom-and-pop stores stay favoured: customers value personal relationships and trust, credit facilities, home delivery, convenience and proximity, flexible timings, local language, small affordable pack sizes and emotional bonds with the neighbourhood shopkeeper. Marketers favour them for the sheer reach - 12 million+ kiranas accounting for roughly 88% of Indian retail - low distribution cost, cash sales, and deep knowledge of local tastes that helps in merchandising and promotions. Together this makes the kirana an efficient, resilient channel that organised retail complements rather than replaces.

Key points to remember: location = demographics, accessibility, rent, infrastructure, competition, growth. Kiranas = trust, credit, convenience for customers; reach and low cost for marketers.

Takeaway: Judge location on hard criteria (population, rent, access) and kiranas on soft ones (trust, credit, relationships) - each part of the question is scored on a different yardstick.
