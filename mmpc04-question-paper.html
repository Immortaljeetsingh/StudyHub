Q1. What is a Journal? Advantages and process of Journalizing, with recording of four transactions

Answer: A Journal is the book of original entry where every financial transaction is recorded first, in date order, before it is posted to the ledger; it is also called the “book of prime entry”, and every entry carries a short explanation called a narration.

This means the Journal gives a complete chronological record, while trade discounts are never journalised — only the net amount appears in the books.

Advantages of the Journal:

  • It gives a complete chronological (date-wise) record of all transactions.
  • Each entry shows both the debit and the credit with a short explanation called narration.
  • It reduces errors because every transaction is analysed before recording.
  • It helps in preparing the ledger and the trial balance.

Process of Journalizing (7 steps of the accounting cycle): identify the transaction from the source document → decide which two accounts are affected → apply the golden rules (Real: debit what comes in, credit what goes out; Personal: debit the receiver, credit the giver; Nominal: debit expenses/losses, credit incomes/gains) → record the debit first, then the credit → write the narration → post to the ledger → prepare the trial balance.

Recording the four transactions:

  • Trade discount: not recorded at all in the journal. Only the net amount is recorded. Example: goods listed at ₹10,000 with 10% trade discount are recorded at ₹9,000.
  • Goods distributed as free sample: Dr Advertising Expenses A/c, Cr Purchases A/c (a selling expense; the goods leave purchases).
  • Depreciation charged on fixed assets: Dr Depreciation A/c, Cr Fixed Asset A/c (or Provision for Depreciation A/c).
  • Bad debts recovered: Dr Cash/Bank A/c, Cr Bad Debts Recovered A/c (an income).

Key points

  • Journal is the first book of entry — chronological record with narration for every entry.
  • Golden rules: Real — debit what comes in, credit what goes out; Personal — debit the receiver, credit the giver; Nominal — debit expenses/losses, credit incomes/gains.
  • Trade discount is never journalised — only the net amount is recorded.
  • Free samples = advertising expense (Dr Advertising Expenses, Cr Purchases).
  • Depreciation: Dr Depreciation A/c, Cr Fixed Asset / Provision for Depreciation A/c.
  • Bad debts recovered is income: Dr Cash/Bank, Cr Bad Debts Recovered A/c.

Takeaway: Journal first, ledger second — and trade discount never appears in the journal at all.

Q2. Short notes: Accrual concept, Materiality concept, Cost of goods sold, Depreciation, Current assets

Answer:

  • Accrual concept: Revenue is recorded when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, not when cash changes hands. Example: March salary paid on 5th April is still an expense of March. This concept makes the profit and loss account match income with the expenses of the same period.
  • Materiality concept: Only items significant enough to influence a user’s decision need separate disclosure. Example: a ₹100 stapler is treated as an expense at once rather than an asset depreciated over 10 years, because its value is not material.
  • Cost of goods sold (COGS): The direct cost of the goods actually sold. Formula: COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases + Direct Expenses − Closing Stock. Example: ₹50,000 + ₹3,00,000 + ₹20,000 − ₹70,000 = ₹3,00,000. Gross Profit = Sales − COGS.
  • Depreciation: The systematic allocation of the cost of a fixed asset over its useful life. Example: machinery costing ₹1,00,000 with a life of 10 years gives ₹10,000 depreciation per year under the straight-line method. It is a non-cash expense, so it is added back in the cash flow statement.
  • Current assets: Assets that can be converted into cash within 12 months — cash, bank balance, debtors, stock, and short-term investments. Example: a bank’s loans maturing within a year are current assets, while its building is a non-current asset.

Key points

  • Accrual = record revenue when earned, expenses when incurred — not when cash moves.
  • Materiality = only items big enough to change a user’s decision need separate disclosure.
  • COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases + Direct Expenses − Closing Stock; Gross Profit = Sales − COGS.
  • Depreciation is a non-cash expense spread over useful life — added back in the cash flow statement.
  • Current assets convert into cash within 12 months: cash, bank, debtors, stock, short-term investments.

Takeaway: Accrual and materiality decide when and what to record; COGS and depreciation decide the profit figure; current assets decide liquidity.

Q3. What is cost? Elements, classification and calculation of total cost

Answer:

Cost is the value of economic resources sacrificed to achieve a specific objective — for example, the money spent on material, wages, rent and power to produce a product. Cost accounting records, classifies and analyses these costs for control and decision-making.

Elements of cost (3 elements):

  • Material: raw materials and components used in production.
  • Labour: wages and salaries paid to workers and staff.
  • Expenses (overheads): all other costs such as rent, power, insurance and advertising.

Classification of costs:

  • By nature: direct (traceable to the product, like steel for Maruti) vs indirect (factory rent, supervisor salary).
  • By behaviour: fixed (rent, constant at any output), variable (raw material, changes with output), semi-variable (electricity).
  • By function: production, administration, selling and distribution.
  • By controllability: controllable vs uncontrollable.

How total cost is calculated (cost sheet ladder): Prime Cost = Direct Material + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses → Works Cost = Prime Cost + Factory Overheads → Cost of Production = Works Cost + Office & Admin Overheads → Total Cost = Cost of Production + Selling & Distribution Overheads → Profit = Sales − Total Cost. Example: Maruti classifies steel (65% of car cost) as direct material and builds up the cost sheet stage by stage to fix the selling price.

Key points

  • Cost has 3 elements: Material + Labour + Expenses (overheads).
  • Classify by nature (direct/indirect), behaviour (fixed/variable/semi-variable), function (production/admin/selling) and controllability.
  • Cost sheet ladder: Prime → Works → Cost of Production → Total Cost → Profit = Sales − Total Cost.
  • Prime cost = Direct Material + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses; works cost adds factory overheads.

Takeaway: Every rupee of cost can be traced up the ladder — learn the 4 classification bases and the cost sheet sequence.

Q4. Budget, significance of budgetary control, installation process and control ratios

Answer:

A budget is a quantitative expression of a plan for a defined future period — for example, L&T’s annual sales budget of ₹2,00,000 crore. Budgetary control is the system of comparing actual results with budgets and taking corrective action, so a budget without control is just a wish.

Significance of budgetary control:

  • It forces planning — every department must decide its targets in advance.
  • It coordinates the activities of sales, production, finance and purchase departments.
  • It fixes responsibility — actual results are compared with the budget of the same manager.
  • It motivates employees by setting clear, achievable targets.
  • It acts as an early warning system through variance analysis.

Process of installing budgetary control: (1) appoint a budget committee and a budget officer; (2) fix the budget period; (3) define budget centres and responsibilities; (4) prepare a budget manual giving procedures; (5) identify the key (limiting) factor such as sales or raw material; (6) prepare functional budgets and integrate them into the master budget; (7) monitor actuals, analyse variances and take corrective action.

Control ratios: Capacity Ratio = (Actual Hours ÷ Budgeted Hours) × 100; Activity Ratio = (Standard Hours for Actual Output ÷ Budgeted Hours) × 100; Efficiency Ratio = (Standard Hours for Actual Output ÷ Actual Hours) × 100; Calendar Ratio = (Actual Working Days ÷ Budgeted Working Days) × 100. Activity = Capacity × Efficiency, which is a favourite exam link.

Key points

  • Budget = plan expressed in numbers; budgetary control = compare actuals with budget and correct.
  • Install in 7 steps: committee & officer → budget period → budget centres → manual → key factor → functional budgets → master budget.
  • Control ratios: Capacity = Actual hrs ÷ Budgeted hrs; Activity = Std hrs for actual output ÷ Budgeted hrs; Efficiency = Std hrs for actual output ÷ Actual hrs.
  • Activity Ratio = Capacity Ratio × Efficiency Ratio — the favourite exam link.
  • Flexible budgets are best for performance evaluation when output changes.

Takeaway: Budget without control is a wish — the exam rewards the 7-step process and the three control ratio formulas.

Q5. (a) Altman’s Z score  (b) Du Pont Analysis

Answer:

(a) Altman’s Z score is a credit-strength model that predicts the probability of a company going bankrupt within two years. Professor Edward Altman combined five financial ratios into one score:

Z = 1.2X1 + 1.4X2 + 3.3X3 + 0.6X4 + 1.0X5

  • X1 = Working Capital ÷ Total Assets (liquidity)
  • X2 = Retained Earnings ÷ Total Assets (cumulative profitability)
  • X3 = EBIT ÷ Total Assets (operating efficiency)
  • X4 = Market Value of Equity ÷ Book Value of Debt (leverage)
  • X5 = Sales ÷ Total Assets (asset turnover)

Interpretation: Z above 2.99 = safe zone; between 1.81 and 2.99 = grey zone; below 1.81 = distress zone, high bankruptcy risk. Banks use it to screen loan applicants, like checking a company before sanctioning a working capital limit.

(b) Du Pont Analysis decomposes Return on Equity (ROE) into three drivers: ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier. Net Profit Margin (NP ÷ Sales) measures profitability; Asset Turnover (Sales ÷ Total Assets) measures efficiency; Equity Multiplier (Total Assets ÷ Equity) measures leverage. A high ROE could come from high margins, efficient use of assets, or heavy debt — so Du Pont shows how a company earns its returns. Example: if a company’s margin is 10%, turnover 2 times and multiplier 1.5, ROE = 10% × 2 × 1.5 = 30%.

Key points

  • Z score uses 5 weighted ratios to predict bankruptcy: Z < 1.81 distress, 1.81–2.99 grey, > 2.99 safe.
  • Z = 1.2X1 + 1.4X2 + 3.3X3 + 0.6X4 + 1.0X5 (liquidity, retained earnings, EBIT, market leverage, asset turnover).
  • Du Pont: ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier.
  • Du Pont separates profitability, efficiency and leverage — a high ROE is not always good (could be debt-driven).

Takeaway: Z-score answers “will the company fail?”; Du Pont answers “where does the return come from?” — memorise both formulas with their components.

Q6. Annual Report: financial contents and Notes to the Accounts for investors

Answer:

An Annual Report is a comprehensive document that communicates a company’s financial performance, governance and strategic direction to stakeholders — shareholders, investors, lenders, employees and regulators. Under the Companies Act 2013, it is published every year after the accounts are audited.

Financial contents of an Annual Report:

  • Balance Sheet: financial position — assets = liabilities + equity (Schedule III format).
  • Statement of Profit & Loss: revenue, expenses and profit for the year.
  • Cash Flow Statement: cash from operating, investing and financing activities.
  • Statement of Changes in Equity: movements in share capital and reserves.
  • Director’s Report: outlook, dividends and risk discussion.
  • Auditor’s Report: the auditor’s opinion — unqualified (clean), qualified, adverse or disclaimer.

Notes to the Accounts: these are the detailed schedules attached to the financial statements. They contain accounting policies (e.g., method of depreciation), contingent liabilities, related party transactions, segment-wise information, loans and guarantees, and details of fixed assets.

Significance for investors: investors cannot judge a company from the balance sheet alone; notes reveal the assumptions behind the numbers, hidden risks (like pending court cases), and how income was recognised. Example: investors reading Infosys’s annual report check the notes for employee cost, forex gains and related party dealings before deciding to buy the share.

Key points

  • Annual report = financial statements + reports (directors, auditors) + notes to accounts.
  • Auditor’s report tells if numbers are reliable — 4 opinions: Unqualified, Qualified, Adverse, Disclaimer (DAUA).
  • Notes expose accounting policies, contingent liabilities, related-party deals and hidden risks.
  • Investors must read notes before deciding — balance sheet alone hides assumptions.

Takeaway: The notes are where the real story is — for an investor, the audit opinion and accounting policies matter more than the headline profit.

Q7. Human Resource Accounting (HRA) as a management decision tool

Answer:

Human Resource Accounting (HRA) is the process of identifying, measuring and communicating information about human resources so that people are treated as assets of the organisation, not just expenses. The traditional view treats salary and training as costs; the HRA view treats employees as value creators whose skills are capital investments.

HRA valuation methods: Historical Cost (actual recruitment + training cost), Replacement Cost (cost to replace an employee at today’s rates), Opportunity Cost (what competitors would pay), and the Lev & Schwartz Model (discounted present value of future earnings). Infosys pioneered HRA disclosure in India and reports its human resource value using the Lev & Schwartz model every year.

HRA as a management decision tool:

  • Recruitment vs training decision: if training a current employee (₹1 lakh) is cheaper than hiring a new one (₹5 lakh), HRA data supports training.
  • Attrition control: Infosys found every 1% rise in attrition reduces its HR asset value by about ₹500 crore — so retention budgets get justified with numbers.
  • Budgeting for HR: manpower planning, salary increments and perks are planned against the value employees create.
  • Performance evaluation: managers are judged on whether the human capital value they manage is rising or falling.
  • Investor communication: in knowledge companies, 70% of market value is intangible (people, brands, IP) — HRA makes this visible to shareholders.

Key points

  • HRA treats people as assets, not expenses — four methods: Historical, Replacement, Opportunity, Present Value (HROP).
  • Infosys pioneered HRA disclosure in India using the Lev & Schwartz present-value model.
  • Decisions supported: recruit vs train, attrition control, HR budgeting, performance evaluation, investor communication.
  • Challenges: no accounting standard, high implementation cost, valuation subjectivity.

Takeaway: In a knowledge company most value sits in people — HRA turns that into numbers managers can plan and budget with.

  • Two-period CVP trick: P/V = change in profit ÷ change in sales.

Takeaway: Sales below BEP destroy margin of safety fast — the same 50% P/V ratio that gave ₹12,00,000 safety in half one left only ₹6,00,000 for the year.

Q2. How are business activities classified for cash flow purposes? Explain the indirect method of preparing the cash flow statement

Answer: Business activities are classified into Operating, Investing and Financing activities; the indirect method derives cash from operating activities by starting with net profit and adjusting for non-cash items, working capital changes and non-operating gains/losses.

Classification (AS-3 / Ind AS 7):

  • Operating activities: the principal revenue-producing activities — cash from customers, cash paid to suppliers and employees, taxes. Examples: sale of goods, payment to creditors, salaries.
  • Investing activities: acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and investments. Examples: purchase of machinery, sale of land, loans given, interest/dividends received.
  • Financing activities: changes in equity and borrowings. Examples: issue of shares, bank loans, repayment of loans, dividends and interest paid.

Indirect method (operating cash flow): Start with Net Profit before tax → add back non-cash items (depreciation, goodwill written off) → adjust for non-operating items (profit on sale of asset deducted, loss added) → adjust for changes in working capital (increase in debtors/stock deducted, increase in creditors added) → the result is Net Cash from Operating Activities.

Key points

  • Operating: day-to-day business; Investing: fixed assets and investments; Financing: equity and borrowings.
  • Indirect method formula: Operating cash = Net profit + non-cash items (depreciation) ± non-operating items ± working capital changes.
  • Increase in stock/debtors reduces cash; increase in creditors increases cash.
  • Profit on sale of asset is deducted; loss on sale is added back.
  • Interest received is investing (AS-3); interest paid may be investing or financing.

Takeaway: Profit is opinion, cash is fact — the indirect method reconciles the two line by line.

Q3. What do you understand by cost? Explain the classification of costs based upon the variability of operations

Answer: Cost is the sacrifice of resources to achieve an objective; on the basis of variability with output, costs split into fixed, variable and semi-variable costs.

Classification by variability (behaviour):

  • Fixed costs: constant in total over a period regardless of output — rent, insurance, salaries of managers, depreciation (time basis). Per unit, they fall as output rises.
  • Variable costs: change in total in direct proportion to output — raw material, direct wages, power per unit. Per unit, they stay constant.
  • Semi-variable (semi-fixed) costs: partly fixed, partly variable — electricity bill (fixed line rent + per-unit charge), telephone, maintenance. They step up when output crosses a level.

Key points

  • Total fixed cost is constant but fixed cost per unit falls as output rises.
  • Total variable cost rises but variable cost per unit is constant.
  • Semi-variable costs need segregation — high-low method or scatter diagram — for marginal costing.
  • This behaviour classification is the foundation of marginal costing and CVP analysis.

Takeaway: Behaviour (fixed/variable) drives every decision — from break-even to pricing to shutdown.

Q4. What is Activity Based Costing (ABC)? Discuss its merits and demerits, and explain the application process

Answer: ABC assigns overheads to cost objects through activities and cost drivers instead of one blanket rate, giving far more accurate product costs; its main drawback is implementation cost and complexity.

Merits:

  • Accurate product costing — overheads are traced to activities, not averaged out (a low-volume product no longer subsidises a high-volume one).
  • Better cost control — managers see which activities drive costs and attack non-value-added activities.
  • Supports pricing, make-or-buy, product-mix and outsourcing decisions.
  • Reveals hidden costs of complexity, batch size and customer demands.

Demerits:

  • Expensive and time-consuming to install and maintain (data, interviews, software).
  • Arbitrary selection of cost drivers — perfect accuracy is still impossible.
  • May not be worth it where overheads are small or products are homogeneous.

Application process (5 steps): (1) identify activities (set-up, inspection, machine running) → (2) assign overheads to activity cost pools → (3) select cost drivers (set-up hours, number of inspections, machine hours) → (4) compute activity rates (pool cost ÷ driver volume) → (5) charge costs to products using their driver consumption.

Key points

  • ABC = resources → activities (cost pools) → cost objects, using cost drivers at each stage.
  • Best for complex, multi-product firms with large overheads.
  • 5 application steps: activities → pools → drivers → rates → charge to products.
  • Limitation: costly, and driver choice is still judgmental.

Takeaway: ABC answers “what causes overheads?” — accuracy at the price of complexity.

Q5. What is Cost-Volume-Profit (CVP) Analysis? Explain the concept of Profit Graph and the assumptions behind it

Answer: CVP analysis studies how profit responds to changes in sales volume, selling price and costs; the profit graph plots profit (or loss) against sales and shows it crossing zero at break-even.

  • CVP: the relationship among Cost, Volume and Profit — contribution (Sales − Variable Cost) first covers fixed costs, and everything beyond break-even is profit.
  • Profit graph: on the Y-axis profit (loss below the zero line), on the X-axis sales. The profit line starts at the loss of fixed cost (zero sales) and rises by the P/V ratio — it cuts the zero-profit line at the break-even point. The graph visually shows margin of safety as the distance from BEP to actual sales.
  • Assumptions: selling price constant; costs can be perfectly split into fixed and variable; variable cost per unit constant; fixed cost constant; production = sales (no stock change); single product or constant sales mix.

Key points

  • Profit line rises at the P/V ratio; it starts at −Fixed Cost on the Y-axis.
  • Break-even = where the profit line crosses zero; every rupee of sales beyond it is profit.
  • Assumptions to list: constant price, linear costs, fixed/variable split, no stock changes, single product or constant mix.
  • Uses: target profit, pricing, make-or-buy, sales mix planning.

Takeaway: CVP turns “how much must we sell?” into one straight line.

Q6. What is a budget? Discuss budgetary control and the process of installing a budgetary control system

Answer: A budget is a quantified plan for a future period; budgetary control is the system that compares actual performance with the budget and takes corrective action, installed through a defined 7-step process.

Process of installing budgetary control:

  • Appoint a budget committee and a budget officer (usually the chief executive chairs it).
  • Fix the budget period (usually one year, subdivided into months/quarters).

  • Monitor actuals, compute variances and take corrective action.

Q2. What do you understand by cash and cash equivalents? Explain the basic structure of the Cash Flow Statement and the Direct Method of preparation

Answer: Cash and cash equivalents are cash on hand plus short-term, highly liquid investments easily convertible to cash (within 3 months, negligible value-change risk); the cash flow statement reports cash from operating, investing and financing activities, and the direct method lists actual cash receipts and payments line by line.

  • Cash and cash equivalents: cash, bank balances, and short-term deposits/investments like 3-month treasury bills. Equity investments are excluded unless effectively cash equivalents.
  • Basic structure (AS-3): (a) Net cash from Operating Activities → (b) Net cash from Investing Activities → (c) Net cash from Financing Activities → net increase/decrease in cash → + opening cash → = closing cash, with a reconciliation to profit and a note on non-cash transactions.
  • Direct method: report actual cash collections from customers and actual cash payments to suppliers, employees, for expenses and taxes; the difference is net operating cash. No accrual adjustments are needed because every item is a real cash flow — it is the cleanest, most transparent presentation.

Key points

  • Cash equivalents: maturing within ~3 months, no material value-change risk.
  • Structure: Operating → Investing → Financing → net change → closing balance.
  • Direct method uses actual cash receipts/payments; indirect starts from net profit.
  • Non-cash transactions (depreciation, credit purchases) are excluded from the direct method’s cash lines.

Takeaway: Direct method = follow the money; indirect method = adjust the profit.

Q3. Explain Absorption and Marginal costing, their differences, why marginal costing is an improvement, and its utility and limitations

Answer: Absorption costing charges all manufacturing overheads (fixed and variable) to products; marginal costing charges only variable costs and treats fixed costs as period costs — the difference changes how profit is measured when stock levels change.

BasisAbsorption costingMarginal costing

  • Marginal costing is for short-run decisions; statutory accounts use absorption costing.

Q4. Short notes: (i) Margin of Safety (ii) CVP Analysis (iii) Performance Budgeting (iv) Current Liabilities

Answer: Four compact notes covering the cushion between actual sales and break-even, the cost-volume-profit relationship, budgeting by activities/results, and obligations due within one year.

  • (i) Margin of Safety (MOS): the excess of actual (or budgeted) sales over break-even sales. MOS = Actual Sales − BEP Sales; MOS% = MOS ÷ Actual Sales × 100. A wide MOS means the business can absorb a sales fall before making losses; a thin MOS signals high operating risk. Ways to widen it: increase sales, raise P/V ratio (price/cost control), lower fixed costs.
  • (ii) CVP Analysis: the study of how cost, volume and profit interact. Key building blocks: Contribution, P/V ratio (C ÷ S), BEP (FC ÷ P/V), MOS. Assumes constant price, linear costs, perfect fixed-variable split, production = sales. Used for target profit, pricing and sales-mix decisions.

Used widely in government and public-sector units (e.g., a district’s health budget is tied to patients served and targets met).
  • (iv) Current Liabilities: obligations expected to be settled within the operating cycle or 12 months — sundry creditors, bills payable, bank overdraft, outstanding expenses, short-term loans, advance income, provision for tax, and the current portion of long-term debt. They are key to liquidity ratios (Current Ratio, Quick Ratio).

Key points

  • MOS = Sales − BEP; higher MOS = lower risk.
  • CVP rests on contribution and the P/V ratio.
  • Performance budgeting links money to measurable results.
  • Current liabilities are due within 12 months and drive liquidity analysis.

Takeaway: All four appear together in ratio and CVP questions — definitions with formulas earn the marks.

DMCV = Standard Cost − Actual Cost = (SQ × SP) − (AQ × AP)

Material Price Variance = (SP − AP) × AQ  |  Material Usage Variance = (SQ − AQ) × SP

Q8. What is Forensic Accounting? Explain the concept of Fraud Triangle and discuss the application of Forensic Accounting

  • Applications: fraud detection, litigation support, damages quantification, money-laundering investigation.

Takeaway: Prevent fraud by removing opportunity — the only corner management fully controls.

Q1. Explain: (a) Concept of Conservation (b) Cost concept (c) Money measurement concept (d) Periodicity concept

Answer: Four basic accounting concepts — conservatism prudence, historical cost valuation, monetary measurement and the division of business life into accounting periods.

  • (a) Conservation (Prudence): anticipate no profits but provide for all possible losses; expenses and liabilities are recognised sooner, incomes only when reasonably certain. Example: stock valued at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower. It protects creditors and avoids overstatement of profit.
  • (b) Cost concept: assets are recorded at the price paid at acquisition (historical cost), not at current market value; the cost is then spread over useful life through depreciation. It is objective and verifiable — but it ignores inflation and current values.
  • (c) Money measurement concept: only transactions expressible in money are recorded. Non-monetary factors — skills of employees, brand loyalty, management quality — are left out, which is a limitation (partly addressed by HRA and ESG reporting).
  • (d) Periodicity concept: the indefinite life of the business is split into equal periods (usually one year) for reporting; this makes accruals, matching and outstanding/prepaid adjustments necessary, so each period’s profit is comparable with the next.

Key points

  • Conservatism: recognise losses early, profits only when sure (lower-of-cost-or-market rule).
  • Cost concept: record at acquisition cost; depreciation spreads that cost.
  • Money measurement: only money-valued items enter accounts.
  • Periodicity: business life divided into years; requires matching and accruals.

Takeaway: These four concepts explain why accounts are conservative, cost-based, monetary and periodic.

Q2. What is a Journal? Explain the process of Journalizing and discuss the advantages of using a Journal

Answer: The Journal is the book of original entry in which every transaction is first recorded in date order with debit, credit and narration; journalizing applies the golden rules to analyse and record each transaction.

Process of Journalizing: (1) read the source document and identify the transaction → (2) decide the two accounts affected → (3) classify them as Real, Personal or Nominal → (4) apply golden rules — Real: debit what comes in, credit what goes out; Personal: debit the receiver, credit the giver; Nominal: debit expenses/losses, credit incomes/gains → (5) write the entry with date, debit and credit amounts, and a narration explaining the transaction.

Advantages: complete chronological record; each entry shows the full double-effect (debit + credit); narration adds clarity; errors are caught early; it provides the raw material for the ledger and trial balance.

Key points

  • Journal = first book of entry; ledger = classified book.
  • Every journal entry has a debit, a credit and a narration.
  • Golden rules depend on the account type (real/personal/nominal).
  • Trade discount is not journalised; only net amounts are recorded.

Takeaway: If the journal entry is right, the whole accounting trail is right.

Q6. Explain: (a) DuPont Analysis (b) Altman’s Z-score model

Answer: DuPont Analysis breaks Return on Equity into profitability, efficiency and leverage; Altman’s Z-score combines five ratios into one bankruptcy-prediction score.

(a) DuPont Analysis: ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier = (NP ÷ Sales) × (Sales ÷ Total Assets) × (Total Assets ÷ Equity)

  • Net profit margin = earnings per rupee of sales (profitability).
  • Asset turnover = how efficiently assets generate sales.
  • Equity multiplier = leverage — how much of assets is funded by equity.
  • Use: diagnose whether a high/low ROE comes from margins, efficiency or debt — e.g., 10% × 2 × 1.5 = 30% ROE.

(b) Altman’s Z-score: Z = 1.2X1 + 1.4X2 + 3.3X3 + 0.6X4 + 1.0X5

  • X1 = Working capital ÷ Total assets • X2 = Retained earnings ÷ Total assets • X3 = EBIT ÷ Total assets • X4 = Market value of equity ÷ Book value of debt • X5 = Sales ÷ Total assets.
  • Z > 2.99 safe; 1.81–2.99 grey; < 1.81 distress (high bankruptcy probability within 2 years).

Key points

  • DuPont: ROE = Margin × Turnover × Multiplier — isolates the driver of return.
  • Z-score: five ratios, weighted; below 1.81 = distress.
  • Both are used for credit screening and investment analysis.
  • Both appear repeatedly across 2023–2025 papers — high-priority revision topics.

Takeaway: DuPont tells you why returns are what they are; Z-score tells you if the company will survive.

Step 5 — Material Mix Variance: revised standard mix on 3,000 kg actual input = A 2,400 kg, B 600 kg. MMV = (RSQ − AQ) × SP = A: (2,400 − 2,200) × 2 = ₹400 (F)  +  B: (600 − 800) × 10 = ₹2,000 (A)  =  ₹1,600 (A)

Step 6 — Material Yield Variance: standard output on 3,000 kg = 2,700 kg; standard cost per kg of output = 3,000 × 3.6 ÷ 2,700 = ₹4. MYV = (Actual yield − Standard yield) × Std cost per kg = (2,850 − 2,700) × 4 = ₹600 (F)

Check: MCV = MPV + MMV + MYV = 100 + 1,600 − 600 = ₹1,400 (A) ✓ also MCV = MPV + MUV = 100 + 1,000 = ₹1,400 (A) ✓

Key points

  • MCV = SC − AC; MCV = MPV + MUV = MPV + MMV + MYV.
  • Mix variance uses the revised standard mix on actual input; yield variance compares actual output with standard output for actual input.
  • Using too much expensive material B is the root cause — an operations/purchase problem.
  • State assumptions clearly when data is missing (here: 4:1 mix, usage = “wage” typo).

Takeaway: Always verify with MCV = MPV + MUV — if it doesn’t reconcile, you’ve miscomputed a component.

Q1. Explain: (a) Business Entity concept (b) Continuity concept (c) Accrual concept (d) Materiality concept

Answer: Four concepts that separate the business from the owner, assume indefinite life, match income with expenses and limit reporting to significant items.

  • (a) Business Entity: the business is a separate legal and accounting entity distinct from its owner — owner’s drawings and capital are recorded separately; business records exclude personal transactions. Only this concept lets a sole proprietor prepare a meaningful profit figure.
  • (b) Continuity (Going Concern): the business is assumed to continue indefinitely; therefore assets are valued at cost less depreciation rather than at liquidation value, prepaid expenses are carried forward, and creditors are settled from future operations. If liquidation were expected, all values would change.
  • (c) Accrual: revenue when earned, expenses when incurred — regardless of cash movement. This requires adjustments: outstanding expenses, prepaid expenses, accrued income, income received in advance. It makes each period’s profit meaningful (matching concept).
  • (d) Materiality: only items significant enough to influence decisions need separate treatment — a ₹500 stapler is expensed immediately rather than depreciated over 10 years. What is material depends on the size and nature of the business.

Key points

  • Entity: business and owner are separate.
  • Continuity: indefinite life → cost-minus-depreciation valuation.
  • Accrual: earned/incurred, not paid/received → drives year-end adjustments.
  • Materiality: ignore trivia, disclose what matters.

Takeaway: These four concepts quietly justify almost every rule in accounting.

Q2. What is a Balance Sheet? Describe its contents and discuss how they are classified

Answer: A Balance Sheet is a statement of the financial position of a business at a point in time, showing Assets = Liabilities + Equity; its contents are classified by period of conversion (current vs non-current) under the Companies Act 2013 (Schedule III).

Contents and classification:

  • Equity and Liabilities: (1) Shareholders’ funds — share capital, reserves and surplus; (2) Share application money pending; (3) Non-current liabilities — long-term borrowings, deferred tax, long-term provisions; (4) Current liabilities — trade payables, short-term borrowings, other current liabilities, short-term provisions.
  • Assets: (1) Non-current assets — fixed assets (tangible, intangible), capital work-in-progress, non-current investments, long-term loans and advances; (2) Current assets — inventories, trade receivables, cash and cash equivalents, short-term loans and advances, other current assets.
  • Classification logic: current = realisable/settled within 12 months of the balance sheet date; the rest is non-current. This split is the raw material for liquidity ratios (current ratio, quick ratio) and for working capital analysis.

Balance Sheet vs statement: it is a position statement (as on a date), not a flow statement — hence the P&L links two successive balance sheets.

Key points

  • BS equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity; always balances.
  • Schedule III classification: current vs non-current for both sides.
  • Fixed assets at cost less depreciation; current assets at cost or NRV, whichever is lower.
  • It is a point-in-time snapshot — compare with the previous year’s for trends.

Takeaway: Balance sheet = “what we own and what we owe” at one date, arranged by how soon they turn into cash.

Q3. What is a Cash Flow Statement? Discuss its features, benefits and objectives, and the items included in cash flow from investing activities

Answer: A cash flow statement summarises the cash inflows and outflows of a business over a period, classified into operating, investing and financing activities (AS-3); its purpose is to explain why profit and cash differ.

Features: based on cash (not accruals); historical and classified into three activities; reconciles opening and closing cash and cash equivalents; includes only cash items and excludes non-cash transactions (except by disclosure).

Benefits: reveals liquidity — a profitable company can still fail if cash is locked in stock and debtors; helps in cash planning, working capital management and comparing cash generation across companies with different accounting policies; aids investors and lenders in judging solvency and dividend-paying capacity.

Objectives: show cash generation from operations, the investing and financing activities undertaken, and the net change in cash — thereby evaluating the entity’s ability to generate future cash flows and its need for external finance.

Investing activities (items): purchase and sale of fixed assets (land, building, plant, machinery); purchase and sale of investments (shares, bonds, subsidiaries); loans given and repaid; and interest/dividends received (in India under AS-3 these are investing inflows).

Key points

  • Cash flow statement is a flow statement for a period; balance sheet is a position statement for a date.
  • Three sections: operating, investing, financing.
  • Investing = long-term assets and investments, including interest/dividend received.
  • Depreciation is non-cash — added back under the indirect method.

Takeaway: Profit is an opinion about the past; cash flow is the fact that pays tomorrow’s bills.

Q4. What is Activity Based Costing? Discuss its features and objectives, and describe the process of implementation

Answer: ABC is a costing system that traces overheads to products through activities and cost drivers, replacing arbitrary plant-wide allocation; its objectives are accurate product costs and better cost control.

Features: overheads are collected in activity cost pools (set-up, inspection, machine running, material handling); costs are traced with cost drivers (number of set-ups, inspection hours, machine hours); both volume-based and non-volume-based drivers are used; costs are assigned in two stages — resources to activities, activities to cost objects.

Objectives: accurate product/service costing; identification of non-value-added activities for elimination; support for pricing, product mix, make-or-buy and cost-reduction decisions; fair allocation of overheads between products, customers and channels.

Implementation process: (1) identify activities and build an activity dictionary → (2) assign overhead costs to activity cost pools → (3) select cost drivers for each pool → (4) compute activity rates (pool cost ÷ driver volume) → (5) charge products/customers based on their consumption of drivers → (6) refine continuously with activity-based management.

Key points

  • ABC uses activities as the bridge between resources and cost objects.
  • Cost drivers: volume-based (machine hours) and non-volume-based (set-ups, orders).
  • Most valuable in multi-product firms with large and diverse overheads.
  • Limits: costly, judgmental driver choice, data-intensive.

Takeaway: ABC replaces guessing with tracing — activities cause costs, products consume activities.

Q5. Explain: (a) Performance Budgeting (b) Zero Base Budgeting

Q6. Briefly discuss the concept of ratio analysis. Explain the significance and purpose of Efficiency/Activity ratios and describe the various activity ratios

Answer: Ratio analysis expresses relationships between financial statement figures to judge profitability, liquidity, solvency and efficiency; activity (turnover) ratios measure how efficiently assets are used to generate sales.

Concept: ratios standardise numbers of different sizes so that performance can be compared across years and companies; they are classified as profitability, liquidity, solvency (leverage) and efficiency (activity) ratios.

Significance and purpose of activity ratios: they reveal the speed with which stock, debtors, creditors and assets move through the business — slow turnover ties up capital and signals weak efficiency; they support working capital decisions and detect over-stocking or lax credit.

Various activity ratios:

  • Inventory (stock) turnover = Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Average Inventory — how fast stock is sold.
  • Debtors (receivables) turnover = Credit Sales ÷ Average Debtors; average collection period = 365 ÷ turnover.
  • Creditors (payables) turnover = Credit Purchases ÷ Average Creditors — how fast suppliers are paid.
  • Fixed asset turnover = Sales ÷ Net Fixed Assets.
  • Total asset turnover = Sales ÷ Total Assets — overall efficiency; also the “efficiency” leg of the DuPont model.
  • Working capital turnover = Sales ÷ Net Working Capital.

Key points

  • Activity ratios measure how efficiently assets generate sales.
  • Inventory: COGS ÷ Avg stock; Debtors: Credit sales ÷ Avg debtors; Creditors: Credit purchases ÷ Avg creditors.
  • Asset turnover = Sales ÷ Assets — also the DuPont efficiency leg.
  • Interpretation is comparative — against prior years, budgets and industry.

Takeaway: Slow turnover ties up cash — activity ratios catch it early.

Q7. What is Forensic Accounting? Describe the fraud triangle and explain the role of pressure and opportunity in perpetuating fraud

Answer: Forensic accounting applies accounting and investigative skills to detect and prove fraud; the fraud triangle shows that pressure and opportunity (with rationalisation) combine to make fraud happen — and organisations can do something about both.

Fraud triangle: three conditions — pressure (motivation), opportunity (weak controls) and rationalisation (self-justification).

Role of pressure: pressure is the why — personal debts, gambling, lifestyle inflation, unrealistic bonus and sales targets, fear of job loss, greed. Management pressure to report earnings targets famously drives financial statement fraud (window dressing, revenue fudging).

Role of opportunity: opportunity is the how — weak internal controls, absence of segregation of duties, management override of controls, lack of independent checks, complex or undocumented transactions. Even a pressured employee cannot commit fraud without opportunity — which is why internal control is the main defence.

Prevention: remove opportunity through controls, audits, whistle-blower channels and monitoring; reduce pressure with realistic targets and grievance mechanisms.

Key points

  • Fraud = pressure + opportunity + rationalisation.
  • Pressure supplies the motive; opportunity supplies the means; rationalisation supplies the excuse.
  • Opportunity is the corner management controls — via internal controls and segregation of duties.
  • Forensic accounting detects, quantifies and proves fraud for courts.

Takeaway: You cannot control employees’ pressures, but you can control their opportunities.

Q3. What are Absorption and Marginal Costing? Differences between them, and methods used for segregation of semi-variable costs

Answer: Absorption costing charges all overheads (fixed + variable) to products; marginal costing charges only variable costs. Semi-variable costs — part fixed, part variable — are separated using the high-low method, scatter diagram or least-squares regression.

Differences: treatment of fixed overheads (absorbed vs period cost); stock valuation (full cost vs variable cost only); profit response (production + sales vs sales only); suitability (statutory reporting vs decision making).

Methods of segregation of semi-variable costs:

  • High-Low method: take the highest and lowest activity levels, compute the variable rate = (Cost at high − Cost at low) ÷ (Units at high − Units at low); fixed cost = Total cost at any level − (variable rate × units at that level). Quick but uses only two points.
  • Scatter diagram: plot cost against activity, draw a line of best fit by eye — the intercept is fixed cost, the slope is variable cost per unit.
  • Least-squares (regression): statistical fit of the line to all points — the most accurate method.
  • Analytical (engineering) method: study each cost item and classify its fixed and variable parts by inspection.

Key points

  • Absorption: fixed OH absorbed into products; marginal: fixed OH written off.
  • Semi-variable segregation methods: high-low, scatter, regression, analytical.
  • High-low formula: Variable rate = ΔCost ÷ ΔUnits between highest and lowest points.
  • Segregation is the precondition for marginal costing and CVP.

Takeaway: Split the semi-variable cost first — every CVP number depends on that split.

  • (c) Cost of goods sold: COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases + Direct Expenses − Closing Stock Gross profit = Sales − COGS; it is the bridge between purchases and profit.
  • (d) Depreciation: systematic allocation of an asset’s cost over its useful life (straight-line, written-down value); it is a non-cash expense — added back in the cash flow statement — and also provides funds logic for replacement.
  • (e) Current assets: assets expected to convert into cash within 12 months — cash, bank, debtors, stock, short-term investments; the basis of current and quick ratios.

Key points

  • Accrual = earned/incurred not paid/received.
  • Materiality = significance decides disclosure.
  • COGS = Opening + Purchases + Direct expenses − Closing.
  • Depreciation = non-cash, systematic; current assets = cash within 12 months.

Takeaway: These five notes recur in nearly every paper — worth one-line-per-concept revision.

Q3. What do you understand by cost? Explain its different elements, how cost is classified and how total cost is calculated

Answer: Cost is the sacrifice of resources to produce goods or services; it has three elements — material, labour and expenses — classified by nature, behaviour, function and controllability, and built up step by step into total cost.

Elements: Material (direct raw materials and indirect consumables), Labour (direct wages and indirect salaries), Expenses/overheads (rent, power, insurance, depreciation).

Classification: by nature (direct/indirect), by behaviour (fixed/variable/semi-variable), by function (production, administration, selling & distribution), by controllability (controllable/uncontrollable).

Total cost calculation (cost sheet):

  • Prime cost = Direct material + Direct labour + Direct expenses
  • Works/factory cost = Prime cost + Factory overheads
  • Cost of production = Works cost + Office & administration overheads
  • Total cost = Cost of production + Selling & distribution overheads
  • Profit = Sales − Total cost

Key points

  • Elements: material + labour + expenses.
  • Classification bases: nature, behaviour, function, controllability.
  • Ladder: Prime → Works → Production → Total cost.
  • Profit = Sales − Total cost — pricing starts from this ladder.

Takeaway: Learn the four classification bases and the four-rung ladder — cost questions are formulaic.

Q4. What is a Budget? Discuss the significance of budgetary control, the process of installing a budgetary control system, and the control ratios used

Answer: A budget is a quantified plan for a future period; budgetary control compares actuals with budgets and corrects deviations; it is installed in a 7-step process and monitored with capacity, activity, efficiency and calendar ratios.

Significance of budgetary control: forces advance planning; coordinates departments; fixes responsibility; motivates through targets; gives early warning via variances; provides the yardstick for appraisal.

Installation process: (1) budget committee and budget officer → (2) budget period → (3) budget centres → (4) budget manual → (5) key (limiting) factor → (6) functional budgets + master budget → (7) monitoring, variance analysis, corrective action.

Control ratios:

Capacity Ratio = (Actual Hours ÷ Budgeted Hours) × 100

Activity Ratio = (Standard Hours for Actual Output ÷ Budgeted Hours) × 100

Efficiency Ratio = (Standard Hours for Actual Output ÷ Actual Hours) × 100

Calendar Ratio = (Actual Working Days ÷ Budgeted Working Days) × 100  •  Activity = Capacity × Efficiency

Key points

  • Budget without control is a wish — control means compare + correct.
  • 7 installation steps ending with the master budget.
  • Capacity = worked vs planned hours; Efficiency = standard hours for actual output vs actual hours; Activity = standard hours vs budgeted hours.
  • Activity Ratio = Capacity Ratio × Efficiency Ratio — the classic link.

Takeaway: Control ratios answer “did we work enough, work efficiently, and achieve the plan?”

Q5. Explain: (a) Altman’s Z score (b) Du Pont Analysis

Answer: Z-score predicts bankruptcy with five weighted ratios; Du Pont decomposes ROE into profitability, efficiency and leverage — together they answer “will it survive?” and “why does it earn what it earns?”.

(a) Altman’s Z score: Z = 1.2X1 + 1.4X2 + 3.3X3 + 0.6X4 + 1.0X5 where X1 = WC ÷ Total assets, X2 = Retained earnings ÷ Total assets, X3 = EBIT ÷ Total assets, X4 = Market value of equity ÷ Book value of debt, X5 = Sales ÷ Total assets. Interpretation: Z > 2.99 safe; 1.81–2.99 grey; < 1.81 distress.

(b) Du Pont Analysis: ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier = (NP ÷ Sales) × (Sales ÷ Assets) × (Assets ÷ Equity) It isolates whether returns come from margins, asset efficiency or leverage; example: 10% × 2 × 1.5 = 30%.

See the diagram in the main solved answers above — the Du Pont tree and Z-score zone chart apply to this question as well.

Key points

  • Z-score: 5 ratios, 2.99/1.81 thresholds.
  • Du Pont: margin × turnover × multiplier.
  • Both are used by banks for credit screening.
  • Du Pont separates quality of returns; Z-score measures survival risk.

Takeaway: Two formulas, two questions — “how are returns earned?” and “will the firm survive?”.

Q6. What is an Annual Report? Describe the financial contents, the Notes to the accounts, and their significance for investors

Answer: An annual report is the company’s statutory yearly disclosure to stakeholders; it contains audited financial statements, reports and notes — the notes being the detail that investors need to trust and compare the numbers.

Financial contents: Balance Sheet; Statement of Profit & Loss; Cash Flow Statement; Statement of Changes in Equity; Auditor’s Report (unqualified/qualified/adverse/disclaimer); Notes to Accounts — all in Schedule III formats under the Companies Act 2013.

Notes to the accounts: significant accounting policies (depreciation method, stock valuation), contingent liabilities, related-party transactions, segment information, fixed-asset schedules, loans and guarantees, employee benefits and commitments.

Significance for investors: the notes expose the assumptions behind the numbers and the risks hidden in them; they make statements comparable across years and companies; analysts recompute earnings and net worth only after reading them. Without notes, two companies with identical balance sheets can have very different real economics.

Key points

  • Financial contents: 4 statements + audit report + notes.
  • Notes: policies, contingencies, related parties, segments, asset schedules.
  • Investor use: trust, comparability, hidden-risk discovery.
  • Audit opinion types: DAUA — Disclaimer, Adverse, Unqualified, qualified.

Takeaway: The notes are where numbers reveal their true assumptions — read them before investing.

Q7. What is Human Resource Accounting (HRA)? Describe how HRA can be used as a management decision tool

Answer: HRA measures and reports the cost and value of people as assets; used as a decision tool it quantifies train-vs-hire, attrition cost, HR budgets and manager performance.

What it is: identification, measurement and communication of human resource value (methods: historical cost, replacement cost, opportunity cost, Lev & Schwartz present value).

As a management decision tool:

  • Train vs recruit: compare development cost of existing staff with hiring cost of new staff.
  • Attrition control: quantify value lost per 1% attrition (Infosys: ~₹500 crore) to justify retention budgets.
  • Manpower planning and budgeting: plan recruitment, increments and perks against measured human value.
  • Performance evaluation: hold managers accountable for the human capital value under them.
  • Investor communication: in knowledge companies most market value is intangible — HRA makes it visible.

Key points

  • HRA treats people as assets, not expenses.
  • Methods: HROP — Historical, Replacement, Opportunity, Present value.
  • Decisions: train/hire, attrition, budgeting, appraisal, investor reporting.
  • Limitations: subjectivity, no standard, cost of implementation.

Takeaway: In a knowledge economy, “people are our biggest asset” stops being a slogan — HRA makes it a number.

R-Q2 (reconstructed). Cash flow statement: objectives, classification of activities and the indirect method

Answer: The cash flow statement reports cash from operating, investing and financing activities and reconciles opening with closing cash; the indirect method converts net profit into operating cash by adding back non-cash items and adjusting working capital.

  • Objectives: assess cash generation ability, explain profit–cash gap, evaluate investing/financing decisions, aid liquidity planning.
  • Classification: operating (day-to-day revenue activities), investing (fixed assets and investments), financing (equity and borrowings).
  • Indirect method: Net profit → + depreciation & non-cash items → ± non-operating items (deduct profit on sale of asset, add loss) → ± working capital changes (stock/debtors up = cash down; creditors up = cash up) → net cash from operations.
  • Benefits: reveals liquidity, supports budgeting, comparable across firms with different depreciation/valuation policies.

Key points

  • Three sections; net change + opening balance = closing balance.
  • Depreciation is added back — it is a non-cash charge.
  • Increase in current assets reduces cash; increase in current liabilities increases cash.
  • Interest/dividend received: investing; dividend paid: financing.

Takeaway: Profit can be paper; cash flow is the scoreboard that decides survival.

R-Q3 (reconstructed). CVP numerical: two periods given, find P/V ratio, fixed cost, break-even and target sales

Answer: P/V ratio 40%, fixed cost ₹20,000, break-even ₹50,000, and sales of ₹87,500 are needed to earn ₹15,000 profit.

Given data:

PeriodSalesProfit
Period 1₹80,000₹12,000
Period 2₹1,00,000₹20,000

Step 1 — P/V ratio: P/V = ΔProfit ÷ ΔSales = (20,000 − 12,000) ÷ (1,00,000 − 80,000) = 8,000 ÷ 20,000 = 40%

Step 2 — Fixed cost: FC = (P/V × Sales) − Profit = (0.40 × 80,000) − 12,000 = 32,000 − 12,000 = ₹20,000 (check Period 2: 40,000 − 20,000 = 20,000 ✓)

Step 3 — Break-even: BEP = FC ÷ P/V = 20,000 ÷ 0.40 = ₹50,000

Step 4 — Sales for ₹15,000 profit: Sales = (FC + Target) ÷ P/V = (20,000 + 15,000) ÷ 0.40 = ₹87,500

Key points

  • Change-in-profit ÷ change-in-sales gives P/V without knowing costs.
  • Verify FC with both periods before using it.
  • BEP = FC ÷ P/V; target sales = (FC + target) ÷ P/V.
  • MOS = actual sales − BEP; here 30,000 (37.5%).

Takeaway: Two (sales, profit) points unlock the entire CVP toolkit — the most repeated numerical pattern in MMPC-04.

R-Q4 (reconstructed). What is a budget? Explain budgetary control and the steps to install a budgetary control system
