Q1. (a) Explain the components of compensation with reference to the Indian context. (10 marks)

Answer: Compensation is all financial returns and benefits of employment - direct cash pay (basic, DA, incentives, bonus) plus indirect fringe benefits (PF, gratuity, ESI, medical cover) - with a typical Indian CTC splitting roughly 40-50% into basic wage.

Compensation means all forms of financial returns and tangible services and benefits that an employee receives as part of the employment relationship. It has two broad components. Direct (financial) compensation is paid in cash: basic pay, dearness allowance, overtime, incentives, bonus, and commission. Indirect compensation covers fringe benefits such as provident fund, pension, gratuity, ESI, medical insurance, sick leave, and housing. In the Indian context, a typical CTC of Rs 10 lakh breaks up as: basic wage (40-50% of CTC), HRA (40-50% of basic in metro cities), special allowance, employer PF, gratuity, medical allowance of Rs 15,000 per year (tax-free under Section 17(2)), and leave travel allowance. Dearness allowance compensates for inflation and is linked to the All-India Consumer Price Index, revised twice a year. Fringe benefits in India have grown from 15-20% of total compensation in the 1990s to 25-35% today, driven by tax advantages and competition for talent. Compensation serves four roles: attracting talent, retaining employees, motivating performance, and building the desired culture.

(b) A cafeteria plan lets each employee choose their own package of benefits from a list of options, like choosing dishes from a cafeteria menu. A young employee may choose more medical cover, while a senior employee may choose a larger pension contribution. It matches benefits to individual needs and raises satisfaction.

Key points to remember:

  • Compensation = direct (cash) + indirect (benefits) components.
  • Basic wage is typically 40-50% of CTC in India; DA tracks inflation via AICPI.
  • Fringe benefits have grown to 25-35% of total compensation.
  • Cafeteria plan = employee chooses benefits from a menu of options.

Takeaway: Compensation = direct (cash) + indirect (benefits); the cafeteria plan lets each employee build a personal benefit menu.

Q2. (a) Describe the four methods of job evaluation with their merits and limitations. (10 marks)

Answer: Job evaluation determines the relative worth of jobs (not people) through four methods - ranking, classification, point rating and factor comparison - with point rating the most popular and defensible.

Job evaluation is a systematic method of determining the relative worth of jobs in an organisation to establish rational pay differentials. It evaluates the job, not the person. The four methods are remembered as RCPF. Ranking is the simplest: jobs are sorted from most to least complex. It is quick and cheap but highly subjective. Classification matches jobs to predefined grades or classes; it is easy to understand but rigid, because each job must fit a fixed grade. Point rating is the most popular quantitative method: points are given to factors such as skill, effort, responsibility, and working conditions, and the total decides the pay rate. In a paper mill, the turner job scored 316 points against 150 for the sweeper, justifying a higher rate. Factor comparison ranks jobs factor by factor and assigns monetary values to each factor; it is precise but time-consuming and complex. Point rating is used by medium and large organisations, including Indian PSUs like BHEL and SAIL, because it gives systematic and defensible pay differentials. Job evaluation flows from job analysis to job description, job specification, job grading, and job assessment. Merit rating is different: it evaluates the person's performance, not the job's worth.

(b) Internal equity means fair pay relationships inside the organisation - "right pay for the right job". Jobs of equal value must be paid equally, and higher-value jobs must earn more. It is achieved through job evaluation and prevents dissatisfaction, jealousy, and attrition among employees doing comparable work.

Key points to remember:

  • Four methods: Ranking, Classification, Point rating, Factor comparison (RCPF).
  • Point rating is the most popular and defensible method.
  • Job evaluation evaluates the job; merit rating evaluates the person.
  • Internal equity = fair pay relationships within the organisation.

Takeaway: RCPF = Ranking, Classification, Point rating, Factor comparison; internal equity means equal pay for jobs of equal value.

Q3. (a) Explain the bases of wage determination and the legal framework for wages in India. (10 marks)

Answer: Wages are set by economic, sociological, political, psychological and ethical factors, moving from minimum wage to fair wage to living wage, under four classic laws now merged in the Code on Wages 2019.

Wages are determined by five sets of factors. Economic factors include market demand and supply of labour, competing employers, cost of living, and productivity. Sociological factors include social status and equity between men and women. Political factors include trade union bargaining and government intervention. Psychological factors include motivation and job satisfaction, because fair wages keep employees engaged. Ethical factors demand fair wages and living wages, not just minimum wages. The wage hierarchy runs from minimum wage (bare subsistence) to fair wage (between minimum and living) to living wage (reasonable comfort). The Indian legal framework has four classic laws: the Minimum Wages Act 1948 fixes minimum wage rates for scheduled employments; the Payment of Wages Act 1936 ensures timely payment and regulates deductions; the Equal Remuneration Act 1976 guarantees equal pay for equal work regardless of gender; and the Payment of Bonus Act 1965 provides a statutory bonus to eligible employees. Wage components under law are basic wage plus dearness allowance, house rent allowance, and other allowances. Statutory deductions like EPF and ESI are made from gross pay. The Code on Wages 2019 now consolidates the four wage laws into a single code to simplify compliance.

(b) The Code on Wages 2019 merges the Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Wages Act, Equal Remuneration Act, and Payment of Bonus Act into one law. It defines a uniform national floor wage, fixes a universal minimum wage, and extends equal remuneration for equal work to all employees, not just women workers.

Key points to remember:

  • Wage factors: economic, sociological, political, psychological, ethical.
  • Hierarchy: minimum wage < fair wage < living wage.
  • Key laws: Minimum Wages 1948, Payment of Wages 1936, Equal Remuneration 1976, Bonus 1965.
  • Code on Wages 2019 consolidates all four wage laws into one code.

Takeaway: Remember the five wage factors and the hierarchy minimum < fair < living wage.

Q4. (a) Explain incentive schemes at individual and group levels with suitable examples. (10 marks)

Answer: Incentives are performance-linked payments above basic pay - individual plans (piece-rate, commission, bonus) for measurable output and group plans (gainsharing, profit-sharing, ESOPs) for team results.

Incentives are additional payments linked to performance, over and above basic pay. They operate at three levels. Individual incentives reward personal output: piece-rate (payment per unit produced), commission (percentage of sales), bonus, and merit pay. These suit jobs where output is measurable, but they can create internal competition. Group incentives reward team or organisational results: gainsharing shares productivity gains with the employees who helped achieve them, profit-sharing distributes a share of company profits, and ESOPs give employees ownership. Group plans suit collaborative work, but carry the risk of free-riding, where some members benefit without contributing. Executive incentives include stock options, performance shares, and golden parachutes for top leadership. In India, sales teams at companies like HUL earn monthly and annual bonuses on target achievement, while IT companies use ESOPs to retain talent. The choice between individual and group incentives depends on the nature of work: measurable individual output favours piece-rate and commission, while team-based or project work favours gainsharing and profit-sharing. A well-designed incentive plan must be transparent, achievable, and aligned with both company goals and employee effort.

(b) An ESOP gives employees the right to buy company shares at a fixed exercise price after a vesting period (minimum one year, typically three to four years). Because shares become valuable over time, employees stay locked into the company to let their options vest - hence the name "golden handcuffs".

Key points to remember:

  • Individual incentives: piece-rate, commission, bonus, merit pay.
  • Group incentives: gainsharing, profit-sharing, ESOPs.
  • Risk of group plans is free-riding; individual plans risk internal competition.
  • ESOPs lock talent into the company - the "golden handcuffs".

Takeaway: Individual plans risk internal competition; group plans risk free-riding; ESOPs are "golden handcuffs" that lock talent in.

Q5. (a) What is a pay survey? How does an organisation use it to achieve external equity? (10 marks)

Answer: A pay survey systematically collects market compensation data to achieve external equity, analysed through mean and median and used for market pricing at the 25th, 50th or 75th percentile.

External equity means paying employees competitively compared with the outside market, so that talent is not lost to competitors. A pay survey is the systematic collection of compensation data from other organisations in the market. Surveys may be industry-specific, regional, or job-specific. The data collected includes basic pay, allowances, bonuses, and benefits for comparable jobs. Analysis methods include the mean (average pay) and the median (middle value), and the median is preferred because it is less affected by extreme outliers. The organisation then compares its own pay levels with the surveyed rates job by job, calculates the pay gap, and decides its market position. Market pricing sets pay directly from survey data. If a Java developer earns Rs 8 lakh in the market and a firm pays only Rs 6 lakh, external inequity will cause attrition, so the firm must raise pay or offer compensating benefits. Surveys also help price new jobs, validate pay structures, and support negotiation with unions and boards. Surveys should be repeated periodically because market rates move with demand, inflation, and industry growth.

(b) Market pricing means positioning pay at a chosen percentile of the market: the 25th percentile is a lag strategy (below market, used when cost control matters), the 50th percentile matches the market average, and the 75th percentile leads the market (above average, used to attract top talent). The choice depends on the company's strategy.

Key points to remember:

  • Pay survey = systematic collection of market compensation data.
  • Median is more reliable than mean for survey analysis.
  • Market pricing: 25th = lag, 50th = match, 75th = lead the market.
  • External equity prevents attrition to competitors paying more.

Takeaway: The median is more reliable than the mean; the 25th percentile lags, the 50th matches and the 75th leads the market.

Q6. (a) Explain the steps of designing a pay structure and the concept of broad banding. (10 marks)

Answer: A pay structure is built from grades, ranges and overlaps in five steps - evaluate jobs, group grades, fix ranges, price with market data, implement - and broad banding replaces many narrow grades with fewer wide bands.

A pay structure is the framework of pay grades, ranges, and overlaps that guides how much each job earns. It has three building blocks: pay grades (groups of jobs with similar worth), pay ranges (minimum, midpoint, and maximum for each grade), and range overlap between adjacent grades. There are five steps to design it. First, conduct job evaluation to measure the relative worth of jobs. Second, group jobs into grades based on similar scores. Third, determine a pay range for each grade. Fourth, price the structure using market data from pay surveys. Fifth, implement and maintain it by reviewing it periodically. Traditional structures have many narrow grades with strict hierarchy. Broad banding replaces many grades with fewer, wider bands. Broad bands give managers more flexibility to pay based on skill and performance, encourage lateral movement and skill development, and simplify administration. The downside is the risk of inconsistent pay for similar work. Indian organisations moving to flat, skill-based structures increasingly use broad banding, while government departments retain traditional grading with pay commissions.

(b) Allowances are recurring cash payments such as DA, HRA, conveyance, and medical allowance. Perquisites are non-cash privileges such as a company car, club membership, or stock options. Benefits are statutory and welfare provisions such as PF, gratuity, pension, and insurance. All three together make up the total rewards package.

Key points to remember:

  • Pay structure = grades, ranges (min-midpoint-max), and overlaps.
  • Five steps: evaluate jobs, group grades, fix ranges, price with market data, implement.
  • Broad banding = fewer, wider bands with more flexibility.
  • Allowances are cash, perquisites are privileges, benefits are welfare provisions.

Takeaway: Broad banding = fewer, wider bands for flexibility; allowances are cash, perquisites are privileges, benefits are welfare provisions.
