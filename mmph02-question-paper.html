Solved Answers - MMPH-02 December 2023 Question Paper

  • Q1. Explain the meaning, importance and evolution of HRD.

    Answer: HRD is the systematic process of improving people's knowledge, skills and attitudes, evolving from welfare to personnel management to training to strategic partnership.

    Human Resource Development (HRD) is the systematic process of improving employees' knowledge, skills, abilities, and attitudes so that both the individual and the organisation grow together. Prof. T.V. Rao, the Father of HRD in India, defined it as "a set of systematic and planned activities designed by an organisation to provide its members with the necessary learning opportunities to improve their performance and enable the organisation to achieve its goals." HRD is important because it builds capability (competent people), commitment (engaged people), culture (a learning and trust-based climate), and alignment of people with business goals - the four Cs often called the HRD purpose. The evolution of HRD happened in stages. In the 1900s, organisations had only welfare officers who looked after basic amenities. From the 1930s-1950s, personnel management emerged, handling hiring, payroll, and discipline. In the 1960s-1970s, training departments were set up, and in 1975 Larsen & Toubro, guided by T.V. Rao, became the first Indian company to establish a separate HRD department with systems for appraisal, feedback, and counselling. From the 1990s, HRD turned strategic - linked directly to business plans, competency building, and talent management. After COVID-19, HRD now handles remote work, hybrid teams, digital learning, and employee well-being. Example: L&T's HRD system became a model copied by companies like BHEL and NTPC. In short, HRD moved from welfare to personnel management to training to strategic business partnership.

    Key points to remember: HRD = systematic development of people; Rao's definition; importance = capability, commitment, culture, alignment; evolution = welfare → personnel → training → strategic HRD; L&T 1975 as India's first HRD department.

    Takeaway: HRD builds capability, commitment, culture and alignment - the four Cs - and has grown from welfare work into a strategic business partnership.

  • Q2. Describe various components of HRD systems citing relevant examples.

    Answer: An HRD system is a set of interlinked sub-systems - appraisal, training, OD, feedback, counselling, career planning, rewards and welfare - that reinforce each other.

    An HRD system is not one activity but a set of interlinked sub-systems that together develop people throughout their life in the organisation. The main components are: performance appraisal, which evaluates current job performance against targets - for example, Infosys's quarterly reviews; potential appraisal, which assesses the employee's future capability and promotability for leadership roles; training and development, which builds today's skills and tomorrow's managerial ability; organisation development (OD), which improves the health of the whole organisation through team building and change management; feedback and coaching, which give employees information to improve - such as 360-degree feedback; counselling, which helps employees resolve personal and work problems; career planning, which maps an employee's growth path, as in Wipro's career ladders; rewards and recognition, which motivate good performance through incentives and awards; and employee welfare, which covers health, safety, and work-life balance. Example: Larsen & Toubro runs all these components together - its development centres conduct performance and potential reviews, its training academies build skills, and its employee assistance programmes provide counselling. The components are interlinked: appraisal feeds training needs, training feeds career plans, and rewards reinforce all of them. A useful memory aid is PPTM-OD-FCRW: Performance appraisal, Potential appraisal, Training, Management development, Organisation development, Feedback, Counselling, Rewards, Welfare. No single component works in isolation - the system works because the components reinforce each other.

    Key points to remember: Components = performance and potential appraisal, training, management development, OD, feedback, counselling, career planning, rewards, welfare; they are interlinked and reinforce each other; PPTM-OD-FCRW memory aid; L&T as a complete example.

    Takeaway: HRD components work only as a reinforcing system, never in isolation - appraisal feeds training, training feeds careers, rewards reinforce all.

  • Q3. Describe the stages of HRD frameworks. Briefly discuss the interlinkages between HRD and outcome of the organisation.

    Answer: An HRD framework runs needs assessment, design, implementation and evaluation, and links improved competencies to individual, unit and organisational outcomes.

    An HRD framework is the step-by-step design through which development activities are planned, delivered, and evaluated. The stages are: needs assessment - identifying the gaps between what people can do and what the job or strategy requires, using job analysis, appraisals, and surveys; designing the intervention - setting objectives, content, and methods (classroom, e-learning, on-the-job) for training, OD, or career programmes; implementation - actually delivering the programme and creating the supporting culture, so that learning is applied; and evaluation - measuring results, classically through Kirkpatrick's four levels of reaction, learning, behaviour, and results. Example: when a bank noticed rising customer complaints, its needs assessment showed a service-skills gap, it designed a customer-service programme, delivered it in two months, and evaluated impact through repeat-complaint data six months later. The interlinkage with organisational outcomes works like a chain: HRD improves employee competencies, which raise individual performance, which collectively improves unit performance, which finally shows up in organisational outcomes such as higher productivity, better quality, lower attrition, higher profits, and stronger customer satisfaction. Kirkpatrick's level four measures exactly this chain - for instance, the Tatas measure the return on investment of leadership programmes by tracking performance improvements against costs. If any stage is skipped - especially evaluation - HRD becomes an expense without proof of impact.

    Key points to remember: Stages = needs assessment, design, implementation, evaluation (ADDIE-style); evaluation uses Kirkpatrick's four levels; linkage = competencies → individual performance → unit performance → organisational outcomes; skip evaluation and HRD has no visible impact.

    Takeaway: Competencies raise individual performance, then unit performance, then organisational outcomes - and skipping evaluation makes HRD an expense without proof.

  • Q4. What are the functions of HRD for employees? Describe how the role has changed over the years.

    Answer: HRD serves employees through training, career development, appraisal, counselling, mentoring, welfare and engagement - roles that shifted from policing and record-keeping to strategic partnering.

    HRD performs several functions directly for employees: training and development, which builds the skills needed for the current job and future roles; career development, which helps employees plan and grow along a defined path; performance management, which sets goals, reviews results, and gives feedback; counselling and coaching, which support employees through personal and work problems; mentoring, which connects juniors with experienced seniors for guidance; welfare and work-life support, which protects health, safety, and well-being; and engagement activities, which build motivation and belonging. Over the years the role has changed dramatically. In the early personnel era, HRD was a record-keeping, policing function - attendance, leave, and discipline. In the 1970s-1980s it became a development function: training employees for long, stable careers in one company. In the 1990s-2000s it became strategic: aligning skills with business goals, talent management, and succession planning. Today the role is a partner in change: HRD builds digital skills, supports remote and hybrid teams, personalises learning through online platforms, uses data analytics to spot skill gaps, and manages well-being. Example: while the old HRD gave a once-a-year classroom course, today's HRD in companies like Infosys runs continuous e-learning, self-paced micro-courses, and career coaches for every employee. The employee is no longer a passive receiver of training but an active owner of their development.

    Key points to remember: Functions = training, career development, appraisal, counselling, mentoring, welfare, engagement; shift = clerk → developer → strategic partner → change enabler; from one-time classroom training to continuous personalised learning.

    Takeaway: HRD moved from clerk to change partner - employees now own their development instead of passively receiving training.

  • Q5. Enumerate the history and need of competency mapping.

    Answer: Competency mapping - from McClelland (1973) and Boyatzis (1982) to Indian adoption by L&T, NTPC and Hindustan Petroleum - matches the right person to the right job.

    A competency is an underlying characteristic of a person - knowledge, skill, motive, or attitude - that leads to effective or superior performance in a job. The history of competency mapping begins in 1973, when psychologist David McClelland published "Testing for Competence Rather Than Intelligence", arguing that academic intelligence tests do not predict job success; what matters is the actual competencies of star performers. In 1982, Richard Boyatzis's book The Competent Manager defined competencies and linked them to managerial effectiveness. In India, competency mapping grew from the 1990s when companies like Larsen & Toubro, NTPC, and Hindustan Petroleum adopted competency dictionaries - L&T's dictionary maps 52 competencies to every role from CEO to shop-floor worker. The need for competency mapping is practical: it puts the right person in the right job by matching role requirements with people's competencies; it identifies training gaps so development money is spent where it matters; it makes performance appraisal objective by measuring against defined competencies; it supports succession planning by showing who is ready for bigger roles; it guides recruitment by specifying the exact competencies to test; and it helps career planning by showing employees what they must build to grow. Example: a retail chain mapping store-manager competencies found that "handling customer complaints" predicted performance better than seniority, so it changed its selection and training accordingly. Competency mapping is therefore the foundation that makes every other HRD system - training, appraisal, careers - work on data instead of guesswork.

    Key points to remember: History = McClelland 1973, Boyatzis 1982, Indian adoption by L&T/NTPC from the 1990s; need = right person-right job, gap analysis, objective appraisal, succession, recruitment, careers; competency dictionary = data foundation for all HRD systems.

    Takeaway: A competency dictionary turns training, appraisal, recruitment and succession into data-based decisions instead of guesswork.

  • Q6. Write a comprehension on career transition and choices.

    Answer: Careers are series of transitions between roles, levels and organisations, requiring choices like technical vs managerial and stability vs growth.

    A career is not a straight ladder anymore; it is a series of transitions - moves between jobs, roles, levels, or even organisations - and each transition requires a choice. Career transition means any significant change in an employee's work life: promotion to a managerial role, lateral movement to a new function, relocation, moving from an individual contributor to a team leader, or leaving to join another company or start a venture. Transitions are triggered by organisational changes (restructuring, mergers), performance and potential review outcomes, and personal aspirations. Choices in a career typically include: technical versus managerial path - staying as a specialist versus moving into people management, a classic choice for engineers and doctors; stability versus growth - a secure role versus a riskier high-growth one; specialisation versus breadth - deep expertise versus general management; and employment versus entrepreneurship - such as an IT professional leaving a service firm to build a startup. Modern careers are boundaryless (moving across organisations) and protean (self-directed, driven by values rather than by the employer). Example: a software engineer may move from coding to project management (a transition), choose between a product company and a service company (a choice), and later become an entrepreneur. Organisations help through career planning, dual career ladders, mentoring, and counselling. The takeaway for a student: treat your career as a portfolio of choices, collect varied experience early, and let HRD systems like competency mapping and mentoring guide each transition deliberately rather than accidentally.

    Key points to remember: Transition = any major change of role, level, or employer; choices = technical vs managerial, stability vs growth, specialisation vs breadth, job vs venture; boundaryless and protean careers; deliberate planning beats accidental drift.

    Takeaway: Treat your career as a portfolio of choices and let HRD systems like competency mapping and mentoring guide each transition deliberately rather than accidentally.

  • Q7. Define HRD culture and climate. Write an overview on elements of HRD climate.

    Answer: HRD culture is the shared development values of an organisation while climate is how employees experience those practices day to day, measured through the OCTAPAC elements.

    HRD culture refers to the shared values, beliefs, and norms of an organisation about developing people - whether learning, openness, and employee growth are genuinely prized or merely talked about. HRD climate is the perception employees hold about the organisation's development practices - it is "culture as experienced", the day-to-day feel of how training, feedback, and growth actually happen. A positive HRD climate exists when employees believe the organisation is serious about their development. The key elements of HRD climate are: openness - employees can say what they think without fear; confrontation - problems are raised and solved rather than hidden; trust - management and employees believe in each other's intentions; autonomy - employees are given freedom to do their work and try new ideas; proaction - the organisation anticipates needs instead of reacting late; authenticity - behaviour matches stated values, and people are genuine; collaboration - teams and departments cooperate rather than compete; and feedback - performance information flows freely. These form the OCTAPAC elements (Openness, Confrontation, Trust, Autonomy, Proaction, Authenticity, Collaboration) - the famous OCTAPAC acronym from T.V. Rao's HRD climate survey. Example: an organisation where appraisals are two-way, learning is funded, and mistakes are treated as learning has a healthy HRD climate; one where feedback is one-way and training is cut at the first cost pressure has a poor climate. Climate is measurable through surveys, and improving it is management's direct responsibility.

    Key points to remember: Culture = shared values about development; climate = employees' perception of development practices; OCTAPAC = Openness, Confrontation, Trust, Autonomy, Proaction, Authenticity, Collaboration; climate is measurable and improvable.

    Takeaway: A healthy HRD climate - openness, trust, autonomy and feedback - is measurable through surveys and improvable by management.

  • Q8. Explain the development approach to industrial relations.

    Answer: The development approach replaces adversarial industrial relations with union-management partnership, worker education, counselling-based discipline and productivity-linked rewards.

    The development approach to industrial relations (IR) treats workers not as adversaries to be controlled but as human resources to be developed - it links industrial relations with HRD. Under the traditional or adversarial approach, management and unions confront each other: management guards its rights, unions fight for theirs, and conflict is resolved through power, strikes, and legal battles. The development approach replaces confrontation with cooperation: both sides accept that the organisation's growth benefits everyone, so the focus shifts to worker development, skill building, and participation. Its key features are: unions and management work as partners in productivity and growth; worker education and training raise capability so workers can handle higher-value work; grievance and discipline issues are handled through counselling and development rather than punishment alone; communication and joint committees build trust and prevent disputes; and wages and welfare are linked to productivity so that development pays for itself. Example: the Tata group's long tradition of constructive IR - recognition of unions, regular joint consultation, and worker development programmes - has produced decades of industrial peace and productivity, in contrast to the adversarial stand-offs seen in some other industries. The development approach also underlines the shift in HRD from "HRD for managers only" to HRD for all employees, including shop-floor workers. Its limitation is that it depends on mutual trust and can be weakened when management is exploitative or unions are politically fragmented. In the era of the new labour codes and automation, the development approach is the most sustainable path: employees who grow with the organisation do not need conflict to be heard.

    Key points to remember: Development approach = workers as partners, not adversaries; features = union-management partnership, worker education, counselling-based discipline, joint committees, productivity-linked wages; Tata as the classic Indian example; needs mutual trust to succeed.

    Takeaway: Developing workers as partners beats confronting them - employees who grow with the organisation do not need conflict to be heard.
