Q1. What is HRD? Explain the various mechanisms of HRD.

Answer: HRD is the systematic development of employees' knowledge, skills, abilities, and attitudes to improve individual and organisational performance. Prof. T.V. Rao, the Father of HRD in India, defined it as "a set of systematic and planned activities designed by an organisation to provide its members with the necessary learning opportunities to improve their performance and enable the organisation to achieve its goals." HRD is not just training; it is a holistic ecosystem with nine mechanisms: performance appraisal (evaluates current job performance), potential appraisal (assesses future capability), training (builds specific job skills), management development (grows managerial capabilities), organisation development (improves organisational effectiveness), feedback (provides performance information), counselling (addresses personal and work issues), rewards (motivates through recognition), and employee welfare (ensures well-being). The memory aid is PPTM-OD-FCRW. L&T pioneered HRD practices in India in 1975. In the post-COVID VUCA world, HRD must respond to remote work, hybrid models, and data-driven people development, making agile interventions essential for organisations like the IT firm that found 43% of its 3,000 employees wanted to work from home.

Key points:

  • HRD is the systematic development of employees' knowledge, skills, abilities, and attitudes to improve individual and organisational performance.
  • Rao, the Father of HRD in India, defined it as "a set of systematic and planned activities designed by an organisation to provide its members with the necessary learning opportunities to improve their performance and enable the organisation to achieve its goals." HRD is not just training; it is a holistic ecosystem with nine mechanisms: performance appraisal (evaluates current job performance), potential appraisal (assesses future capability), training (builds specific job skills), management development (grows managerial capabilities), organisation development (improves organisational effectiveness), feedback (provides performance information), counselling (addresses personal and work issues), rewards (motivates through recognition), and employee welfare (ensures well-being).
  • L&T pioneered HRD practices in India in 1975.

Q2. Explain Kirkpatrick's four-level model of training evaluation with suitable examples.

Answer: Kirkpatrick's model evaluates training effectiveness at four levels. Level 1 is Reaction: did participants enjoy the training? It is measured through feedback forms immediately after the programme. Level 2 is Learning: did participants learn? It is tested through tests and demonstrations at the end of training. Level 3 is Behaviour: are participants applying the learning on the job? It is assessed through observation and 360-degree feedback three to six months later. Level 4 is Results: did organisational performance improve? It is measured through KPIs and productivity data six to twelve months later. The model builds upward like a pyramid, with each level providing stronger evidence of effectiveness. Most organisations only measure Level 1, the "happy sheets"; progressive firms measure up to Level 4. The Tatas use a modified Kirkpatrick model and add a Level 5 (ROI), calculating (Benefits - Cost) / Cost x 100 to show the return on training investment, for example after leadership programmes. The memory aid is RLBR: Reaction, Learning, Behaviour, Results. Training design itself follows the ADDIE framework: Analyse, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluate.

Key points:

  • Kirkpatrick's model evaluates training effectiveness at four levels.
  • The Tatas use a modified Kirkpatrick model and add a Level 5 (ROI), calculating (Benefits - Cost) / Cost x 100 to show the return on training investment, for example after leadership programmes.
  • Level 1 is Reaction: did participants enjoy the training?

Takeaway: The memory aid is RLBR: Reaction, Learning, Behaviour, Results.

Q3. What is competency mapping? Explain the steps involved in competency mapping.

Answer: A competency is an underlying characteristic of a person that leads to effective and/or superior performance in a job (Boyatzis, 1982). Competencies are of two types: threshold competencies, the minimum required to do a job (for example, typing speed for a data entry operator), and differentiating competencies, which distinguish superior performers from average ones (for example, leadership initiative). Competency frameworks have four categories: technical (job-specific knowledge and skills), behavioural (communication, teamwork, ethics), managerial (planning, organising, controlling), and leadership (vision, inspiration, change) - remembered as TBML. Competency mapping follows five steps: identify roles and responsibilities, identify the competencies required for each role, assess current competency levels (using assessments and 360-degree feedback), identify gaps between required and current levels, and develop an action plan using training, coaching, and job rotation. L&T uses a competency dictionary with 52 competencies mapped to every role, from CEO to shop-floor worker. Competency mapping is conceptually linked to the KSAA framework of human capital: knowledge, skills, abilities, and attitudes.

Key points:

  • A competency is an underlying characteristic of a person that leads to effective and/or superior performance in a job (Boyatzis, 1982).
  • Competencies are of two types: threshold competencies, the minimum required to do a job (for example, typing speed for a data entry operator), and differentiating competencies, which distinguish superior performers from average ones (for example, leadership initiative).
  • Competency mapping follows five steps: identify roles and responsibilities, identify the competencies required for each role, assess current competency levels (using assessments and 360-degree feedback), identify gaps between required and current levels, and develop an action plan using training, coaching, and job rotation.

Q4. Differentiate between counselling, coaching, and mentoring. Explain the GROW model of coaching.

Answer: Counselling, coaching, and mentoring are three distinct helping relationships. Counselling deals with personal and work problems; it is past-and-present focused and therapeutic, and is delivered by a trained counsellor. Coaching improves performance; it is present-focused, performance-driven, and delivered by a line manager or coach. Mentoring develops careers; it is future-focused and development-driven, delivered by a senior colleague. Counselling has three types: directive (the counsellor tells the employee what to do), non-directive (the employee finds their own solutions while the counsellor listens), and participative (collaborative problem-solving). The GROW model structures coaching conversations in four steps: Goal (define the destination - what do you want to achieve?), Reality (assess the current situation - where are you now?), Options (explore possibilities - what could you do?), and Will (commit to action - what will you do?). For example, a team member who wants to improve client presentations might plan to practice twice a week and present at the next team meeting. Mentoring can be formal (organisationally assigned pairs), informal, or reverse, where juniors teach seniors digital skills. Wipro's formal mentoring programme pairs senior leaders with high-potential employees, resulting in 40% faster promotions for mentees.

Key points:

  • Counselling, coaching, and mentoring are three distinct helping relationships.
  • For example, a team member who wants to improve client presentations might plan to practice twice a week and present at the next team meeting.
  • Counselling deals with personal and work problems; it is past-and-present focused and therapeutic, and is delivered by a trained counsellor.
