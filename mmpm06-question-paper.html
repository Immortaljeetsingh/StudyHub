Question Paper - MMPM-06 Marketing Research (June 2023)

Actual IGNOU Term-End Examination question paper (June 2023, from the official IGNOU previous-year papers archive). Answer any three questions from Section A; Section B is compulsory.

Course: MMPM-006 - Marketing Research (MBA)

Examination: Term-End Examination, June 2023  |  Time: 3 Hours  |  Maximum Marks: 100 (Weightage: 70%)

Note: Answer any three questions from Section A. Section B is compulsory.

Section—A (60 marks)

  • Q1. (a) What is the difference between a marketing information system and marketing research? Justify the need for marketing research. (b) Briefly comment on the problems faced by researchers in conducting marketing research in India. [10 + 10 marks]
  • Q2. (a) Distinguish between Exploratory and Descriptive research designs, with the help of examples. (b) Explain with the help of suitable examples, the factors that influence an experiment’s validity. [10 + 10 marks]
  • Q3. (a) Distinguish between primary and secondary data. When should each be used? Explain with suitable examples. (b) What are the main sources of secondary data? Elucidate. [10 + 10 marks]
  • Q4. Write short notes on any three of the following: (a) Internet-based Marketing Research (b) Cluster Analysis (c) Coding of Data (d) Classification of Data (e) Conjoint Analysis. [20 marks]

Section—B (40 marks, compulsory)

  • Q5. A manufacturer of bakery items markets several brands of cookies to a chain of supermarkets. The product category is very competitive, and competitors use a great deal of newspaper and television advertising, with some moving into social media advertising. The owner of the firm desires more information for making promotional decisions on social media. (a) Design a social media marketing research process for the firm. (b) Discuss the benefits of using social media for marketing research. [20 + 20 marks]

Solved Answers - MMPM-06 Question Paper

Easy model answers in plain English for every question of the June 2023 paper, grounded in the 15 units of the StudyHub study notes.

Q1(a). Difference between a marketing information system and marketing research; need for marketing research. Q1(b). Problems faced by researchers in India.

Answer: An MIS is a continuous system feeding routine information from existing data, while marketing research is a project-based activity generating fresh problem-specific data - and research is needed whenever the cost of a wrong decision exceeds the cost of research.

A marketing information system (MIS) and marketing research both supply information for decisions, but they work differently. An MIS is a continuous, on-going system that collects, stores, analyses and distributes routine data from internal and external sources; it works with existing data such as sales records, company databases and regular reports, and answers recurring questions. Marketing research is a project-based, one-time activity that generates new, problem-specific information through surveys, experiments and observation. Simply put: MIS feeds routine information continuously, while research creates fresh information for a specific problem at a point in time.

The need for marketing research is clear: managers make decisions under uncertainty and risk, and wrong decisions are expensive. Research supplies facts, reduces risk, reveals customer needs and market opportunities, evaluates alternatives like new products or campaigns, and tracks competitors - whenever the cost of research is less than the cost of a wrong decision.

Researchers in India face several problems:

  • Linguistic diversity: 22 official languages and 400+ dialects make questionnaire translation and back-translation essential.
  • Rural accessibility: about 70% of the population is rural; fieldwork is costly and slow.
  • Respondent issues: low literacy limits some techniques; respondents give hurried or socially acceptable answers.
  • Quality and fraud: interviewers may straight-line or steer answers; 58% of businesses feel research quality falls short.
  • Resource problems: high staff attrition (about 30% yearly) and delays - 42% of managers feel research takes too long to be useful.

Key points to remember: MIS = continuous, existing data, routine decisions; research = project-based, new data, specific problems. In India: translate, respect culture, verify quality, plan for delays.

Takeaway: Contrast the two on continuity (MIS = ongoing, research = project) and data (existing vs new), then justify research by decision risk and finish with the India-specific problems.

Q2(a). Exploratory vs Descriptive research designs with examples. Q2(b). Factors that influence an experiment’s validity.

Answer: Exploratory research is flexible and small-scale, used when the problem is unclear, while descriptive (conclusive) research is pre-planned and structured, used to describe market characteristics with specific hypotheses - and experiment validity splits into internal (true cause) and external (generalization).

Exploratory research is used when the problem is unclear or poorly understood. It is flexible, low-structure, and small-scale, using literature review, expert interviews, focus groups and pilot studies to generate insights and hypotheses. It answers “what is happening?” - for example, a company that discovers falling sales in Pune conducts dealer interviews and focus groups to find possible reasons. Descriptive research describes market characteristics with pre-planned structure and specific hypotheses. It uses surveys, panels and observation, and answers “who, how many, when and where?” - for example, a structured customer satisfaction survey of smartphone users measuring ratings by age and city.

Experiment validity has two sides. Internal validity asks whether the change in the dependent variable was really caused by the independent variable; it is threatened by history (events like a festival or competitor action), maturation (natural change over time), testing effect, instrumentation (measuring tools changing), selection bias, and mortality (subjects dropping out). External validity asks whether results generalise to other markets, people or times.

Example: testing a new store layout during Diwali - the festive rush (history) may raise sales even without the new layout; adding a control branch and randomisation isolates the true effect.

Key points to remember: Exploratory = insight when the problem is vague; Descriptive = structure and hypotheses. Validity = internal (did X really cause Y?) + external (does it generalise?).

Takeaway: Exploratory = insight and hypotheses for vague problems; conclusive = structured description; and validity always splits into internal (true cause) and external (generalization).

Q3(a). Primary vs secondary data, when to use each. Q3(b). Main sources of secondary data.

Answer: Primary data is collected first-hand for the specific problem (accurate but costly), secondary data already exists (cheap and fast) - so start with secondary and add primary only when gaps remain, drawing on internal records, government data, associations and syndicated agencies.

Primary data is collected first-hand for the specific research problem through surveys, interviews, observation or experiments. It is accurate, current and problem-specific, but costly and time-consuming. Use it when secondary data is unavailable, outdated or insufficient, and when you need control over quality and methodology - for example, your own survey of college students’ brand preferences. Secondary data has already been collected by someone else for another purpose, such as government reports, journals and company records. It is cheap, fast and useful for background, defining the problem and building hypotheses - for example, NSSO consumption data to size a market. Researchers typically start with secondary data and add primary data only when gaps remain.

Main sources of secondary data:

  • Internal sources: company sales records, invoices, customer databases, warranty claims, earlier research reports.
  • Government publications: NSSO surveys, Census of India, RBI bulletins, CMIE databases.
  • Trade and industry associations: FICCI, CII, chamber reports and industry surveys.
  • Commercial agencies: syndicated research from agencies like Nielsen or Kantar.
  • Other sources: newspapers, journals, magazines, libraries, company annual reports, and the internet.

Key points to remember: Secondary first (cheap, fast), primary when precision is needed. Sources: internal records, government data, associations, syndicated agencies, internet.

Takeaway: Answer the “when” question - secondary first for background and problem definition, primary when you need current, problem-specific, controlled data.

Q4. Short notes: Internet-based Marketing Research, Cluster Analysis, Coding of Data, Classification of Data, Conjoint Analysis.

Answer: The short notes cover internet-based research (speed and reach with representativeness limits), cluster analysis (grouping respondents into segments), coding (converting raw answers into analysable codes), classification (arranging data into classes) and conjoint analysis (measuring attribute trade-offs).

(a) Internet-based Marketing Research: conducting research over the web - online surveys, web panels, e-mail surveys, click-stream and cookie tracking, and online communities. Benefits are speed, low cost, global reach, real-time data and easy automation; limitations are sample representativeness, low response control and authenticity of respondents.

(b) Cluster Analysis: a multivariate technique that groups objects or respondents into homogeneous segments so that members within a group are highly similar and groups differ from each other. It is widely used for market segmentation - for example, grouping buyers into price-sensitive, quality-conscious and convenience-seeking clusters.

(c) Coding of Data: assigning numerical or symbolic codes to responses so they can be entered, tabulated and analysed; a codebook records each code. It converts raw answers into analyzable form and is part of data processing.

(d) Classification of Data: arranging data into classes or categories on the basis of common characteristics, such as age groups, income slabs or regions, so patterns become visible and analysis becomes manageable.

(e) Conjoint Analysis: a technique that measures the relative importance of product attributes by presenting respondents with combinations of attributes (profiles) and asking them to rank or choose. It helps in new product design, feature decisions and pricing - for example, whether phone buyers value camera over battery life.

Key points to remember: Internet research = speed + reach, watch representativeness; cluster = segmentation; coding + classification = preparing data for analysis; conjoint = attribute trade-offs.

Takeaway: Link each technique to its purpose - internet = collection, cluster = segmentation, coding and classification = preparation, conjoint = attribute importance.

Q5. Case: social media marketing research for a bakery cookies manufacturer (a) design a process (b) benefits of social media for marketing research.

Answer: A social media marketing research process runs from problem definition through objectives, secondary data, social listening, qualitative research, surveys and analysis to reporting - and social media research wins on speed, cost, reach and authentic opinions, but loses on representativeness and privacy.

(a) Designing a social media marketing research process:

  • Problem definition: the owner wants to know which social media platforms and messages will make cookie promotions effective.
  • Objectives: e.g., find customer content preferences, best platform, and likely response to promotions.
  • Secondary data: competitor advertising, industry reports on social media usage.
  • Social listening: monitor brand, competitor and category conversations; track sentiment and trending topics.
  • Qualitative research: online communities, virtual focus groups, comments analysis (netnography).
  • Surveys: social media polls, web-based questionnaires and panels targeting cookie buyers.
  • Analysis and reporting: quantify engagement, sentiment and preferences; report recommendations for the promotion decision.

(b) Benefits of social media for marketing research: real-time, fast feedback; low cost compared with fieldwork; access to large and specific audiences; unfiltered, authentic consumer opinions; the ability to track campaign performance and engagement; and ideas for new products and messages. Caution is needed - samples may not represent the full population, fake profiles and privacy concerns exist, so social media research works best alongside traditional methods.

Key points to remember: process = define, listen, qualify, survey, analyse. Benefits = speed, cost, reach, authentic opinions; balance the answer by noting limitations.

Takeaway: Structure the case answer as the seven-step process and always balance the benefits of social media research with its sample and privacy limitations.
