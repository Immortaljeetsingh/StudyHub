Q1. (a) Contract under Section 2(h) and essentials of a valid contract under Section 10  |  (b) Doctrine of frustration

Answer: Under Section 2(h) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, a contract is an agreement enforceable by law. Every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract — enforceability by law is the deciding feature. Section 10 lays down the five essentials of a valid contract: (1) Free consent — parties must agree on the same thing in the same sense, and consent must not be caused by coercion (Section 15), undue influence (Section 16), fraud (Section 17), misrepresentation (Section 18) or mistake (Sections 20-22); (2) Competency of parties — under Section 11, parties must be major (18 years or above), of sound mind, and not disqualified by law; (3) Lawful consideration — the price of the promise, which need not be adequate but must be lawful; (4) Lawful object — the purpose must not be illegal or immoral; and (5) the agreement must not be expressly declared void. The offer (Section 2(a)) must be communicated and certain, and acceptance (Section 2(b)) must be absolute and unqualified. When performance becomes impossible after the contract is made, the doctrine of frustration under Section 56 discharges both parties — for example, where a house contracted to be sold is destroyed by fire before the sale.

Key points:
  • Contract (S.2(h)) = agreement + enforceability; every contract is an agreement, not vice versa (Unit 5).
  • Five S.10 essentials: free consent, competency, lawful consideration, lawful object, not expressly void (Unit 5).
  • Free consent (S.14): no coercion (S.15), undue influence (S.16), fraud (S.17), misrepresentation (S.18), mistake (S.20-22) (Unit 5).
  • Offer (S.2(a)) must be communicated and certain; acceptance (S.2(b)) must be absolute and unqualified (Unit 5).
  • Frustration (S.56): supervening impossibility discharges both parties — house destroyed by fire example (Unit 5).

Takeaway: S.2(h) + S.10 is the exam gateway — test every essential before calling an agreement a contract; breach opens the S.73-74 remedy toolkit.

Q2. (a) Types of partners and the position of a minor  |  (b) Essentials of partnership

Answer: Under Section 4 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. The Act recognises six types of partners: active or ostensible partners, who participate in management, have unlimited liability and must give public notice on retirement; sleeping or dormant partners, who only invest capital and need no notice; nominal partners, who lend only their name and are still liable to third parties; partners in profits only, who share profits but not losses; sub-partners, who share a partner's profits with no liability to the firm; and partners by estoppel, who are held out as partners and become liable to third parties. A minor cannot become a full partner but may be admitted to the benefits of partnership: he is entitled to a share of profits and access to accounts, and his liability is limited to his share in the firm. On attaining majority, he has six months to decide whether to become a full partner; if no public notice is given, he is deemed to have become a partner. The five essentials of partnership are: minimum two persons (maximum fifty under the Companies Act, 2013), an agreement, sharing of profits, a business carried on by all or any acting for all, and mutual agency.

Key points:
  • Partnership (S.4): relation of persons agreeing to share profits of a business carried on by all or any acting for all (Unit 3).
  • Six types: active/ostensible, sleeping/dormant, nominal, profits-only, sub-partner, by estoppel (Unit 3).
  • Liability & notice: active and nominal need public notice on retirement; sleeping partner needs none (Unit 3).
  • Minor (S.30): benefits only — profit share + accounts; liability limited to share; opt-in within 6 months of majority (Unit 3).
  • Five essentials: 2+ persons (max 50), agreement, profit sharing, business, mutual agency (Unit 3).

Takeaway: Six partner types + minor's limited role are the sure-shot marks of Unit 3.

Q3. (a) Characteristics of a company and Salomon v Salomon  |  (b) MOA vs AOA

Answer: Under Section 2(20) of the Companies Act, 2013, a company is an incorporated association — an artificial person created by law, having a separate legal entity, perpetual succession and a common seal. Its main characteristics are: incorporation (registration is compulsory), separate legal entity — the company owns assets and can sue and be sued in its own name, established in Salomon v Salomon & Co. Ltd (1897), where the House of Lords held that the company was a distinct person from its owner even though Salomon held almost all the shares; perpetual succession, because members may come and go but the company continues; limited liability, limited to the unpaid amount on shares; artificial person, acting through its directors; transferability of shares (Section 44); and a common seal, now optional after the 2015 amendment. The Memorandum of Association is the company's charter that defines its relationship with the outside world through six clauses (name, registered office, objects, liability, capital, subscription), while the Articles of Association are the internal rules governing the management of the company. MOA cannot be altered easily because it protects outsiders and creditors; AOA can be changed by a special resolution. The Tata Group illustrates perpetual succession — it has continued for over a century while its members have changed.

Key points:
  • Company (S.2(20)): artificial person, separate legal entity, perpetual succession, common seal (Unit 4).
  • Salomon v Salomon (1897): company is distinct from its members even with one dominant shareholder (Unit 4).
  • Features: incorporated, limited liability, transferable shares (S.44), acts through directors, common seal optional since 2015 (Unit 4).
  • MOA = external charter with 6 clauses; AOA = internal rules; AOA is subordinate to MOA (Unit 4).
  • Formation: promotion → registration → commencement of business (S.10A) (Unit 4).

Takeaway: MOA/AOA distinction + Salomon case are the guaranteed marks of Unit 4.

Q4. (a) FEMA 1999 objectives and differences from FERA 1973  |  (b) Capital vs current account transactions

Answer: The Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999 replaced the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA), 1973, shifting from regulation to management of foreign exchange. FEMA has 7 chapters and 49 sections, with rules framed by the Central Government and regulations by the RBI. Its objectives are to facilitate external trade and payments and to promote the orderly development and maintenance of the foreign exchange market in India. FEMA differs from FERA on four counts: FERA was restrictive while FEMA is liberal; FERA conserved foreign exchange while FEMA manages it; contraventions under FERA were criminal offences while under FEMA they are civil offences; and FERA presumed guilt until proven innocent, whereas FEMA presumes innocence. Under FEMA, transactions are classified into capital account transactions, which alter assets or liabilities — such as FDI, FPI and external borrowing — and current account transactions, such as exports, imports and remittances, which are generally free. A person resident in India is one who stays 182 days or more in the preceding financial year or is registered or incorporated in India. The Enforcement Directorate investigates contraventions of FEMA.

Key points:
  • FEMA 1999 replaced FERA 1973: restrictive→liberal, criminal→civil, guilty-presumed→innocent-presumed (Unit 8).
  • Objectives: facilitate external trade and payments; orderly forex market development (Unit 8).
  • Capital account (S.6) alters assets/liabilities — FDI, FPI, external borrowing; current account — exports, imports, remittances, generally free (Unit 8).
  • Resident: 182+ days in preceding financial year or incorporated in India (Unit 8).
  • Structure: 7 chapters, 49 sections, 6 rules (Central Govt), 23 regulations (RBI); ED investigates (Unit 8).

Takeaway: FEMA-CCA — FEMA, Capital, Current, Authorised person; FERA vs FEMA comparison appears almost every year.

Q5. (a) CSR under Section 135  |  (b) Anti-competitive agreements

Answer: Under Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013, a company becomes eligible for CSR obligations if, in any financial year, its net worth is Rs. 500 crore or more, or its turnover is Rs. 1000 crore or more, or its net profit is Rs. 5 crore or more. Such companies must constitute a CSR committee of at least three directors, including one independent director, and must spend at least two per cent of the average net profits of the preceding three years on CSR activities. The activities are specified in Schedule VII and include education, healthcare, environment protection, rural development, slum area development, gender equality and women empowerment, contributions to PM funds, disaster management, protection of national heritage and welfare of the armed forces. Companies must disclose CSR spending, and the reasons for non-spending, in the Board's report. Global frameworks such as the UN Global Compact, ISO 26000 and GRI Standards guide responsible business practice. Reliance Industries spends over Rs. 1000 crore annually on CSR through the Reliance Foundation. The Competition Act, 2002 prohibits anti-competitive agreements — agreements that cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition in India. These include horizontal agreements (cartels, price fixing, bid rigging among competitors) and vertical agreements (tie-in arrangements, exclusive supply and distribution, resale price maintenance). The Competition Commission of India (CCI) investigates and penalises such conduct.

Key points:
  • CSR triggers (S.135): net worth Rs. 500 cr, turnover Rs. 1000 cr or net profit Rs. 5 cr (Unit 12).
  • Spend 2% of average net profit of preceding three years; Schedule VII lists 10 activity areas (Unit 12).
  • CSR committee: min 3 directors incl. 1 independent; reasons for non-spending disclosed in Board's report (Unit 12).
  • Global standards: UN Global Compact, ISO 26000, GRI (Unit 12).
  • Anti-competitive agreements (Competition Act 2002): horizontal — cartels, price fixing, bid rigging (per se); vertical — tie-in, exclusive dealing, RPM (rule of reason) (Unit 13).
  • Penalties: up to 10% of turnover; cartels 3x profit; leniency up to 100% for first applicant (Unit 13).

Takeaway: CSR = 2% rule with three financial triggers; horizontal vs vertical anti-competitive agreements is the CCI question.

Q6. (a) ADR methods  |  (b) Patents, copyright and trademarks

Answer: Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) covers methods of settling disputes outside the courts. The four main methods are: arbitration, where a neutral third party (arbitrator) makes a binding decision after hearing both sides; mediation, where a facilitator helps the parties reach their own voluntary agreement; conciliation, where a conciliator studies the dispute and proposes a settlement, which the parties may accept or reject; and negotiation, where the parties discuss directly without any third party. Compared with litigation, ADR is faster, cheaper, more flexible and confidential — but arbitration alone gives a binding decision, while the others depend on the consent of the parties. ADR is widely used in commercial, family and consumer disputes in India, and courts increasingly refer cases to it under Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure. Intellectual Property Rights protect creations of the mind. A patent grants an exclusive right over an invention (product or process) for 20 years from filing, preventing others from making or selling it without permission. Copyright protects original literary, artistic and musical works for the life of the author plus 60 years, covering expression rather than ideas. A trademark distinguishes goods or services of one business from another (brand names, logos, slogans) and is renewable indefinitely. Indian companies such as Tata, Mahindra and Zomato protect their brands as trademarks, while patent filings in India are growing in pharma and software.

Key points:
  • ADR: arbitration (binding), mediation (agreement), conciliation (proposal), negotiation (direct) (Unit 2).
  • ADR vs litigation: faster, cheaper, flexible, confidential; courts refer cases under S.89 CPC (Unit 2).
  • Patent (Patents Act 1970): 20 years from filing; novelty, inventive step, industrial application (Unit 9).
  • Copyright (Copyright Act 1957): life + 60 years, automatic, covers expression not ideas; moral rights — attribution, integrity (Unit 9).
  • Trademark (Trade Marks Act 1999): 10 years renewable, protects brand identity (Unit 9).

Takeaway: ADR binds only in arbitration; patent 20 yr, copyright life+60, trademark 10 yr renewable — the durations carry marks.

Q1. Define Business Law. Discuss the objectives of Business Law.

Answer: Business Law is the portion of the legal system that guarantees orderly conduct of business affairs and the just settlement of legitimate business disputes, resting on three pillars — Law, Rule of Law and Property (Unit 1).

Key points:
  • Three pillars: Law (state-backed rules), Rule of Law (equal application), Property (right to possess, use, transfer resources) (Unit 1).
  • 12 objectives: universal standards of conduct, industrial growth, establishment procedure, enforcement of rights, healthy business relationships, reduced fraud, maintaining equilibrium, ethical conduct, social responsibility, evolving standards, penalties for violations, insurance against risks (Unit 1).
  • Governs all commercial relationships: contracts, partnerships, companies, negotiable instruments, insurance and IP (Unit 2).
  • Satyam Scam (2009) triggered Companies Act 2013 reforms — independent directors, strict related-party rules, class actions (Unit 1).
  • Internal factors (controllable) vs external factors (laws, economy — uncontrollable) shape business decisions (Unit 1).

Takeaway: LRP-SEC — Law, Rule of Law, Property + Sources, Environment, Courts; memorise the 12 objectives (Unit 1).

Q2. Discuss the modes of dissolution of a partnership and explain the grounds on which a court can order dissolution of a firm.

Answer: A firm may be dissolved by agreement, compulsorily, on contingencies, by notice (partnership at will), or by order of court on the grounds listed in Section 44 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 (Unit 3).

Key points:
  • Modes: by agreement (S.40); compulsory — e.g., business becomes illegal (S.41); on contingencies — death/insolvency of a partner (S.42); by notice — partnership at will (S.43); by court order (S.44) (Unit 3).
  • Court grounds (S.44): partner's insanity; permanent incapacity; misconduct prejudicial to business; persistent breach of agreement; transfer of whole interest; business only viable at a loss; just and equitable grounds.
  • Reconstitution ≠ dissolution: in reconstitution the firm continues with changed members (Unit 3).
  • Unregistered firm: cannot sue third parties; settlement of accounts follows dissolution (Unit 3).
  • Registration is not mandatory but is needed to enforce rights against third parties (Unit 3).

Takeaway: Five modes of dissolution; the seven S.44 court grounds are the examiner's favourite list (Unit 3).

Q4. Describe the process of formation of a contract. Discuss the main characteristics that an offer/proposal must possess. Also explain the essentials of a valid acceptance.

Answer: A contract forms when a lawful offer (S.2(a)) is met with absolute, unqualified acceptance (S.2(b)) supported by consideration (S.2(d)), producing an agreement (S.2(e)) that is enforceable by law (S.2(h)) (Unit 5).

Key points:
  • Offer (S.2(a)): willingness to do or abstain from doing something; must be communicated, create legal relations, and have certain terms; distinct from an invitation to offer (Unit 5).
  • Offer may be express or implied, general or specific; lapses on rejection, revocation, or expiry (Unit 5).
  • Acceptance (S.2(b)): absolute and unqualified; communicated to the offeror in the prescribed manner or a usual manner; within a reasonable time; only by the person to whom the offer is made (Unit 5).
  • Consideration (S.2(d)): price of the promise; need not be adequate but must be lawful and real (Unit 5).
  • Validity under S.10: free consent (S.14), capacity (S.11), lawful consideration and object, not expressly void (Unit 5).
  • Sequence: offer → acceptance → consideration → agreement → contract (Unit 5).

Takeaway: "Offer + absolute acceptance + consideration = agreement; agreement + enforceability = contract" (Unit 5).

Q5. What is the objective of the Foreign Exchange Management Act? Discuss the mechanism for acquiring property in India by a non-resident and outside India by a resident.

Answer: FEMA 1999 (7 chapters, 49 sections) aims to facilitate external trade and payments and promote an orderly forex market; property acquisition follows FEMA capital/current account rules administered by the RBI (Unit 8).

Key points:
  • Objectives: facilitate external trade and payments; promote orderly development of the foreign exchange market (Unit 8).
  • Non-resident acquiring property in India: permitted to purchase immovable property (other than agricultural land, plantation or farmhouse) in compliance with FEMA/RBI regulations (Unit 8).
  • Resident acquiring property outside India: restricted; generally requires RBI approval, except property acquired by inheritance or under specific permissible provisions (Unit 8).
  • Funds must move through authorised persons and banking channels; all transactions reported to RBI (Unit 8).
  • Contraventions are civil offences; the Enforcement Directorate investigates (Unit 8).

Takeaway: FEMA manages rather than conserves foreign exchange — property rules flip with residency status (Unit 8).

Q6. Explain the necessity for the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 (IBC-2016) and briefly discuss the four pillars of Institutional Infrastructure under IBC-2016.

Answer: IBC 2016 replaced fragmented insolvency laws with one time-bound (330-day), creditor-led resolution code; its institutional infrastructure stands on four pillars — adjudicating authority, IBBI, insolvency professionals and information utilities (IBC 2016).

Key points:
  • Necessity: consolidated SICA, RDDB and winding-up regimes; earlier laws were slow, debtor-friendly and produced poor recovery (IBC 2016).
  • IBC shift: creditors drive resolution; default triggers CIRP; moratorium protects the corporate debtor.
  • Pillar 1 — Adjudicating Authority: NCLT (companies) and DRT (individuals/firms) decide time-bound (IBC 2016).
  • Pillar 2 — IBBI: regulator framing rules and overseeing the insolvency process and profession.
  • Pillar 3 — Insolvency Professionals: licensed experts who run the resolution process and liquidation.
  • Pillar 4 — Information Utilities: repositories of financial information (defaults, liabilities) for quick verification.

Takeaway: NCLT/DRT + IBBI + IPs + IUs = the four pillars; resolution first, liquidation only as last resort (IBC 2016).

Q7. Discuss the role of 'Privacy' in the context of Digital World. Briefly, discuss the Personal Data Protection Bill, 2019.

Answer: Privacy in the digital world means individual control over personal data — a fundamental right in India (Puttaswamy 2017) now implemented through the DPDP Act 2023, which superseded the Personal Data Protection Bill 2019 (Unit 10).

Key points:
  • Digital context: profiling, targeted ads, data breaches and biometric identification make data the new currency (Unit 10).
  • Puttaswamy (2017): right to privacy is a fundamental right under Art. 21 (Unit 10).
  • PDP Bill 2019: proposed a comprehensive personal-data law with data fiduciary obligations, but was withdrawn and replaced by the DPDP Act 2023 (Unit 10).
  • DPDP Act 2023: applies to digital personal data; roles — data principal, data fiduciary, data processor (Unit 10).
  • 6 principles: lawfulness, purpose limitation, minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity & confidentiality; consent must be free, informed, specific, unambiguous and withdrawable (Unit 10).
  • Rights: access, correction, erasure, portability, objection; breach notification with significant penalties (Unit 10).

Takeaway: DPC-CLR — Data Protection Consent, Correction, Lawfulness, Rights; PDP Bill 2019 → DPDP Act 2023 (Unit 10).

Q8. Explain the applicability of Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 and discuss the duties and liabilities of e-commerce entities under this rule.

Answer: The E-Commerce Rules 2020 apply to all e-commerce entities selling or offering goods and services online to Indian consumers — both marketplace and inventory models — imposing transparency duties and consumer-liability standards (Unit 14).

Key points:
  • Applicability: e-commerce entities operating in India, including marketplaces and the sellers selling through them (Unit 14).
  • Duties: display seller details (name, address, contact), publish clear return/refund policies, appoint a grievance officer (Unit 14).
  • Prohibitions: misleading advertisements, unfair trade practices, manipulation of prices or search results.
  • Liability: entities liable for misleading information, counterfeit or defective goods sold on their platform.
  • Consumers may file complaints from their place of residence; the CCPA can act suo motu (Unit 14).
  • CPA 2019 covers e-commerce transactions with online complaint filing and product liability (Unit 14).

Takeaway: Online sellers must be transparent — seller details, refund policy and grievance officer are mandatory (Unit 14).

Q1. What do you mean by 'Business Law'? How is it significant to Business? What are the sources of Business Law?

Answer: Business Law is the body of rules governing commercial relationships; it is significant for orderly conduct, rights enforcement and fraud prevention, and its sources run from the Constitution down to courts and customs (Unit 1).

Key points:
  • Significance: the 12 objectives — universal standards, industrial growth, enforcement of rights, reduced fraud, equilibrium, ethical conduct, penalties, risk insurance (Unit 1).
  • Sources: Constitution of India → legislatures (Parliament, State) → regulatory bodies (SEBI, RBI, CCI) → courts/precedents (Unit 1).
  • Basic principles: law & morality, business ethics, common law, natural law, contractual principles (Unit 2).
  • Protects businesses and consumers; penalty for violation deters misconduct (Unit 1).

Takeaway: Significance = 12 objectives; sources = Constitution → legislature → regulators → courts (Unit 1).

Q2. What is 'Partnership'? Discuss the rights and duties of a partner.

Answer: Partnership (S.4, Partnership Act 1932) is the relation between persons who agree to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all; partners enjoy profit, management and account rights balanced by good-faith duties (Unit 3).

Key points:
  • Rights: share profits, participate in management, access books and accounts, be indemnified for expenses, retire from the firm (Unit 3).
  • Duties: act in utmost good faith, render true and accurate accounts, indemnify the firm for fraud or negligence (Unit 3).
  • Duties (contd.): use firm property only for the firm, not carry on a competing business, work for common advantage (Unit 3).
  • Mutual agency (S.4): every partner is an agent of the firm and of the other partners (Unit 3).
  • Rights and duties may be modified by the partnership deed; unregistered firms cannot sue third parties (Unit 3).

Takeaway: Partner rights = profit + management + accounts; duties = good faith, true accounts, no competition (Unit 3).

Q3. Discuss the fundamental documents that are important in formation of a company and briefly explain the contents of these documents.

Answer: A company is formed through three fundamental documents — the Memorandum of Association (MOA), the Articles of Association (AOA) and the Prospectus — filed with the Registrar for incorporation (Unit 4).

Key points:
  • MOA: the charter defining the company's relationship with the outside world — six clauses: name, registered office, objects, liability, capital, subscription (Unit 4).
  • AOA: internal rules governing management and administration; subordinate to the MOA (Unit 4).
  • Prospectus: document inviting the public to subscribe to shares; required for public issues (Unit 4).
  • Formation stages: promotion (idea by promoter) → registration/incorporation → commencement of business (declaration within 180 days, S.10A) (Unit 4).
  • On incorporation the company gains separate legal entity (Salomon v Salomon) (Unit 4).
  • Share capital recorded in MOA: authorised, issued, subscribed, paid-up (Unit 4).

Takeaway: MOA (external charter, 6 clauses) + AOA (internal rules) + prospectus; promotion → registration → commencement (Unit 4).

Q4. What are the essentials of a valid contract? When is a consent of a party to the contract said to be free? Discuss.

Answer: A valid contract (S.10) requires free consent, competency, lawful consideration, lawful object and not being expressly void; consent is free when it is not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake (S.14) (Unit 5).

Key points:
  • S.10 essentials: (1) free consent, (2) competency (S.11 — 18+, sound mind, not disqualified), (3) lawful consideration (S.2(d)), (4) lawful object, (5) not expressly declared void (Unit 5).
  • Free consent (S.14) = consent not caused by coercion (S.15), undue influence (S.16), fraud (S.17), misrepresentation (S.18) or mistake (S.20-22) (Unit 5).
  • Contract (S.2(h)) = agreement + enforceability; every contract is an agreement, not vice versa (Unit 5).
  • Void (S.2(j)): no legal effect from the beginning; voidable (S.2(i)): enforceable until avoided by the aggrieved party (Unit 5).
  • Frustration (S.56): supervening impossibility discharges both parties — house destroyed by fire example (Unit 5).

Takeaway: Five S.10 essentials + five vitiating factors (S.15-22) — consent is free only when none is present (Unit 5).

Q5. What is the need for regulating Banks? Discuss the evolution of Banking regulation.

Answer: Banks are regulated to protect depositors and ensure financial stability — in India by the RBI under the Banking Regulation Act 1949, with prudential norms (Basel I-III) evolving since the 1930s (Unit 7).

Key points:
  • Need: banks hold public deposits, run the payment system and perform maturity transformation (short-term liabilities vs long-term assets), making them fragile (Unit 7).
  • RBI = primary regulator and lender of last resort (Unit 7).
  • Evolution: RBI 1935 → Banking Regulation Act 1949 → nationalisation 1969/1980 → financial sector reforms 1991 → Basel I (1988, 8% capital) → Basel II (2004, three pillars) → Basel III (2010, higher capital + liquidity) (Unit 7).
  • Deposit insurance: DICGC insures up to Rs. 5 lakhs per depositor per bank (Unit 7).
  • Bank types: payment banks (max Rs. 2 lakh balance), small finance banks (75% priority sector), commercial banks (Unit 7).
  • FATF: anti-money-laundering guidelines; NBFCs regulated by RBI but cannot accept demand deposits (Unit 7).

Takeaway: RBI-FLB — RBI, Banking Regulation Act, Insurance (DICGC Rs. 5 lakh), Licensing, Basel norms (Unit 7).

Q6. Explain the justification for 'Intellectual Property' and discuss the basic aspects of patents and copyright.

Answer: IP law protects creations of the mind to incentivise creation (incentive theory) and reduce search costs; patents protect inventions for 20 years while copyright protects original expression for life + 60 years (Unit 9).

Key points:
  • Justification: incentive theory (rewards creators), non-rivalrous consumption (use by one does not diminish others), trademarks reduce search costs (Unit 9).
  • Patent (Patents Act 1970): 20 years from filing; needs novelty, inventive step, industrial application; S.3 exclusions — business methods, computer programmes per se, traditional knowledge (Unit 9).
  • Copyright (Copyright Act 1957): life + 60 years; automatic on creation; covers expression, not ideas (Unit 9).
  • Copyright rights: reproduce, issue copies, perform, translate, adapt; moral rights — attribution and integrity (Unit 9).
  • TRIPS: WTO minimum standards; compulsory licensing for public-health emergencies (Unit 9).
  • IP family: patent (20 yr), trademark (10 yr renewable), copyright (life+60), design (15 yr), GI (10 yr), trade secret (unlimited) (Unit 9).

Takeaway: PTCG-TD — Patents, Trademarks, Copyrights, GIs, Trade secrets, Designs, each with its duration (Unit 9).

Q7. Discuss the need for 'Right-to-Privacy' and 'Data Protection Laws'. Also describe the data protection framework in India.

Answer: Privacy is needed to protect personal autonomy and dignity in the data economy; India's framework runs Puttaswamy (2017) → PDP Bill 2019 → DPDP Act 2023 with six principles and five principal rights (Unit 10).

Key points:
  • Need: profiling, surveillance, identity theft and targeted marketing harm individuals without consent controls (Unit 10).
  • Puttaswamy (2017): privacy is a fundamental right under Art. 21 (Unit 10).

  • Authorised person: entity authorised by RBI to deal in foreign exchange — banks, money changers (Unit 8).
  • Duties: comply with RBI directions, KYC, maintain records, report to RBI, deal only within authorisation (Unit 8).
  • Enforcement: adjudication, penalties, compounding; ED investigates contraventions (Unit 8).

Takeaway: FEMA-CCA — capital account regulated (S.6); authorised persons are RBI's compliance arms (Unit 8).

Q6. Discuss 'Privacy' as an evolving concept in the Digital context and explain status of 'Right to Privacy' in India.

Answer: Privacy has evolved from physical seclusion to digital data control; in India it is a fundamental right (Puttaswamy 2017) implemented through the DPDP Act 2023 (Unit 10).

Key points:
  • Evolving concept: offline (home, body) → online (data, profiling, biometrics) (Unit 10).
  • Puttaswamy (2017): privacy is a fundamental right under Art. 21 with a proportionality test for state action (Unit 10).
  • Aadhaar judgment (2018): validated the scheme with safeguards built on Puttaswamy (Unit 10).
  • Framework: 6 principles; consent free/informed/specific/unambiguous/withdrawable (Unit 10).
  • Rights of data principal: access, correction, erasure, portability, objection (Unit 10).
  • DPDP Act 2023: digital personal data, extraterritorial application, breach notification, penalties (Unit 10).

Takeaway: Privacy = Art. 21 right; Puttaswamy → DPDP Act 2023 is the framework (Unit 10).

Q7. Explain the Environmental Protection Framework in India and its impact of businesses and industries.

Answer: India's environmental framework — Constitution (Art. 48A, 51A(g)), EPA 1986 umbrella, sectoral acts and the NGT — imposes EIA and pollution compliance on industry, raising costs but also creating sustainability opportunities (Unit 11).

Key points:
  • Framework layers: constitutional → EPA 1986 (umbrella) → Water Act 1974, Air Act 1981, Forest Act 1980 → NGT Act 2010 (Unit 11).
  • EIA categories A/B1/B2 with public hearing; CPCB (central) and SPCB (state) boards (Unit 11).
  • Impact on business: compliance costs, licences, carbon norms (Unit 11).
  • Penalties: fines, imprisonment, closure of operations — Sterlite Copper example (Unit 11).
  • Opportunities: green products, CSR (S.135), ESG reporting (Units 12, 14).

Takeaway: EPA-NGT; compliance is a business cost with a closure risk (Unit 11).

Q8. Briefly explain the evolution of Consumer Protection in India and discuss the role of Authorities which have been created under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, for the protection of consumer rights.

Answer: Consumer protection evolved MRTP 1969 → CPA 1986 → CPA 2019; the authorities — District/State/National Commissions and the CCPA — enforce consumer rights with e-commerce coverage and product liability (Unit 14).

Q2. What is dissolution of a firm? Discuss the different modes of dissolving a firm.

Answer: Dissolution ends the partnership relation among all partners; the modes are agreement, compulsory, contingencies, notice (partnership at will) and court order under S.44 (Unit 3).

Key points:
  • Modes: by agreement (S.40); compulsory — business becomes illegal (S.41); on contingencies — death/insolvency (S.42); by notice (S.43, partnership at will); by court order (S.44) (Unit 3).
  • Court grounds (S.44): insanity, permanent incapacity, misconduct, persistent breach, transfer of interest, business at a loss, just and equitable grounds.
  • Reconstitution ≠ dissolution — the firm continues in reconstitution (Unit 3).
  • Accounts settled and partners' rights adjusted on dissolution (Unit 3).
  • Registration matters: unregistered firms cannot sue third parties (Unit 3).

Takeaway: Five modes of dissolution; the seven S.44 court grounds carry marks (Unit 3).

Q3. What is a 'Prospectus'? Why is it needed? Explain the different types of Prospectuses.

Answer: A prospectus invites the public to subscribe to a company's securities with full disclosure; it is needed to raise capital and protect investors, and takes red herring, shelf, abridged and deemed forms (Unit 4).

Key points:
  • Meaning: public offer document with financials, objects, risks and promoter details (Unit 4).
  • Why needed: capital raising, investor protection, statutory requirement for public issues (Unit 4).
  • Red Herring Prospectus: no final price/issue size (Unit 4).
  • Shelf Prospectus: covers multiple issues in a period; Abridged: condensed with application form (Unit 4).
  • Deemed Prospectus: any document offering securities to the public (Unit 4).
  • Misstatement liability for promoters and directors (Unit 4).

Takeaway: Prospectus = disclosure + investor protection; 4 types (Unit 4).

Q4. What is a contract? Discuss the essentials of a valid contract in India.

Answer: A contract (S.2(h)) is an agreement enforceable by law; under S.10 it needs free consent, competency, lawful consideration, lawful object and must not be expressly void (Unit 5).

Key points:
  • Offer (S.2(a)) + acceptance (S.2(b)) + consideration (S.2(d)) = agreement (S.2(e)) (Unit 5).
  • Free consent (S.14): no coercion (S.15), undue influence (S.16), fraud (S.17), misrepresentation (S.18), mistake (S.20-22) (Unit 5).
  • Capacity (S.11): 18+, sound mind, not disqualified (Unit 5).
  • Void (S.2(j)) vs voidable (S.2(i)); frustration (S.56) discharges both parties (Unit 5).
  • Breach remedies: damages (S.73-74), specific performance, injunction (Unit 5).

Takeaway: S.10 essentials + five vitiating factors — ECLAB-OC (Unit 5).

Q5. Why is regulation of banking institutions needed? Describe the evolution of banking regulations.

Answer: Banking regulation protects depositors and ensures stability; it evolved from the RBI (1935) and Banking Regulation Act 1949 through nationalisation and Basel norms I to III (Unit 7).

Key points:
  • Need: public deposits, payment systems, maturity transformation, contagion risk (Unit 7).
  • Evolution: RBI Act 1934 → Banking Regulation Act 1949 → nationalisation 1969/1980 → 1991 reforms → Basel I (1988) → Basel II (2004) → Basel III (2010) (Unit 7).
  • Tools: licensing, CRR/SLR, capital adequacy, supervision, lender of last resort (Unit 7).
  • DICGC: deposit insurance up to Rs. 5 lakhs (Unit 7).
  • Bank types: payment (Rs. 2 lakh cap), small finance (75% priority sector), commercial (Unit 7).

Takeaway: RBI-FLB; Basel I → II → III progression is a favourite question (Unit 7).

Q6. Define 'Insolvency'. Explain the four pillars of institutional infrastructure created by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016.

Answer: Insolvency is the inability to pay debts as they fall due; IBC 2016 resolves it time-bound through four pillars — NCLT/DRT (adjudicating authority), IBBI, insolvency professionals and information utilities (IBC 2016).

Key points:
  • Insolvency = cash-flow/balance-sheet failure; bankruptcy = the final legal state (IBC 2016).
  • IBC 2016: consolidates fragmented laws; creditor-led 330-day CIRP; moratorium; liquidation waterfall (S.33) (IBC 2016).
  • Pillar 1 — Adjudicating Authority: NCLT (companies), DRT (individuals and firms) (IBC 2016).
  • Pillar 2 — IBBI: regulator of the insolvency process and profession.
  • Pillar 3 — Insolvency Professionals: licensed experts running CIRP and liquidation.
  • Pillar 4 — Information Utilities: repositories of financial information (defaults, liabilities).

Takeaway: NCLT + IBBI + IPs + IUs = the four pillars; resolution first, liquidation last (IBC 2016).

Q7. Write notes on the following Intellectual Property: (a) Patent (b) Copyright.

Answer: Patents protect inventions for 20 years (novel, inventive, industrially applicable); copyright protects original expression for the author's life plus 60 years (Unit 9).

Key points:
  • Patent (Patents Act 1970): 20 years from filing; exclusive right to make, use and sell (Unit 9).
  • Patent requirements: novelty, inventive step (non-obvious to a person skilled in the art), industrial application (Unit 9).
  • S.3 exclusions: business methods, computer programmes per se, traditional knowledge, plants/animals (Unit 9).
  • Copyright (Copyright Act 1957): automatic on creation; literary, artistic, musical works (Unit 9).
  • Copyright rights: reproduce, issue copies, perform, translate, adapt; moral rights — attribution and integrity (Unit 9).
  • TRIPS: minimum standards; compulsory licensing for public health (Unit 9).

Takeaway: Patent = invention monopoly (20 yr); copyright = expression (life + 60 yr) (Unit 9).

Q8. What are the objectives of Competition Law? Describe the evolution of Indian Competition Law. Explain the enforcement mechanism of Competition Law in India.

Answer: Competition law aims to prevent anti-competitive conduct, promote competition, protect consumers and ensure free trade; it evolved MRTP (1969) → Competition Act 2002, enforced by the CCI with appeals to NCLAT and the Supreme Court (Unit 13).
