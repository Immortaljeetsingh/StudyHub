IGNOU MMPF-02 Question Paper - TEE Pattern

Time: 3 Hours  |  Maximum Marks: 75  |  Answer any five questions. Each question carries 15 marks (part a: 10 marks, part b: 5 marks).

  1. (a) What is the nature of long-term financial decisions? Distinguish them from short-term (working capital) decisions. (10)
    (b) Explain the three key decision areas of financial management and why they are interlinked. (5)
  2. (a) Define cost of capital. Explain the components of the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) and its importance in investment decisions. (10)
    (b) A firm has equity of Rs. 40,00,000 costing 15% and debt of Rs. 25,00,000 costing 10% before tax. Tax rate is 30%. Calculate the weighted average cost of capital. (5)
  3. (a) Discuss the main theories of capital structure: Net Income approach, Net Operating Income approach, the Traditional view and the Modigliani-Miller approach. (10)
    (b) What is an optimum capital structure? State the factors that influence the capital structure decision. (5)
  4. (a) What is capital budgeting? Explain the Net Present Value (NPV) method and the accept-reject rule. (10)
    (b) A project requires an initial outlay of Rs. 10,00,000 and is expected to generate cash inflows of Rs. 3,00,000 per year for 5 years. Using a discount rate of 12% (PVIFA 12%, 5 years = 3.605), compute the NPV and state whether the project should be accepted. (5)
  5. (a) Explain PERT and CPM. How is the critical path identified in a project network? (10)
    (b) Differentiate between PERT and CPM and give one application of each in Indian industry. (5)
  6. (a) Explain the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) and its use in measuring the required rate of return. (10)
    (b) The risk-free rate is 7%, the expected return on the market is 15% and a stock has a beta of 1.2. Calculate the required rate of return on the stock using CAPM. (5)

Q1. Nature of long-term financial decisions

Answer: Long-term financial decisions lock funds in for more than a year — large, risky and hard to reverse — while short-term working capital decisions are routine and reversible, and all three decision areas interlink.

Long-term financial decisions involve funds locked in for more than a year — buying plant and machinery, entering new projects, raising equity or long-term debt, and deciding dividends. They are large in size, irreversible (hard to resell a factory), risky because cash flows are uncertain over many years, and made at the top level of management. Short-term decisions (working capital) deal with cash, inventory and receivables that convert to cash within one year; they are routine, reversible and repeated many times.

Financial management has three interlinked decision areas:

  • Investment decisions — where to put money (long-term capital budgeting and short-term working capital).
  • Financing decisions — how to raise funds (debt vs equity mix = capital structure).
  • Dividend decisions — how much profit to pay out vs retain for reinvestment.

They are linked because a good investment raises profits, which funds more investment; the financing mix decides the cost of capital that determines which investments are profitable; and dividends decide how much profit stays inside the firm.

Key points: long-term = large, irreversible, risky; short-term = routine and reversible; the three pillars are investment, financing and dividend decisions; all three aim at one goal — maximising shareholder wealth.

Takeaway: Contrast long-term (large, irreversible, risky) with short-term (routine, reversible), then show investment, financing and dividend decisions feeding the single goal of shareholder-wealth maximisation.

Q2. Cost of capital and WACC + numerical

Answer: Cost of capital is the minimum return a firm must earn to keep its value unchanged, and WACC blends the after-tax cost of every source weighted by its share of total funds.

Cost of capital is the minimum rate of return a firm must earn on its investments to keep its value unchanged; it is the opportunity cost of the funds shareholders and lenders have provided. Each source of finance has a cost: equity (expected return of shareholders), preference shares (fixed dividend), and debt (interest, which is tax-deductible, so the effective cost is interest × (1 − tax rate)). The WACC is the weighted average of these costs, weighted by the proportion of each source in total funds. It is used as the discount rate for NPV, the hurdle rate for projects, and the benchmark for performance.

Numerical (part b): Total capital = 40,00,000 + 25,00,000 = Rs. 65,00,000
Cost of debt after tax = 10% × (1 − 0.30) = 7%
WACC = (15% × 40,00,000/65,00,000) + (7% × 25,00,000/65,00,000)
WACC = (15% × 0.6154) + (7% × 0.3846) = 9.23% + 2.69% = 11.92%.

So the firm must earn at least 11.92% on new investments to satisfy both shareholders and lenders.

Key points: cost of debt is after tax = kd(1−t); WACC = sum of (cost × weight) of each source; WACC is the discount rate for project appraisal; lower WACC = more profitable projects become viable.

Takeaway: Compute after-tax debt cost as 10% × 0.7 = 7%, then WACC = 15% × 0.6154 + 7% × 0.3846 = 11.92% — the hurdle every new project must clear.

Q3. Capital structure theories

Answer: Capital structure theory asks how much debt a firm should carry — NI says more debt is always better, NOI says the mix is irrelevant, the Traditional view finds an optimum zone, and MM says debt matters only through taxes.

Net Income (NI) approach: assumes debt is cheaper than equity, so the more debt a firm uses, the lower its overall cost of capital and the higher its value. It assumes no tax complications and constant costs. Net Operating Income (NOI) approach: the opposite — overall cost of capital and firm value stay constant whatever the mix, because cheap debt is exactly offset by the rising cost of equity. Traditional view: middle path — up to a point, adding debt lowers the cost of capital (the 'optimum zone'); beyond that, equity holders demand so much more that cost of capital rises. Modigliani-Miller (MM): in a perfect market with no taxes, capital structure is irrelevant — value depends only on operating income; with corporate taxes, debt becomes valuable because interest saves tax.

Optimum capital structure is the debt-equity mix that minimises the overall cost of capital and maximises firm value.

Factors influencing it: cost of debt and equity, business and financial risk, tax rates, control considerations, market conditions, growth plans, asset structure (fixed assets support debt), and lender/rating-agency expectations.

Key points: NI says more debt = lower cost; NOI says mix is irrelevant; Traditional says optimum zone exists; MM says irrelevant in perfect markets, valuable with taxes; debt is cheaper because interest is tax-deductible.

Takeaway: Present NI, NOI, Traditional and MM in one line each, then define the optimum structure as the mix that minimises the cost of capital and list the influencing factors.

Q4. Capital budgeting and NPV + numerical

Answer: Capital budgeting evaluates long-term projects, and the NPV method discounts future cash flows at the cost of capital and accepts a project whenever NPV is positive.

Capital budgeting is the process of evaluating and choosing long-term investment projects (new plants, machinery, expansions). The NPV method discounts all future cash inflows and outflows at the firm's cost of capital and subtracts the initial outlay. Rule: accept the project if NPV is positive, reject if negative; among competing projects, choose the highest NPV.

Numerical (part b): Present value of inflows = Annual inflow × PVIFA = 3,00,000 × 3.605 = Rs. 10,81,500
NPV = PV of inflows − Initial outlay = 10,81,500 − 10,00,000 = Rs. 81,500
Since NPV is positive, the project should be accepted.

Advantages of NPV: it uses cash flows, considers the time value of money, and adds value directly in rupees. Its limitation: it needs a good estimate of the discount rate (WACC).

NPV vs IRR: IRR is the discount rate at which NPV = 0; both usually give the same accept/reject verdict, but NPV is preferred when they conflict (e.g., mutually exclusive projects of different size) because NPV shows rupee wealth created, while IRR may mislead.

Key points: NPV = PV of inflows − outlay; accept if NPV > 0; NPV = cash inflow × PVIFA − initial investment; NPV is superior to IRR for ranking projects.

Takeaway: Work PV = 3,00,000 × 3.605 = Rs 10,81,500, so NPV = Rs 81,500 — positive, accept — and prefer NPV over IRR when the two conflict on ranking.

Q5. PERT and CPM

Answer: PERT and CPM plan projects as networks of activities and events, and the critical path is the longest path of zero float that fixes the project duration.

CPM (Critical Path Method) is a deterministic technique for projects where activity durations are known with certainty (e.g., construction). PERT (Programme Evaluation and Review Technique) is probabilistic — used when durations are uncertain (e.g., R&D), with three time estimates: optimistic (a), most likely (m) and pessimistic (b); expected time = (a + 4m + b)/6.

A project network has activities (arrows/tasks) and events (nodes/milestones). The critical path is the longest time path through the network — the path of zero float. Steps: list activities and dependencies, draw the network, compute earliest start/finish (forward pass) and latest start/finish (backward pass), find float for each activity; activities with zero float lie on the critical path. The project duration equals the critical path length, and any delay there delays the whole project.

Differences: CPM uses one time estimate, PERT uses three; CPM suits repetitive/known work, PERT suits one-time R&D projects; CPM controls cost-time trade-offs via crashing, PERT focuses on time uncertainty.

Applications in India: CPM in highway or housing construction (e.g., NHAI road projects) and PERT in defence/R&D projects and new product development.

Key points: critical path = longest path = zero float; any delay on it delays the project; PERT expected time = (a + 4m + b)/6; CPM = certain durations, PERT = uncertain.

Takeaway: Define the critical path as the longest path with zero float, quote PERT's (a + 4m + b)/6 formula, and match CPM to construction and PERT to R&D projects.

Q6. CAPM + numerical

Answer: CAPM prices systematic risk: required return = risk-free rate + beta × market risk premium — the security market line on which every fairly priced asset sits.

The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) links the required return on an asset to its systematic (non-diversifiable) risk, measured by beta (β). Formula: Required return = Risk-free rate + Beta × (Market return − Risk-free rate). The term (Market return − Risk-free rate) is the market risk premium. A stock with beta above 1 moves more than the market (aggressive), below 1 moves less (defensive). CAPM helps set discount rates for projects, price securities and evaluate portfolio performance.

Numerical (part b): Required return = 7% + 1.2 × (15% − 7%) = 7% + 1.2 × 8% = 7% + 9.6% = 16.6%.

So investors in this stock expect 16.6% for bearing its level of market risk; if the stock's expected return is above 16.6% it is undervalued, below it overvalued.

Limitations: CAPM rests on assumptions like efficient markets and a single-factor market risk; real-world returns may need extra factors, which is why models such as the Arbitrage Pricing Theory were developed.

Key points: CAPM: R = Rf + β(Rm − Rf); beta measures systematic risk only; higher beta = higher required return; CAPM is used to estimate the cost of equity for WACC.

Takeaway: Apply R = 7% + 1.2 × 8% = 16.6%, read beta above 1 as aggressive, and say CAPM is how the cost of equity feeds WACC.
