Solved Assignment Questions & Answers - MMPB-06

Q1. Explain the three ethical theories of business ethics. How are they applied in banking decisions? (10 Marks)

Answer: Business ethics refers to the moral principles that guide behaviour in business, including banking. The three major ethical theories are Utilitarianism, Deontology and Virtue Ethics. Utilitarianism, associated with Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, judges an action by the greatest good for the greatest number; a banker applies it through cost-benefit analysis and stakeholder impact assessment while sanctioning credit. Deontology, associated with Immanuel Kant, holds that rules and duties must be followed regardless of outcome; in banking this means honest advertising, fair treatment of borrowers and strict compliance even when violation would be profitable. Virtue Ethics, rooted in Aristotle, focuses on the character of the decision-maker: a virtuous banker asks what a good person would do and builds an ethical culture by example. Banks operationalise these theories through a code of ethics, the ethical decision-making process (identify the issue, gather facts, evaluate alternatives, choose, implement and review) and whistleblowing mechanisms for reporting unethical or illegal activity. The governance principles of Transparency, Accountability, Fairness and Responsibility (T-A-F-R) translate these theories into practice. In India, the Satyam scam (2009) showed the cost of abandoning ethical standards and triggered stricter audit and disclosure reforms. Remember the mnemonic U-D-V and always quote an Indian example.

Key points:

  • Business ethics refers to the moral principles that guide behaviour in business, including banking.
  • Virtue Ethics, rooted in Aristotle, focuses on the character of the decision-maker: a virtuous banker asks what a good person would do and builds an ethical culture by example.
  • The three major ethical theories are Utilitarianism, Deontology and Virtue Ethics.

Takeaway: Remember the mnemonic U-D-V and always quote an Indian example.

Q2. What is the Basel framework? Explain the three pillars of Basel II and the key enhancements made by Basel III. (20 Marks)

Answer: The Basel framework is a set of international banking regulations issued by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) to promote global financial stability. Basel I (1988) focused only on credit risk and fixed a minimum Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) of 8%. Basel II (2004) made capital requirements more risk-sensitive and rested on three pillars. Pillar 1 - Minimum Capital: banks must maintain CAR = (Tier 1 + Tier 2) / Risk-Weighted Assets at or above 8%. Pillar 2 - Supervisory Review: banks assess their own capital needs and regulators review and intervene where risks are inadequately covered. Pillar 3 - Market Discipline: banks must publicly disclose their risk profile, capital adequacy and risk management practices so markets can discipline them. Following the 2008 financial crisis, Basel III (2010) strengthened the framework: Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) of 4.5%, Tier 1 of 6%, a capital conservation buffer of 2.5%, a countercyclical buffer of 0-2.5%, a leverage ratio of 3% and a Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) of 100%. In India, the RBI implements Basel norms, and banks such as SBI maintain a CAR of about 13-14%, comfortably above the 10.5% minimum (8% + 2.5% buffer). For the exam, memorise the three pillars with the mnemonic M-S-M (Minimum capital, Supervisory review, Market discipline) and all Basel III ratios.

Key points:

  • The Basel framework is a set of international banking regulations issued by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) to promote global financial stability.
  • In India, the RBI implements Basel norms, and banks such as SBI maintain a CAR of about 13-14%, comfortably above the 10.5% minimum (8% + 2.5% buffer).
  • Basel I (1988) focused only on credit risk and fixed a minimum Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) of 8%.

Takeaway: For the exam, memorise the three pillars with the mnemonic M-S-M (Minimum capital, Supervisory review, Market discipline) and all Basel III ratios.

Q3. What are the four types of banking risks? Discuss RBI's role in the corporate governance of Indian banks. (10 Marks)

Answer: Banks face four principal types of risk - Credit, Market, Operational and Liquidity (C-M-O-L). Credit risk is the possibility of borrower default, visible in non-performing assets and loan defaults. Market risk arises from changes in interest rates, forex rates and security prices. Operational risk stems from inadequate processes, systems or human errors, including fraud and system failures. Liquidity risk is the inability to meet short-term obligations, which can lead to a bank run. The Reserve Bank of India is the regulator and supervisor of Indian banking. Its governance role includes licensing banks, on-site inspections and off-site monitoring, prescribing capital adequacy and exposure norms, resolution through the Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework, and consumer protection through the Banking Ombudsman. RBI's regulatory architecture was shaped by the Narasimham Committee (1991 and 1998), which introduced prudential norms and capital adequacy, and the P.J. Nayak Committee (2014), which recommended board composition and governance reforms for public sector banks. Within banks, governance is enforced through the audit committee, the risk management committee, the compliance function and internal audit. Governance failures, such as those exposed by the Satyam scandal, led to stronger independent director requirements, enhanced audit committee powers and whistleblower protection.

Key points:

  • Banks face four principal types of risk - Credit, Market, Operational and Liquidity (C-M-O-L).
  • The Reserve Bank of India is the regulator and supervisor of Indian banking.
  • Credit risk is the possibility of borrower default, visible in non-performing assets and loan defaults.

Takeaway: Governance failures, such as those exposed by the Satyam scandal, led to stronger independent director requirements, enhanced audit committee powers and whistleblower protection.

Q4. What is a Non-Performing Asset (NPA)? Explain asset classification norms and the recovery mechanisms available to Indian banks. (10 Marks)

Answer: A Non-Performing Asset (NPA) is a loan or advance whose interest or principal remains overdue for 90 days or more. RBI's asset classification system is S-S-D-L. Standard assets are regular accounts requiring only 0-10% provisioning; Sub-standard assets are overdue between 90 days and 12 months, requiring 15-25% provisioning; Doubtful assets remain sub-standard for 12 months or more and require 25-100% provisioning across the D1, D2 and D3 stages; Loss assets are identified as uncollectible and written off with 100% provisioning. RBI maintains CRILC, a central database of borrowers with exposure of Rs 5 crore and above, to detect stress early. NPA management has three stages: prevention through better credit appraisal, monitoring and early warning systems; resolution through Corporate Debt Restructuring (CDR), Strategic Debt Restructuring (SDR) and the Scheme for Sustainable Structuring (S4A); and recovery through the SARFAESI Act 2002, which lets banks seize and sell collateral without court intervention, Debt Recovery Tribunals (DRTs), and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016, which provides time-bound resolution of 180+90 days. The Prompt Corrective Action framework disciplines weak banks. Indian NPAs peaked around 11.5% in 2018 after RBI's Asset Quality Review, which is why strict classification and Pillar 3 disclosure matter for banking health.

Key points:

  • A Non-Performing Asset (NPA) is a loan or advance whose interest or principal remains overdue for 90 days or more.
  • Indian NPAs peaked around 11.5% in 2018 after RBI's Asset Quality Review, which is why strict classification and Pillar 3 disclosure matter for banking health.
  • RBI's asset classification system is S-S-D-L.

Takeaway: Indian NPAs peaked around 11.5% in 2018 after RBI's Asset Quality Review, which is why strict classification and Pillar 3 disclosure matter for banking health.
